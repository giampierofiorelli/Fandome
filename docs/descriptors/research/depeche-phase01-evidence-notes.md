# Depeche Mode — Phase 01 evidence notes

Research cut-off 27 October 2026. **Four new studio albums only**. All eight scales are comparative Fandome editorial ratings, supported by the album-specific accounts indicated here, **not objective measurements**. Sources full names and working URLs occur in `depechemode_phase01_sources.csv`; genre-by-genre support in `depechemode_phase01_genre_evidence.csv`. Album ID/artist addition prerequisite is documented in CLAUDE_IMPORT.md.

## 1. Speak & Spell (1981) — depechemode-speakspell

**Editions/credits.** Original Mute UK LP (STUMM5), 5 October 1981, has 11 tracks; subsequent international/expanded versions alter the sequence. Produced by Depeche Mode and Daniel Miller, with recordings at Blackwing. Vince Clarke wrote the large majority; the Martin Gore-written 'Tora! Tora! Tora!' and instrumental 'Big Muff' already show a different temperament. The producer's retrospective confirms Clarke was the driving force and arranger. **Landmark = 1** because it is the band's debut, Clarke's sole studio album with them, and an early synth-pop breakthrough. (Official Archives; original credits; Electronic Beats.)

**Genres.** Main synthpop and an album-level hi-NRG tag (Album of the Year), with newwave and alt-dance / dance-rock and post-punk tags from AllMusic. The latter two are broad/weak database taxonomies: rhythmic synthesized pop is the core, not a post-punk guitar group. New Romantic is only a low-strength scene association from Record Club; pop and some disco rhythm derive from Apple Music. Detailed sources and scope for all nine tags appear in genre-evidence CSV.

**Instruments / production.** Original sleeve says 'synthetics and voices'; personnel credits keyboards and programming. Reverb's equipment survey credits Daniel Miller's **ARP 2600** for synthesised drum sounds rather than relying on the earlier demo's drum machine presets. `synth`, `sequencer`, `drummachine` capture functional parts; `arp2600`, `drumprogramming`, `synthprogramming` are album/session-supported production. Specific Kawai 100F / Yamaha CS-5 / Roland Jupiter-4 combinations vary in recollections, so no model beyond the ARP 2600 is entered. Vince Clarke and Miller's later discussion is consistent with simple interlocking analogue lines.

**Scales (0–5).** `energy=3`: energetic, buoyant singles tempered by lighter/brooding material. `electronic=5`: album's intentionally synth-only backing. `mood=4`: predominantly optimistic singles, counterweighted by 'Photographic' and Gore's tracks. `theatrical=2`: straightforward youthful delivery (Raggett). `experimental=2`: early synth advances within compact hook-led pop, not avant-garde. `polish=3`: efficient studio production with some youthful roughness. `tempo=4`: brisk percolating songs, not sustained extreme speed. `groove=4`: notable disco energy and dance impetus without continuous DJ-style mixing. Supported by AllMusic, Apple Music, Reverb, and Miller interview; numbers are judgements.

**Voice/lyrics.** Gahan's early relatively restrained, tuneful leads justify `clear|soft`. AllMusic identifies love, life and clubs; album lyrics offer romantic hooks and humour, hence `love|party|irony`; these are not claims that every track concerns the listed topics. `en` for English. The `sounds_like` artists are editorial timbral neighbours; `influenced_by` includes Kraftwerk, Human League, OMD and Gary Numan because the original producer/former member attest to the early trio while Ned Raggett directly names Numan among the inspirations for this *particular debut*. Gary Numan is a critic-supported **album-level** influence rather than a direct first-person career testimony.

**Disagreement.** Rolling Stone's contemporary scepticism about monotonous grooves contrasts with Raggett's description of catchy freshness; the electronic nature is uncontested, not universal critical acclaim. Some secondary release dates reflect territory/edition rather than Mute UK date.

## 2. A Broken Frame (1982) — depechemode-abrokenframe

**Editions/credits.** Original UK 27 September 1982, Mute STUMM9; produced by Depeche Mode and Daniel Miller. The band recorded with only Gahan, Gore and Fletcher. Wilder toured in 1982 but did **not** play on the album; Side-Line documents the contemporaneous issue and Gore's decision to demonstrate independence after Clarke left. (Official Archives; original sleeve; Side-Line.) **Landmark = 0** as an exploratory bridge rather than a most-defining album, though historically necessary.

**Genres.** Synthpop and newwave are explicit. Album of the Year lists `minimalwave` as secondary (new token). AllMusic uses broader alternative-dance/post-punk/dance-rock/alternative-pop-rock/alternative-indie-rock/college-rock taxonomies. Apple Music calls the *track* 'Satellite' electro-dub, encoded as the new token `electrodub` **last**, not a dominant album genre. Retrospective use of minimal wave is weaker than synthpop; the style evidence file preserves provenance.

**Instruments / production.** Martin Gore describes his **PPG Wave 2** in an early-1982 interview; Daniel Miller specifically says they used the PPG heavily on *A Broken Frame*. Side-Line traces ARP 2600 synthesized kicks and Roland TR-808/Korg KR-55 drum sound sources to the sessions; `tr808` and `arp2600` therefore have medium confidence, while `ppgwave` has high. Synth, keyboard parts, drum machines and sequenced basslines are independently attested. Miller says individual monophonic parts were tracked sequentially; `overdubbing` represents that multitrack method. There is an in-studio marching recording from guests Blancmange in 'Shouldn't Have Done That', **but no separate 'sampling' token**, because Miller dates widespread sample-based production to the next album and a marching recording need not be digital sampling.

**Scales.** `energy=2`: quieter and less propulsive on balance than the first album. `electronic=5`: still predominantly electronic keyboard and programmed drum material. `mood=2`: more wistful, darker heartbreak alongside cheerful 'The Meaning of Love'. `theatrical=2`: intimate and relatively tentative Gahan/Gore vocal manner. `experimental=3`: greater sound experimentation ('Monument', 'Shouldn't Have Done That', 'Satellite') but pop structure. `polish=2`: reviewers acknowledge thinner, metallic arrangement and uneven production. `tempo=2`: slower/varied overall than the Clarke debut. `groove=3`: catchy singles amid reflective material. Criticism and contrast supported by Miller, AllMusic, Side-Line and Apple Music; numbers are editorial.

**Voice/lyrics.** `clear|soft`: Gahan primarily with Gore on the duet 'Shouldn't Have Done That'; instrumental 'Nothing to Fear' does not justify categorising the whole record `instrumental`. Themes love, failed relationships, personal introspection and the dictator-story marching song (political/storytelling), with song-level caveat. `influenced_by` deliberately blank: no album-specific named artist influence independently verified; generic 1980s synth lineage must not be invented for this album.

**Disagreement.** Gore has dismissed the album as uneven, while Miller finds its uncertainty fertile. The 1982 group was using PPG instruments, but exact drum machines and sample construction are differently described across later reconstructions. Do not include Wilder or elevate engineer John Fryer to producer.

## 3. Construction Time Again (1983) — depechemode-constructiontimeagain

**Edition/credits.** Original Mute STUMM13, 22 August 1983. Depeche Mode and Daniel Miller produced; Gareth Jones was officially credited **tonmeister / engineer** and became co-producer only on the following album. Recorded at John Foxx's Garden (London), mixed Hansa Mischraum (Berlin). First studio recording with Alan Wilder (keyboard, piano, sampling/programming). The liner-based discography credits Gore guitar and melodica on 'Everything Counts', Wilder piano ('Love, In Itself'), and sampler/drum-machine parts. **Landmark = 1** for the new technology and political songwriting despite mixed album reviews. (Official Archives, STUMM13 credits, Miller Electronic Beats.)

**Genres.** AOTY explicitly lists synthpop and industrial. Apple Music, writing about the subsequent record, identifies the 1983 music as **industrial pop**, warranting the existing `industrialpop` token. AllMusic additionally gives newwave, alternativedance and broad dance-rock/alternative-rock categories. Metal-on-metal textures are musical production evidence but not by themselves enough to invent new genre names. Genre CSV preserves each source and scope.

**Instruments / production.** Alan Wilder told Sound on Sound that the group used a **BBC Micro sequencer** in 1983, still used **PPG Wave**, a **Synclavier** synthesiser/sampler and an original **E-mu Emulator**. In the 1990 interview republished by Australia's ABC he and/or Martin Gore described recording and extensively sampling anything around them. Miller confirms the process was recording **found sounds and toy instruments**, not sampling pre-existing records. Therefore `sampling`, `fieldrecordings`, `sonicmanipulation`, `synclavier`, `emulator` (**not emulatorii**), `ppgwave`, `drumprogramming`, `programming` and `synthprogramming` are supported. Instruments `synth|sampler|sequencer|drummachine|percussion|piano|guitar|melodica` rely on member credits and the producing witnesses. Other off-album B-side accounts, such as 'Work Hard', only corroborate the production approach and are not treated as direct track credits.

**Scales.** `energy=4`: heavier percussive attacks and dance-pop momentum than before. `electronic=5`: sequenced electronics, samplers and synth timbres dominate despite some conventional keyboard/guitar/melodica contributions. `mood=2`: pointed/cold sound, although the 'Everything Counts' refrain is bright. `theatrical=3`: more dramatic song presentation and contrast. `experimental=4`: found-object sounds turned into rhythm and new industrial textures. `polish=3`: inventive, increasingly complex Hansa mix with occasional rough edges. `tempo=4`: largely driving electronic pulse. `groove=4`: pronounced 'Everything Counts' dance dynamics and many propulsive tracks. Based on Miller, ABC, Apple and critical reviews; album-level assessments.

**Voice/lyrics.** `clear|powerful` reflects Gahan's firmer delivery with group backing and occasional Gore lead ('Pipeline'). Main lyrical shift to greed, corporate conduct, the environment, nuclear threat, and conscience: `social|political|introspection|love`. The exact Song credit for 'Two Minute Warning' and 'The Landscape Is Changing' belongs to Wilder; **writing credit is not production credit**. `influenced_by` left blank: Gore explicitly **rejects** the claim that they were copying Einstürzende Neubauten, despite sampled industrial materials and Berlin mixing.

**Disagreement.** Sound-alike Neubauten and DAF are editorial descriptions of sonic proximity, never documented artistic influence. Some reviewers regard the record as transitional and inconsistent; technical innovation is well attested independently of quality judgement.

## 4. Some Great Reward (1984) — depechemode-somegreatreward

**Edition/credits.** Original Mute STUMM19, 24 September 1984. Recorded London and Hansa Berlin, with original release listing **Depeche Mode, Daniel Miller, and Gareth Jones as producers**. Gareth is now a genuine co-producer. Alan Wilder contributed musical arrangements and programmed instruments; he is not an additional separately credited producer. **Landmark = 1**: 'People Are People' as international breakthrough, 'Master and Servant', 'Blasphemous Rumours', and the distinctive industrial dance plus ballad juxtaposition. (Original sleeve in depmode.com; electronicbeats.net; AllMusic.)

**Genres.** Main `synthpop|industrial` appears in reviews and album classifications; album index further identifies `ebm|darkwave|newwave`. AllMusic adds alternative dance and various umbrella genres; its review explicitly calls it an *electronic music* album (`electronic`). Genre evidence preserves these sources, with the weaker genre metadata towards the end. Genre labels do not imply membership in the industrial or EBM subcultural scenes.

**Instruments / production.** On 'People Are People', Wilder described most sounds as sampled in the **Synclavier**, including sampled guitar plucks, with choir-like layers involving PPG and Emulator voices. Gareth Jones's detailed Sound on Sound account names an **Emulator II** for the 1984 work and the Synclavier's audio/sonic design. Wilder's quoted 1984 account of 'Blasphemous Rumours' identifies a **concrete slab hit with a hammer** as the sampled snare, captured with Gareth's **Stellavox** portable recorder. This supports `sampling|synclavier|emulatorii|ppgwave|fieldrecordings|programming|drumprogramming|sonicmanipulation`. Main instruments are synthesiser/sampler/sequenced rhythm, acoustic **piano** on Gore's 'Somebody', some credited **guitar** (sometimes resampled), and physical percussion made musical. Do not label string synthesizer pads as actual strings solely on musical resemblance. Session sources distinguish **Emulator II (1984)** from the original **Emulator (1983)**.

**Scales.** `energy=4`: 'People Are People', 'Something to Do' and 'Master and Servant' are heavy and propulsive, but Gore's ballads bring dynamic contrast. `electronic=5`: electronic/sampled arrangement dominates; piano is a salient minority. `mood=2`: sombre religion/alienation/intimacy contrasts with buoyant dance singles. `theatrical=4`: commanding performances, prominent dramatic juxtaposition, charged song concepts. `experimental=4`: dense studio-constructed found-sound rhythms. `polish=4`: sophisticated Hansa production with deeper layers than earlier records. `tempo=3`: alternation of fast machine songs and slow ballads. `groove=3`: several genuine dancefloor tracks but ballads interrupt the flow, making 5 inappropriate. Supported by AllMusic, Apple Music, Miller, Gareth Jones and Wilder; numbers remain judgements.

**Voice/lyrics.** Gahan's mature and intense lead (`clear|powerful`) contrasts with Gore's intimate `soft` lead on 'Somebody'. 'People Are People' handles prejudice, 'Master and Servant' combines sexual dominance and social allegory, 'Blasphemous Rumours' questions providence, 'Somebody' addresses vulnerability in love. Thus `social|sensuality|spiritual|love|heartbreak|introspection|irony`. `influenced_by` blank without a named contemporary influence verified for this **specific** record. Sonic parallels to DAF, Front 242, Neubauten and Soft Cell are merely editorial comparisons.

**Disagreement / false attributions.** Avoid using 12-inch single remix practices as if every effect is on the nine-track original LP. The 'Master and Servant' alleged borrowed Frankie Goes to Hollywood sample is disputed/refuted in specialist sample research and is not entered. There is a documented concrete snare sample on the album, but no warrant to claim every drum was acoustically recorded. AllMusic depicts the record as a creative peak; Miller recalled the same 1984 sessions as exhausting, a disagreement about **process**, not credits.
