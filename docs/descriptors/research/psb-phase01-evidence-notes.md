# Pet Shop Boys — Phase 01: evidence and editorial decisions

Research cut-off: **27 October 2026**. Artist ID **`psb`** verified in `seed.ts`. Only `Actually` is already described (`p-actually` in `docs/descriptors/psb.csv`). Existing `p-behaviour`, `p-very` and `p-nonetheless` are seed-album IDs, not descriptor rows.

Every score (eight 0–5 fields) is Fandome editorial judgment against the repository anchors, not a measured feature or claim from a critic. Album-level confidence expresses evidential strength across the row; it does not imply every numerical value is objective. Source IDs refer to `psb_phase01_sources.csv` and `psb_phase01_genre_evidence.csv`.

## `psb-please` — Please (1986)

* **Genre evidence.** AllMusic assigns Alternative Dance, Dance-Pop, Dance-Rock and Club/Dance. Classic Pop describes the UK electropop/synthpop sound, synthesizer arpeggios and hip-hop drum influence. The genre `dancerock` is AllMusic's stylistic label, not a claim that guitars dominate the record. Do not assign `hiphop` album-wide just because *West End Girls* uses rap phrasing and hip-hop beats; its underlying music is synth-pop and electronic dance-pop.
* **Scales.** Energy **3**, electronic **5**, mood **2**, theatrical **2**, experimental **2**, polish **4**, tempo **3**, groove **4**. Dance programming and catchy mid/uptempo tracks coexist with slower wistfulness; ironic, detached singing offsets the buoyant beats. Modern-sounding but not a purely club-maximalist LP.
* **Voice and lyrics.** Tennant's clear, often semi-spoken phrasing (*West End Girls*), softer melodic leads and the supporting female vocal do not make `rap` the album's dominant voice classification. Themes: city, aspirational wealth and irony (*Opportunities*), urban disorder (*Suburbia*), courtship and desire (*Love Comes Quickly*, *I Want a Lover*) and the sequence's loose storyline. `spoken` applies only as a secondary delivery.
* **Instruments.** Production engineer David Jacob's first-hand March 1986 interview for *West End Girls* identifies the Oberheim DMX rhythm machine, Emulator I/II samples, DX7/Jupiter 6 composite synth bass, Roland MSQ-700 sequencing, cowbell, cymbal and processed samples; PSB's 2002 recollection confirms guitars and pianos on the debut album. Andy Mackay saxophone on *Love Comes Quickly* and original personnel list provide `saxophone`, not a synthesized sax inferred from timbre. Chris Lowe's synthesizers and keyboards are central.
* **Production.** `fieldrecordings` is secure for the street/walking traffic captured outside Advision for *West End Girls*; primary engineer describes a **Professional Walkman**, while later liner recollection says **DAT**. Consequently **do not** assign `datrecording`. The `rolandmsq700` token is exact and absent from the current production-token list. `delay` is documented in the engineer account. `sampling` and `synthprogramming` reflect explicitly described recordings rather than genre deduction. These machine credits are **track-level**, not claims each device appears on all 11 cuts.
* **Producers.** Stephen Hague led most final recording. J. J. Jeczalik and Nicholas Froome produced the earlier *Opportunities* sessions that the album incorporates; Ron Dean Miller, Pet Shop Boys and Blue Weaver have explicitly credited original-track production roles. Original production credits are retained where the recording contributes to the issued album. **Bobby Orlando is not credited as a producer of the released Parlophone Please masters**; his 1984 *West End Girls* recording was superseded. David Jacob was the engineer, not producer.
* **Comparisons and influences.** Classic Pop makes *I Want a Lover* → Soft Cell (*Non-Stop Erotic Cabaret*) an explicit sonic comparison. Tennant himself names Grandmaster Flash's *The Message* as the lyrical/rap model for *West End Girls* (Rhino); both members explicitly cite The Flirts' *Passion* and Bobby Orlando as early compositional models (MAGNET/Independent). Influence tokens are based on those firsthand recollections, not a guest appearance.
* **Landmark** 1. Debut establishes the pairing of electronically programmed arrangements, social observation and Tennant's understated voice.

## `psb-disco` — Disco (1986)

* **Format decision.** The band's website categorizes this record under **Compilation**, but its own official description calls it an LP of *disco remixes*; album and specialist discographies classify it as the **first remix album**. This project excludes routine compilations, while explicitly admitting landmark remix albums, so the chosen row type is `remix`. This is an **exception requiring owner review**: if the owner interprets the compilation exclusion as applying to *every* assembled remix collection, omit this row rather than silently changing it to `compilation`.
* **Genre evidence.** AllMusic assigns Alternative Dance, Dance-Pop, House and Dance-Rock; its original discographic genre lists also include synth-pop and disco. A specialist PSB history expressly notes the music is **not strongly disco in the historic stylistic sense**. `disco` consequently comes last; `house` is an editorially debatable retrospective AllMusic tag for 1986 and should remain lower priority than synthpop and dance-pop. No invented `hinrg` label has been added merely from Bobby Orlando's earlier sound.
* **Scales.** Energy **4**, electronic **5**, mood **3**, theatrical **2**, experimental **2**, polish **4**, tempo **4**, groove **5**. Lengthened rhythm-first club mixes, sometimes dub-like spacing; material reworks both energetic and melancholic songs.
* **Voice and lyrics.** Extended tracks leave Tennant's original English lyric topics intact: ambitions, social satire, romantic loss, suburb and fashion identities. `spoken` reflects *Paninaro* and Tennant's delivery rather than an overall spoken-word work. No Italian-language main-song row solely from 'Italian Remix'.
* **Instruments and production.** Original liner personnel names Fairlight input from Andy Richards and Adrien Cook, Blue Weaver/Khris Kallis extra keyboards, and saxophone from Gary Barnacle and Andy Mackay. Producers and mix technicians reused synth, programmed drum and sampler masters from the original recordings (e.g., the documented 1985 *West End Girls* master), but exact per-remix machine presence is less secure than for Please. Avoid assigning particular DX7 or Oberheim models to the entire remix LP. `remixing` is essential and documented.
* **Producers versus remixers.** Credit Pet Shop Boys, Phil Harding, Julian Mendelsohn, J.J. Jeczalik, Nicholas Froome and Stephen Hague, as listed in original-album production credits. **Arthur Baker, Shep Pettibone, Ron Dean Miller, Latin Rascals and David Jacob are documented as remixers/mixers on this LP, not new overall credited producers**; do not automatically put them in `producers`. This is why the record does not claim those remixers as musical `influenced_by`.
* **Influences and comparisons.** New album-specific influences are unknown: deliberately empty, not extrapolated from the band's broader likes. Dance remix sounds compared editorially with New Order, The Flirts and Divine's robotic 1980s underground club productions. Stuart Price calls the album a pivotal influence *on him*, which supports `landmark=1` rather than a backward PSB influence.

## `psb-introspective` — Introspective (1988)

* **Genre evidence.** MusicBrainz lists synth-pop, house, dance-pop and electro-disco; AllMusic assigns Alternative Dance, Club/Dance, Dance-Pop and Dance-Rock and describes Latin-derived rhythms. `orchestralpop` is **track-specific**, from the original *Left to My Own Devices* arrangements, confirmed by the official PSB/Rhino documentation. `electrodisco` is a texture-style metadata tag, hence placed last. Generic `latin` or `latinpop` were **not** added as album genres just because the Miami *Domino Dancing* track has Latin rhythm and a brass section.
* **Scales.** Energy **4**, electronic **4**, mood **2**, theatrical **4**, experimental **3**, polish **5**, tempo **4**, groove **5**. 12-inch-length arrangements with high club drive, but occasionally introspective/ambivalent lyrics and extravagantly staged orchestration.
* **Voice and lyrics.** Tennant clear/near-spoken leads, plus the extensive credited guest backing ensemble on *It's Alright* making secondary `choral` appropriate. Political anxiety in *It's Alright*, isolation in *Left to My Own Devices* and longing in *I'm Not Scared* justify thematic tags; some lyrics, notably *Always on My Mind*, are covers.
* **Instruments.** The original 1988 LP credits Richard Niles's orchestra, Josh Milan piano (*I Want a Dog*), Fro Sossa/Mike Bakst keyboards, Nestor Gomez guitar, Tony Concepción and other brass/players (*Domino Dancing*), Fairlight programming by Blue Weaver, Andy Richards and Gary Maughan. `sampler` reflects explicitly named Fairlight work. The user is not asked to infer drum-machine models from the era: none listed.
* **Production.** `fairlight` explicitly documented on multiple tracks; string/orchestra and brass arrangements have clear credits; `remixing` captures intended extended forms/Frankie Knuckles mixing. Do not equate his `additional production` / mix for *I Want a Dog* with top-line credited producer status. **Stephen Lipson is explicitly a credited co-producer on Horn tracks** and included. Pet Shop Boys are associate-only for *Domino Dancing* but producer on three other tracks, so are correctly included globally. 'Produced with thanks to' names on *Always on My Mind* are not producer credits.
* **Influences.** In contemporary recollections, the duo cite admiration for Trevor Horn's work with Grace Jones's *Slave to the Rhythm*, ABC's *The Look of Love*, Art of Noise and lavish Phil Spector/Beatles productions. These are actual producer/artist references made by Lowe/Tennant, not a critic's extrapolation. Trevor Horn **himself** is already a credited producer on this album and is intentionally omitted from `influenced_by`.
* **Landmark** 1. The six-extended-track format and early house experiments establish a distinct dance-album approach.

## `p-behaviour` — Behaviour (1990)

* **Genre evidence.** The SHFL critic Ned Raggett explicitly tags synthpop, dance-pop and sophisti-pop; AllMusic also lists Alternative Dance and Dance-Rock. Pitchfork describes a Larry Heard-like deep-house line on *Being Boring*, and house-piano stylings around *My October Symphony*: `house` is **some tracks**, not proof the LP is mainly house. `orchestralpop` is **some tracks**, confirmed by arranger and orchestra credits. Do not blanket-tag the whole album as industrial, trance or hi-NRG on the basis of its predecessors.
* **Scales.** Energy **2**, electronic **4**, mood **1**, theatrical **3**, experimental **2**, polish **5**, tempo **2**, groove **3**. Analog textures preserve a dance-pop core yet the slower ballads, losses and carefully controlled arrangements predominate. Huge melodic passages still create occasional dramatic peaks.
* **Voice and lyrics.** AllMusic notes a move from spoken to sung lines; `soft|clear` fits the reserved delivery. *Being Boring* explores friendship, memory and AIDS-era loss, *So Hard* infidelity and mistrust, *My October Symphony* the political personal, while other tracks cover love and self-doubt. `nostalgia` and `heartbreak` dominate rather than `party`.
* **Instruments.** Guitar on *Being Boring* by J.J. Belle, Johnny Marr guitar and feedback on *This Must Be the Place ...*, and Marr guitar on *My October Symphony*. Orchestra and Balanescu strings on specific tracks; **the album's Jealousy uses synth strings, unlike the subsequently re-recorded single** (Classic Pop). Synths, keyboards and Roland drum machines are substantiated by Faltermeyer's first-hand gear account. `sampler` is documented by his Synclavier recollection, but is not presumed widespread.
* **Production caveat.** Lowe and Tennant *intended* to eschew the era's pervasive sample-heavy production and favor analog synthesis, but producer Faltermeyer reports using the Synclavier's sampling facilities for percussion. For that reason a blanket **'sample-free'** claim is false; `sampling` stays in, with this disagreement surfaced. His gear recollection explicitly includes Moog and Oberheim families plus Roland TR-808, TR-909, TB-303 and Synclavier. No uncorroborated specific Moog/Oberheim model added. Balanescu quartet/orchestra arrangement justify `stringarrangements`; Marr's credited feedback and wah-wah in *My October Symphony* support corresponding processing terms.
* **Producers.** Only Pet Shop Boys and Harold Faltermeyer. Julian Mendelsohn was the mixer, Badalamenti/ Bălănescu arrangers and Marr guest guitar; none is an album producer. The selection of Faltermeyer followed an explicitly stated wish to recall 1970s Giorgio Moroder dance records, justifying `influenced_by=Giorgio Moroder`, not `Harold Faltermeyer`. Comparisons to Larry Heard and Marvin Gaye derive from Pitchfork hearing similarities; these are **sounds_like only**, never evidence of direct influence. Depeche Mode's *Violator* comparison is broader sonic/era adjacency and should be treated as relatively weak.
* **Landmark** 1. A reflective and critically acclaimed tonal departure rather than an abandonment of the electronic idiom.

## Research and import cautions

* The existing 1987 *Actually* entry was read before planning. Existing `p-behaviour` has **no** row in `docs/descriptors/psb.csv`. Import **only the four new rows** with header. No existing rows should be overwritten.
* `psb-disco` is a **format-policy edge case**. Seek owner resolution before inserting if a strict ban on anything the official website labels 'Compilation' takes precedence over the explicit permission for landmark remix albums.
* `rolandmsq700` is the one proposed new production token, from the 1986 first-hand studio report. New album IDs must be inserted in `seed.ts`/`catalog.ts` before descriptor validator runs; `psb-please`, `psb-disco`, `psb-introspective` are not currently catalogued.
* Descriptors use album-original editions, never reissue bonus discs, modern remasters, original 1984 Bobby Orlando single recordings, the alternate 1991 single recording of *Jealousy*, or 1986 12-inch bonus mixes beyond the 1986 Disco track list.
* Historical sources vary on whether the 1988 Introspective release day was 10 or 11 October; use the artist's official **10 October 1988**. No release date disagreement affects the required year.
* No producer is inferred from being a mixer, engineer, featured instrumentalist, guest singer, or from reissued liner thank-yous. Where specific instruments occur in only one or two tracks, they are still sourced at album level but placed after dominant synthesizer textures.
* The `sounds_like` column is editorial comparison. It is not a documented influence database and can be tuned independently by the editor.
