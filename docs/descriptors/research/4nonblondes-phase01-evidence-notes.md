# 4 Non Blondes — Phase 01 evidence notes

Research checked: 28 October 2026. One incremental released-album row only; status `draft`.

## Bigger, Better, Faster, More! (1992)
**Fandome id:** `4nonblondes-biggerbetterfastermore` · **Original type:** studio · **Album producer credit:** David Tickle.

### Release and eligibility
*The only released 4 Non Blondes studio album as of the research date.* A second project titled *1994* was announced by Linda Perry for **2027** (NME, 22 May 2026); it is not yet a released record and must not enter the Fandome catalogue. Later live material and compilations are excluded.

- AllMusic original album review: https://www.allmusic.com/album/r53797/review
- Pitchfork 2025 reunion announcement: https://pitchfork.com/news/4-non-blondes-prepping-first-new-album-in-34-years/
- NME 2026 updated projected release: https://www.nme.com/news/music/linda-perry-4-non-blondes-reunion-confirms-first-new-album-in-33-years-1994-3946949

### Genres, specific first
`alternativerock|bluesrock|funkrock|hardrock|poprock|folk|rnb|psychedelia|blues`

- Alternative rock, blues rock, funk rock and pop rock are identified in album-specific metadata (Album of the Year). AllMusic calls the music an alternative rock/blues/quasi-funk combination.
- Mark Jenkins's **contemporaneous album review** in The Washington Post calls most of the record funk-conscious hard rock; it identifies folk-leaning passages in “What's Up” and “Drifting”, and specifically cello on the latter.
- Folk, R&B and psychedelia derive from a November 1992 Los Angeles Times concert review comparing the band's set with the album, so they appear **after** the main album genres and psychedelia is a texture-level judgement, not an asserted central style.
- The parent `funk` appears in sources but is not duplicated in the ranked row because the more specific `funkrock` already captures it; all supporting evidence retained in genre_evidence.

### Voices
`powerful|raspy` — both supported directly by Tom Demalon's AllMusic review and Mark Jenkins's Washington Post account of Perry's alto and belting. Vocal resemblance to Concrete Blonde's Johnette Napolitano comes from Demalon's review; comparison with Toni Childs comes from Jenkins, while a 1993 Los Angeles Times profile compares her vocal delivery to Janis Joplin. The 1992 Los Angeles Times concert review compares the voice to Ann Wilson of Heart. These are *comparisons*, **not proven influences**.

### Instruments — instrument credits rather than genre deductions
`electricguitar|acousticguitar|bass|drums|mellotron|keyboards|cello|accordion|piano|harmonica`

- Original 1992 MusicBrainz track credits identify Linda Perry's electric/acoustic guitars; Christa Hillhouse's bass; Dawn Richardson's drums; guitarists Louis Metoyer, Roger Rocha and Shaunna Hall; Mellotron by Rory Kaplan; and accordion/cello by Suzie Katayama. AllMusic lists keyboards by Dave Rickets. Piano and harmonica are present among release personnel or track credits, but are peripheral rather than defining.
- **Important performer-credit caveat:** guitar personnel shifted in the recording period. Roger Rocha is credited on some release records but joined after much of the album had been completed; Shaunna Hall and Louis Metoyer were also involved. These facts do not warrant attributing every guitar part to Rocha.
- A 1993 review documents slapped electric bass and a cello-driven track, but these are individual moments, not full-record production signatures.
- Do **not** infer a synthesizer, drum machine, or any specific model from the alternative-rock genre. A Mellotron is already explicitly documented as an instrument.

### Production
`belting` only: Perry's belted voice is expressly described in The Washington Post review. No synthesizer/drum-machine model, analog-tape format, compressor, sampler or specific reverb is proven for this album, so no other tokens are added.

**Critical credit distinction:** Official producer credit remains **David Tickle**. Linda Perry has publicly explained that she rejected the original production of “What's Up?” and organised a new recording at The Plant that was ultimately used for the hit; she was not given an album production credit. Document her role in this note, not the `producers` column.

### Lyrics / scene
`social|political|introspection|storytelling`. Sources discussing “What's Up?” highlight dissatisfaction, self-questioning and social/political frustration. “Dear Mr. President” adds an explicitly political song. Lyrics vary across the album; there is no basis to make romantic love the principal theme. Scene is the San Francisco alternative-rock/blues-funk crossover of the early 1990s.

### Artist references vs. influence evidence
`sounds_like` = Concrete Blonde (via Johnette Napolitano comparison), Toni Childs, Janis Joplin and Heart (Ann Wilson). These are music criticism/vocal comparisons; no artist is claimed as a documented **1992 album influence**. `influenced_by` deliberately left empty.

**ARTIST-LEVEL influence evidence (keep out of album field):** The 14 Dec 2023 description of Linda Perry's interview with Matt Pinfield explicitly states that she talks about being influenced by Karen Carpenter, Tracey Thorn and The Who. These are broader-career influences and have **not** been independently tied to making *Bigger, Better, Faster, More!*: https://podcasts.apple.com/us/podcast/linda-perry/id1629471594?i=1000638541816

### Disagreements and scope limitations
1. AllMusic credits and MusicBrainz track credits are complementary but not identical. Preserve album-wide instrument inventory with no track-by-track generalisation.
2. The Washington Post contemporaneous critic calls most material hard rock, whereas genre aggregators lead with alternative rock and blues/funk rock. Both are represented, with evidence scope recorded.
3. Perry's dissatisfaction is about the original production of “What's Up?”, not proof that every album track was rerecorded.
4. No released second album as of 28 October 2026. The currently announced title *1994* may change before 2027 and must not be preimported.
5. A source's description of an *artist influence* does not imply a verified album-specific influence; blank means **unknown**, not denied.
