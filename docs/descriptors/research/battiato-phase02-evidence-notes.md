# Franco Battiato — Phase 02: evidence notes

This file supports **only four Phase 02 records**, never recycled Phase 01 rows. Research checked 26 October 2026. All judgments refer to **original 1975–1978 album programmes**. The descriptors are editorial proposals, not claims of audio-forensic certainty.

## `battiato-mellelegladiator` — *M.elle le “Gladiator”* (1975)

- **Identity/year:** Bla Bla BBXL 10008 (1975), independently itemised by ItalianProg. The official artist biography also places this album in 1975. Capitalisation and punctuation vary (`M.lle`, `M.elle`, French quotation marks); retain proposed title and ID after checking project's canonical spelling.
- **Genre ordering and scope:** Qualified critics describe the overall LP as **avant-garde/experimental**. Side A's `Goûtez et comparez` uses fragmented recorded voices, broadcast fragments, tape collage and the VCS3, grounding `musiqueconcrete`, `experimentalelectronic` and broader `electronic`, all **partial-album** genres. Organ improvisations on side B point toward cultivated concert music (`modernclassical` is a broad, medium-confidence tag). Do **not** call the complete album electronic or a rock record.
- **Instrumentation:** Battiato's **cathedral organ** (Monreale) dominates most of the record; **EMS VCS3 synth** is reported on the first side. `organ|synth` is ordered by prominence, not by the opening tracks. Sampled voices or sounds are sources, not additional credited instrumentalists.
- **Production:** **Tape edits/collage**, **samples**, **overdubs**, **EMS VCS3** explicitly supported by Rolling Stone, OndaRock and the album credit transcription. The cathedral setting does **not** automatically justify `liverecording` (defined by Fandome as live studio capture) or `fieldrecordings` (audio recorded outside the compositional studio), since sources describe an organ recording, not necessarily a field-recordings methodology.
- **Voice, language, lyrics:** Primarily instrumental; spoken voice scraps appear as found audio. Do not infer a coherent lyrical theme or assign a principal singing language from sampled dialogue. `instrumental|spoken`; lyrics and language deliberately empty.
- **Scores (0–5):** Energy 2 (contrast of collage interruptions with subdued organ); electronic 2 (mostly organ, some synth/tape); mood 2 (uneasy/meditative); theatrical 3 (radio and staged fragments); experimental 5 (broken-form sound montage); polish 2 (rough collage relative to standard studio); tempo 2 (irregular movement); groove 0 (no dance structure). These are critical-listening estimates, not quantified measurements.
- **Producer:** **Pino Massara**, original credits. **Comparisons** to John Cage (collage) and Tangerine Dream (synth textures) are **editorial sound likenesses**, never claimed as direct historical influences. No album-specific documented artist influence passes the `influenced_by` threshold.
- **Critic disagreement:** Fabio Zuffanti appreciates its collage as a high point; OndaRock questions the organ improvisations as possibly transitional filler. This affects valuation, not basic instrumentation. `landmark=0` under Fandome’s narrow “defining album” definition, despite importance as a pivot.

## `battiato-battiato` — *Battiato* (1977)

- **Identity/year:** Original Ricordi SMRL 6201 (1977), official album page agrees; some older official-biography chronology says **1976**, contradicted by the original discographic entry. This is not a second LP titled *Zâ*: *Zâ* was an alternative title on a later reissue.
- **Structure:** Side A `Zâ` is a radically reduced piece for solo **piano** (Antonio Ballista); side B `Cafè-Table-Musik` is a patchwork of piano, soprano singing (Alide Maria Salvetta) and spoken fragments. `piano` is the only unambiguously confirmed instrumental token. Label metadata also associates the performing artist with a synthesizer, but track-specific evidence does **not** place synth prominently on these two pieces, so it is **not** inferred.
- **Genres:** `minimalism` is unambiguously named by Jon Dale for `Zâ`. `modernclassical|avantgarde|experimental` cover the two-side experimental composition. The collage on side B is a **technique**, not sufficient alone to add the *genre* `musiqueconcrete` when reviewed sources do not explicitly assign that album-level style.
- **Production:** `tapeeffects|sonicmanipulation` describes conspicuous editing/montage of side B, substantiated by Jon Dale and Battiato’s album statement. We do **not** extrapolate specific electronics, tape machines, software, or processed voice effects; and acoustic piano resonance is **not** electronic reverb.
- **Voice, language, lyrics:** `instrumental|spoken|clear`: two sides differ, second has voiced/recited and soprano fragments. The spoken material is in Italian, but no clear source-grounded album-wide thematic lyric argument is available; lyrics blank.
- **Scores:** Energy 1; electronic 0; mood 2; theatrical 2; experimental 5; polish 3; tempo 1; groove 0. Long pauses and almost unchanged piano figure prevent treating quick local keystrokes as a fast **album** tempo.
- **Producer:** **Pino Massara** on track-by-track Qobuz master credit and Apple Music. Battiato composes; Antonio Ballista performs; Alide Maria Salvetta sings; **none** should be silently promoted to producer.
- **Critical dispute:** The official sleeve statement via OndaRock says the damper/sustain pedal is **not used**, while Jon Dale on The SHFL describes Ballista as using damper pedal technique. A real disagreement; the CSV avoids a precise pedal-mechanism assertion. The technical merit of the minimalist work has mixed reception; Jon Dale calls it a peak, an AllMusic-syndicated review is more dismissive. `landmark=1` for one of his defining non-pop releases.
- **Influence vs sonic similarity:** Terry Riley, Steve Reich and John Cage are resemblance references to minimalism and sound collage, **not source-proven artist-to-album influence**.

## `battiato-jukebox` — *Juke Box* (1978)

- **Original release/type:** Ricordi SMRL 6218, **1978 LP** with six tracks. Apple Music Classical displays it as an **EP**, but that is a *digital storefront format*, not the 1978 issue. `type=studio` reflects the original album. Initially prepared for a RAI Brunelleschi production but **rejected**; not classify as released film soundtrack. Some secondary discographies date it 1977, contradicted by original LP and ℗1978 digital metadata.
- **Genres:** Contemporary composed **chamber** repertoire (`modernclassical|classical`), experimental/avant-garde in the four-album late-1970s phase. `sacredmusic` is a **new genre token** from track `Agnus` and a discographic sacred-music categorisation; place last because it does not describe all six tracks. There is insufficient album-specific genre evidence for ambient, prog, synthpop or soundtrack sound.
- **Instrumentation:** Parts explicitly specify **violin**, **piano**, soprano and chorus, plus **strings/violin ensemble** and **trumpets** on `Agnus`; `violin|piano|strings|trumpet`. Sources credit Giusto Pio violin; Antonio Ballista and Franco Battiato piano; Alide Maria Salvetta soprano; Juri Camisasca vocal; Roberto Cacciapaglia conducts. Apple lists Battiato generically as synthesizer player, but original arrangement evidence for the six tracks is acoustic, so synth is **not** inserted.
- **Production:** `stringarrangements` has positive evidence from Rolling Stone’s `Agnus` string arrangement discussion. No particular recording chain, synthesizer, tape effect or field-recording process confirmed; no inferred gear.
- **Voice/lyrics/language:** Most music is instrumental chamber writing, but soprano and choir are materially present (`instrumental|clear|choral`). `Agnus` is a Latin mass prayer (`la`) with clearly spiritual content; other vocal passages include Italian text derived from Fleur Jaeggy (`it`). Ordering `it|la` is a broad assessment rather than an exact sung-word count.
- **Scores:** Energy 2; electronic 0; mood 2; theatrical 3 (soprano and choral scenes); experimental 4; polish 3; tempo 2; groove 0. Mixed brief pieces differ strongly from the side-length piano work before and after.
- **Production credits disagreement:** Apple’s digital label metadata names **Pino Massara and Paolo Scarnecchia** as producers; an Italian-language Wikipedia infobox instead names **Franco Battiato**. The CSV follows professionally furnished digital label credits, **medium confidence**, and calls for a scan of the original Ricordi LP production line before marking reviewed. **Giusto Pio** is a **co-composer and performer**, not an implied producer and **not a co-billed album artist**.
- **Reception:** OndaRock and Fabio Zuffanti (Rolling Stone) both regard it as unusually forbidding, but cite the effective soprano and `Agnus` string settings. `landmark=0` within the artist's broader discography; an important transitional experiment.
- **Similarity/influence:** Luciano Berio is only an **editorial sonic comparison** grounded in abstract chamber-vocal composition. He appeared among music substituted into the *Brunelleschi* film, **not evidence that his work inspired the rejected score**. `influenced_by` intentionally blank.

## `battiato-legittoprimadellesabbie` — *L’Egitto prima delle sabbie* (1978)

- **Release-year issue:** Ricordi **SMRL 6220 (1978)**, corroborated by ItalianProg, the artist biography and 1978 ℗ master metadata. Apple Music and AllMusic show **1979** in some release-date headings, likely later catalogue/storefront dating. Use **1978 original Italian LP**, document discrepancy. This is a single Battiato album (not Giusto Pio co-billing).
- **Works and players:** Title piece (c.14m) **solo piano, Antonio Ballista**. `Sud Afternoon` (c.18m) **two pianos, Ballista and Bruno Canino**. AllMusic's Ned Raggett explicitly says the **music heard is only piano**, with no Battiato performance and no voices. Library Braidense confirms duo piano scoring and performers. Retail metadata's generic Battiato synth/vocal artist role is **not track-specific**: do not put `synth`, any production effect or `clear` vocals in these rows.
- **Genres and influence:** `minimalism` is directly described by Mediterranean Minimalism and Rolling Stone; repeated notes, acoustic resonances, the spaces between notes and subtle harmonic activity are central. `modernclassical|avantgarde|experimental` are similarly sourced. `classical` is the broad parent; AllMusic metadata tags `Neo-Classical` (`neoclassical`) but its own reviewer stresses unusual minimalistic concert music, so the last tag is **low-strength**, with explicit provenance. AllMusic also shows generic Pop/Rock, Euro-Pop, New Age, etc.; these conflict with a piano-only original recording and are excluded as unreliable site-taxonomy carryovers, **not independent album genre analysis**.
- **Production:** No source documents a distinctive recording **technique/machine** for the 1978 original. Empty `production` is required: natural piano decays are a performance/compositional strategy, not evidence for artificial reverb or overdubbing. `instruments=piano` solely.
- **Producer credit:** Producer **Pino Massara** appears as **Natale Massara** in AllMusic; Apple also credits **Paolo Scarnecchia**, whose exact original LP function requires review. **Franco Battiato** is the composer, but Ned Raggett loosely calls him 'producer' in prose while the formal master credits distinguish other names. Keep `Pino Massara|Paolo Scarnecchia` as discographic streaming credits pending a label scan. Giusto Pio is documented as a compositional collaborator/contemporary, **not album co-producer or performing pianist** here.
- **Lyrics/voice/language:** Entirely instrumental, leave `lyrics` and `language` empty; `voice=instrumental`.
- **Scores:** Energy 1 (quiet/meditative), electronic 0, mood 3 (reflective, neither dark nor euphoric), theatrical 0, experimental 5, polish 3, tempo 2 (short repeated fast notes in very long slowly changing compositions), groove 0. These descriptions represent full-album averages.
- **Influence restraint:** The album title is associated with **Gurdjieff**, and musical scholarship notes spiritual engagement. A philosopher is not a source-qualified **musician influence for `influenced_by`**, and AllMusic's Steve Reich comparison is **similarity**, not documentary proof of a direct influence: therefore `sounds_like=Steve Reich`, `influenced_by` blank. Stockhausen’s name is attached to an award, which is also **not** proof of album-specific musical influence.
- **Landmark:** `1` because of its award, substantial classical-minimalist contribution and repeated critical appraisal as a defining experimental Battiato LP.

## Cross-album / source reliability

- Full published review citations and exact links are in `battiato_phase02_sources.csv`; individual genre attributions, including their whole-album / some-tracks scope, are in `battiato_phase02_genre_evidence.csv`.
- Editorial scales and `sounds_like` are **human qualitative descriptors**. The source table provides the anchors for judgement; it does not imply a critic used numeric values.
- **Piano resonance ≠ electronic effects.** A catalogue recording year (℗) or a storefront reissue year is not by itself an original release date. Editorial names appearing as reviewers/arrangers/composers are not automatically producers.
- No `influenced_by` claims were invented to make rows more complete. Prior artist-level influence study (Stockhausen / Cage / Bach etc.) belongs to the earlier *artist influences* supplement; none is assigned to these individual releases unless original statements directly connect them.
- `new genre`: **minimalism**, **sacredmusic**; no unlisted instrument or production token; every `instruments`/`production` entry corresponds to the respective original recording, not genre inference.
