# Cerrone — Phase 01: evidence notes

**Scope:** original 1976–78 albums, *not* remastered bonus tracks, singles, extended remix packages or the 2014 anthology *Cerrone I, II, III*. Numeric scales are original Fandome album-wide editorial judgements. Per-genre entries distinguish album-level style from track-limited evidence. All descriptions are `draft`.

## 1. Love in C Minor (1976) — `cerrone-loveincminor`

- **Format/credits.** Cerrone's own catalogue verifies three original pieces: the long title cut, a disco rearrangement of “Black Is Black”, and “Midnite Lady”. Original label/LP transcription credits Cerrone as **producer and drummer**, with Mo Foster (bass), Alan Hawkshaw and Don Ray (keyboards), Hugh Burns/Collin Green (guitars), Ray Swinfield (flute/sax), John Watson's brass section, Pat Halling's strings and live percussion. **Alec R. Costandinos is co-writer, not securely album-credited producer**: some retrospective essays mention co-production, but the explicit original jacket producer line says Cerrone. Verify jacket before adding another producer.
- **Genres/sound.** Original label: Disco; Qobuz wider Soul/Funk/R&B classification. AllMusic files album under Electronic but styles it Disco. OndaRock locates the long orchestral suite in *European disco* and identifies funk pulses. `electronic` is a low-ranked texture: actual recording is mainly acoustic band/orchestra, not late synth-disco. `dance` captures the specialist dance-music description; it is a broad parent.
- **Production.** Cerrone told MusicRadar he recorded kick, snare and hi-hat in separate passes, then added bass and guitar, and explicitly played Barry White examples for Raymond Donnez/Don Ray to arrange **harmonised brass**. This supports `overdubbing|hornarrangements|stringarrangements`; it does **not** justify a drum-machine tag. His famous machine-like four-on-the-floor kick was **played on drums**.
- **Scales/lyrics.** Energy 4 / groove 5 reflect club suite and pronounced live pulse; electronic 1 reflects orchestra/band focus; experimental 3 reflects an extended 16-minute track; vocal `choral|soft` reflects the recurring backing chorus. Sensuality/love dominate, not elaborate story lyrics. Original reviewer John Bush calls it midtempo; tempo 4 refers to steady dance pace rather than rapid beats.
- **Influence.** Primary quotes in FLOOD, Mixmag, La Face B and MusicRadar explicitly establish **Barry White's strings**, **Chicago's horn voicing**, and the **Isaac Hayes / Shaft** wah guitar reference, rather than an inferred comparison. They justify the `influenced_by` list. Cerrone's collaborators **Don Ray** and Costandinos never go in `influenced_by`.

## 2. Cerrone's Paradise (1977) — `cerrone-cerronesparadise`

- **Catalog.** This is the **second original Cerrone studio LP** but it is missing from Fandome `catalog.ts`. It has four tracks including an end reprise. The artist's 1977 Bandcamp issue and original Italian Malligator LP both support this structure. Original pressing credits **Producer: Cerrone**; **Alain Wisniak** is a *writer*, not the co-producer. Don Ray arranged the music according to a detailed historical album review but is not explicitly original-album producer.
- **Genres.** Qobuz's licensed AllMusic review calls the dance numbers *Euro-disco* and “Time For Love” a **quiet-storm R&B slow jam**. A 2015 specialist track analysis documents the funk-inflected “Take Me”, layered strings and brass, and both synth and wah-wah-guitar textures. This justifies `disco|eurodisco|funk|soul|rnb|electronic|quietstorm`, with `quietstorm` explicitly **some tracks**. It is *not* uniformly up-tempo.
- **Instrumentation.** Analyst identifies **steel pan/steel drum** percussion on “Take Me”, and guitars, strings, brass, synthesizers and rhythm section across the record. `steeldrums` is track-limited; this is not a Caribbean-music genre claim.
- **Language.** Detailed retrospective says “Time For Love” alternates **English verses and French chorus**: `en|fr`; this deserves checking against original vocals but is more precise than inferring language from French LP location.
- **Scales.** Strong 4-on-floor album framework (groove 5, energy 4) tempered by the single slow soul ballad (tempo 4 album-wide, experimental 2). High polish 4, moderate electronics 2.
- **Influenced_by.** Empty: citing Cerrone's own 1976 blueprint or arranging personnel does not establish an album-specific third-party musical influence. The Barry White comparisons belong to `sounds_like`.

## 3. Supernature (Cerrone III) (1977) — `cerrone-supernaturecerroneiii`

- **Catalog.** Fandome's *exact* title/ID is preserved. The LP comprises six titles, not the **2024** digital package also titled *Supernature*, which contains alternative edits of the title song only. Label metadata and the Philharmonie de Paris music library confirm Cerrone's **sole production**; Wisniak co-wrote music, and Lene Lovich wrote lyrics. Their writing credits do **not** establish producer status.
- **Genres.** AlbumOfTheYear lists **Euro-Disco, Electro-Disco and Space Disco**, all independent valid tokens; Qobuz lists Disco; OndaRock verifies electronic disco sequences; Shfl and SensCritique note a **funk/soul** core, more apparent on side B. The synthetic title suite receives stronger influence on the whole-album `electronic=4`; do not imply *every song* is sequenced.
- **Production & instrument evidence.** First-person 2018 Electronic Sound interview explicitly associates *Supernature* sessions with Cerrone's **ARP Odyssey** and a repeating sequence; RBMA lecture independently confirms his struggle to work the synth. `arpodyssey` is justified. Technical caveat: his informal story describes a “sequencer” sound, but does **not** prove the Odyssey contained an internal sequencer. Other instruments follow Philharmonie de Paris's listing: electric piano, synthesizer, electronic keyboards, electric guitar, bass guitar, drums, bongos, congas and percussion. Synth and sequencer are separate instrument tokens.
- **Lyrics/voice.** Sci-fi ecology and the warning against chemical genetic manipulation explain `social|political` plus the love-themed flip side. Voice is female-led `clear|choral`; `instrumental` would be wrong for the entire album. **The original vocalist is disputed** in old accounts: authoritative Pitchfork and a 2024 first-person statement reported by OndaRock identify **Kay Garner**, though Cerrone has previously given imprecise recollections linking Lene Lovich to singing. The `voice` field avoids a singer identity claim.
- **Influence.** In Electronic Sound Cerrone directly says he was a **big Kraftwerk fan** and impressed by their records at the time of making *Supernature*, qualifying Kraftwerk for `influenced_by`. Critical comparisons with Giorgio Moroder and Donna Summer's “I Feel Love” are editorial `sounds_like`, not automatic historical influences. The science-fiction novel inspiration is **non-musical** and not an `influenced_by` artist.
- **Scales.** Conceptual sci-fi darkness drives mood 2, theatrical 4, experimental 4. The more hopeful “Give Me Love” and “Love Is The Answer” stop the record from scoring mood 0. Groove 5 and polish 5 for repeated sequences layered with ensemble.

## 4. Cerrone IV: The Golden Touch (1978) — `cerrone-cerroneivthegoldentouch`

- **Catalog.** Match *exactly* the Fandome title including “Cerrone IV:” and existing ID. Original four-track LP has “Je Suis Music”, “Rocket In The Pocket”, “Look For Love”, “Music Of Life”. Cerrone's official Bandcamp item describes original 1978 album, not bonus remixes.
- **Genres.** Critic Alex Henderson calls it richly orchestrated *Euro-disco* and notes a brighter, less dystopian mood than 1977. Qobuz tags Soul/Funk/R&B Disco, while period LP metadata also groups Electronic/Pop. The latter two are subordinate flavours (`rnb`, `electronic`, `pop`), not claims that this is primarily a synth-pop record.
- **Instruments/recording.** Original LP credits list Cerrone drums, Christian Padovan bass, guitar Larry Juber, Don Ray, Georges Rodi and Cerrone synthesizers, Lance Dixon piano, strings and brass including French horn, percussion and chorus. Don Ray is **arranger**; producer is **Cerrone**. The 1978 sleeve states a **48-track** recording at Trident (London) and Ferber (Paris) in May–July 1978; multi-tracking is factual but an unverified specific tape recorder model must not be inserted.
- **Page-credit conflict.** Cerrone's first-person Gonzaï and RBMA accounts say **Jimmy Page** contributed to fourth LP and specifically “Rocket In The Pocket”; accessible original-sleeve lists name **Larry Juber** and do not show Page. Do not put Page in `instruments` (which uses instrument names) or `influenced_by`, do not replace the credited guitarist, and preserve the conflict for an original liner-note scan. Similarly the LP transcription's “Translated By Lene Lovich” is **not** a singer/producer credit.
- **Lyrics/scales.** “Je Suis Music” contains social concerns and songs “Look For Love” and “Music Of Life” celebrate love/community. Hence mood 4, theatrical 4, polish 5, groove 5; a cohesive four-track club LP is less experimental than *Supernature* (2).

## General review limitations

- Producer fields only contain people explicitly credited **as producers**. Arrangement, co-writing, a cameo, a track's cover composition or a credit for an unrelated release never makes a producer or influence.
- `sounds_like` is an editorial similarity list; it is **not** a historical claim that these acts directly influenced the recording. Some songs cited in album descriptions come from source critics and may differ between revised editions.
- Qobuz's broad shelf “Soul/Funk/R&B Disco” supports subgenre presence but not equal weighting; the genre-evidence CSV explicitly labels track-limited and textural entries.
- The exact date within 1977 is inconsistent among **1977 original LP documentation, metadata backfills and different national releases**. Fandome uses release **year** only. The official Bandcamp “January 1” dates are placeholders for original years, not documented actual street days.
- Some of the original-sleeve personnel are retail transcriptions of Discogs submissions, independently corroborated where possible; verify scan against original press before marking anything `reviewed`.
