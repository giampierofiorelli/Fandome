# Public Enemy — phase 01 evidence notes

Research scope: four chronological albums, 1987–1991. Descriptors are independently judged; all sources are in the companion CSV. Every row is **draft**, not second-person reviewed. Citations use real published URLs; critical interpretation differs from liner-note fact.

## 1. Yo! Bum Rush the Show (1987)

- **Production/credits.** The original credits distinguish Bill Stephney (producer), Carl Ryder and Hank Shocklee (co-producers), and Rick Rubin (executive producer and mixer of one track). Only the first three belong in `producers`. Both AllMusic and MusicBrainz supply those roles. **No Rick Rubin producer credit has been invented.**
- **Instruments.** Drum programming (Eric Sadler and Hank Shocklee), synth, scratches/turntables (Terminator X and Johnny Juice), played guitar (Vernon Reid and Bill Stephney) and bass (Stephney and Steven Linsley) are in credits. The 2006 *Mix* analysis names the Ensoniq Mirage used for the debut; that supports `sampler` and `ensoniqmirage`. Avoid hard-coded drum-machine model for the album because the equipment source is less specific than it is for Nation.
- **Genres.** AllMusic lists political/hardcore/East Coast rap; canonical Fandome types use hiphop, hardcorehiphop and eastcoasthiphop. Political Rap becomes **new token `politicalrap`**. Official PE store describes prominent guitar blended with rap/rock; raprock is an album-level crossover rather than pretending the record is predominantly conventional rock.
- **Scales.** Energy 4 and theatrical 4 reflect commanding and emphatic delivery; electronic 3 balances programmed rhythms against played guitar/bass; experimental 3 and polish 2 are deliberately lower than the 1988 breakthrough. Groove 4 reflects sharp and danceable syncopation, below full club-oriented 5.
- **Influence vs comparison.** The stylistic Run-D.M.C. resemblance is editorial and sits in `sounds_like`, not `influenced_by`. The Clash comparison made by Apple editorial is not by itself a documented debut-specific production influence. Album-specific influences are left empty.
- **Date caveat.** The year 1987 is secure, but release-month claims differ across sources; the CSV intentionally specifies only the year.

Sources: https://www.allmusic.com/album/yo%21-bum-rush-the-show-mw0000194784, https://musicbrainz.org/release/eeea7eb2-4cc5-4255-a29a-59976df2bf53, https://publicenemy.clothing/collections/shop-all/products/yo-bum-rush-the-show-lp, https://reverb.com/news/the-production-of-public-enemy-gear-sampling-and-embracing-distortion, https://www.worldradiohistory.com/Archive-All-Audio/Mix-Magazine/00s/2006/Mix-2006-11.pdf.

## 2. It Takes a Nation of Millions to Hold Us Back (1988)

- **Production/credits.** Original LP/track credits name Hank Shocklee and Carl Ryder as producers; Eric Sadler is documented as assistant producer, and Bill Stephney as production supervisor. Neither should be silently promoted to album producer. Rick Rubin was executive producer. The Bomb Squad is a group term, not an additional producer person.
- **Instruments/production.** Samples, turntables, programmed beat sources and synth/electronic timbres build the record. *MusicTech* and Reverb describe SP-1200 and Akai S-900 / sampler work; a compression-and-distortion method is given in Reverb's first-person Shocklee statements. The guitar timbre on 'She Watch Channel Zero?!' is a **sampled Slayer riff**, not evidence of a fresh Slayer performance. `guitar` denotes an audible instrument texture, not a new instrumentalist.
- **Genres.** AllMusic's broad rap and hardcore/East Coast styles, MusicBrainz's conscious/political tags, plus the critic's precise discussion of musique concrète and free jazz, explain nine ordered genre tokens. `musiqueconcrete`, `funk`, `freejazz` and `raprock` are **subordinate source-texture/track labels**; the last four do not claim the album is primarily rock/funk/jazz.
- **Scales.** The near-continuous shouted hooks, short high-density samples and confrontational arrangements justify energy 5, theatrical 5, electronic 4, experimental 5. Polish 3 acknowledges complex deliberate layering without mistaking deliberately rough/noisy sound for glossy recording. Mood 1 reflects urgency rather than literal lyric happiness.
- **Influences.** In an interview specifically about the album, Chuck D names **Bob Marley, James Brown and the Clash** in his account of what the group drew on. This meets the stricter artist testimony rule. Sampled artists not separately named by band/producer as influences are **not** auto-added. AllMusic compares the rap-crew-as-rock-band template with Run-D.M.C.; this is `sounds_like`, not album-specific influence testimony. Chuck D has called the record an effort akin to Marvin Gaye's *What's Going On*, a conceptual aim but not direct evidence of copying musical arrangements, so left out of influenced_by.

## 3. Fear of a Black Planet (1990)

- **Credits.** AllMusic individually credits Carl Ryder, Eric Sadler, Hank Shocklee and Keith Shocklee as producers, and sequenced/programmed sources. MusicBrainz attributes recording production to the Bomb Squad. Eric Sadler and Keith Shocklee are **not** dropped merely because some overviews mention only the team.
- **Instruments.** Sampler/turntable-led rhythmic and media sound fragments, sequenced elements, and guitar sounds sampled from Dennis Coffey are documented. Branford Marsalis appears with **saxophone** in AllMusic album credits; that is a specific player credit. Do not confuse a sax part on *Fight the Power*'s film version with the album recording; the row asserts an album-wide credited instrument rather than that every version uses it.
- **Genres.** MusicBrainz release group separately identifies conscious and experimental hip hop, and AllMusic identifies political rap. Hard funk and touches of dub reggae receive **lower priority** because AllMusic describes textures/individual pieces, not a pure funk or dub album. The descriptors deliberately retain the production style's wider range.
- **Scales and comparison.** Denser collages but also more elastic grooves than *Nation*, per Pitchfork. Electronic 4, experimental 5, groove 4. Beastie Boys' *Paul's Boutique* is AllMusic's explicit sample-collage comparison. Apple Music uses Sonic Youth as a sonic metaphor. Both belong to `sounds_like` only.
- **The Beatles.** Apple Music says Chuck D aimed for the scale of *Sgt. Pepper's Lonely Hearts Club Band*. This statement may describe an artistic **ambition/benchmark**, not a precise musical influence on the recordings. The album's `influenced_by` is left blank pending stronger testimony. No sampled artist is silently treated as an influence.

Sources: https://www.allmusic.com/album/fear-of-a-black-planet-mw0000206260, https://musicbrainz.org/release-group/13cf2244-c850-304d-bc46-b30e3f10e0cc, https://musicbrainz.org/release/4c288b5d-6f29-492c-9669-9b0796412636, https://pitchfork.com/reviews/albums/19997-public-enemy-it-takes-a-nation-of-millions-to-hold-us-backfear-of-a-black-planet/, https://music.apple.com/us/album/fear-of-a-black-planet/1440837282, https://au.rollingstone.com/music/music-features/public-enemy-fear-of-a-black-planet-history-36890/.

## 4. Apocalypse 91... The Enemy Strikes Black (1991)

- **Credits critical distinction.** The contemporaneous *Gavin Report* advert directly credits Imperial Grand Ministers of Funk (Stuart Robertz, Cerwin C-Dawg Depper, Gary G-Wiz and the J.I.B./JBL) as **producers**, the Bomb Squad as **executive producers**. AllMusic's individual credits independently support Gary G-Wiz, Stuart Robertz, Cerwin Depper, The JBL as producers. Their stylized form is retained; the original ad spells J.I.B., while AllMusic spells JBL. Verify person identity/alias when importing, rather than inventing a resolution. The original team is NOT entered as producer by virtue of an executive credit. Amoeba also offers a non-specific Hank Shocklee 'producer' listing, which conflicts with the more precise period advert; the row follows the **album-level period credit** and notes possible track-specific exceptions.
- **Instruments.** Specific AllMusic/Amoeba credited players: Terminator X (turntables), Al MacDowell (bass), Richard/Rick Gordon (drums), Fred Wells and Anthrax personnel (guitar), Frank Abel (keyboards), Steve Moss (congas). Horn credits exist, but instrument type is unspecified, so horns are not inaccurately mapped to trumpets or saxophones.
- **Production.** Gary G-Wiz and Stuart Robertz have explicit programming credits, and record uses sampled material. Avoid claiming particular drum-machine/sampler models because the sources don't establish an exact model for **this** album.
- **Genre scope.** Hardcore, political and East Coast hip-hop dominate. Rap-rock and thrash-metal refer **only to the Anthrax version of 'Bring tha Noize'**, at the end; neither genre is promoted to album's primary style. These are downstream tokens in the genre column.
- **Scales and disagreement.** Contemporary *Washington Post* called the record funkier and less chaotic; AllMusic found its attack slicker, aggressive and relentless. This supports relative experimental 3/polish 4 compared with 1988–90, while preserving energy 5.
- **Influences/comparisons.** Anthrax are guest performers, never listed in `influenced_by`. No credible album-specific artist statements were found for other direct influences; the field is blank, not fabricated.
