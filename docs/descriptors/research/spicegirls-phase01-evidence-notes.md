# Spice Girls — Phase 01 evidence notes

Scope: UK girl-group's **three released studio albums**, through 25 October 2026. The entire source-backed draft is incremental; no other artists or previous phases. These are editorial judgements keyed to the same 0–5 anchors used by Fandome, **not** claims that a review assigned a numerical rating. The genre evidence file separately marks whole-album, some-tracks and texture scope.

## Spice (1996) — `spicegirls-spice`

- **Core genres:** AllMusic lists dance-pop/pop/teen pop/Euro-pop/Euro-dance/club-dance/adult contemporary; Pitchfork explicitly analyses a pop/R&B album. No inference that a source's umbrella “Pop/Rock” means rock is entered.
- **Minor/track genres:** Aimee Cliff (Pitchfork, 2019) calls out Northern-soul-flavoured singles, disco on “Who Do You Think You Are,” G-funk flavour in “Say You'll Be There,” and softer R&B ballads. A 2026 Albumism review discusses funk and hip-hop elements. Those labels occur **late in the genre list**; they do not define the entire album.
- **Scales:** energy 4 and groove 4 reflect multiple uptempo/chant-driven singles balanced against several ballads (tempo 3). Electronic 3 represents credited programmed keyboard parts together with guitar, harmonica and acoustic strings. Mood 4 tracks the deliberately positive, friendship-first stance tempered by tenderness; theatrical 3 means extroverted performances without a theatrical concept. Experimental 1 and polish 4 reflect short, conventional song structures with tightly produced arrangements (Pitchfork/AllMusic).
- **Voices:** five shared leads and harmonies are documented by retrospective critics. `rap` is present in selected verses (“Last Time Lover,” per Pitchfork) rather than an album-wide primary vocal approach; ordering reflects typical leads. `layeredvocals` describes polyphonic multi-singer harmonies, **not** an assertion of a particular overdubbing device.
- **Instruments:** keyboard parts credited to Stannard/Rowe, Judd Lander harmonica, Greg Lester guitar, cello by Tony Ward and violin by Jackie Drew (MusicBrainz, original CD). `strings` covers credited orchestral strings on “2 Become 1”; `synth` has a separate critic's description of synthesizer squeals on “Who Do You Think You Are” (Pitchfork). **Do not infer a particular drum machine from programming credits**.
- **Production:** `sampling` is backed by the identified Digital Underground sample in “If U Can't Dance.” No Auto-Tune credited or assigned. 
- **Producers:** Stannard, Rowe, and Absolute are principal producers; Andy Bradfield is documented as **additional/co-producer on selected tracks**, so retained in the producer field. Do not list Eliot Kennedy or Jon B merely because they are co-writers, and do not confuse engineer/mixer Mark Stent with this album's producer.
- **Lyrical themes:** friendship / female self-definition (mapped conservatively to `identity|social`), romance (`love`), “2 Become 1” (`sensuality`) and introspective ballads (`introspection`). The controlled vocabulary does not include “friendship.”
- **Influence caution:** Quentin Harrison's Albumism account states the band had studied En Vogue, TLC and Bananarama; this is a **qualified critic's retrospective inference** rather than a first-person quote. For audit conservatism, an importer could remove `influenced_by` until a contemporary band interview is checked. `sounds_like` is editorial resemblance.
- **Critic disagreement:** Pitchfork argues that imperfect vocal delivery contributes personality; AllMusic prefers charm and efficiency over claims of vocal virtuosity. Both depict a crafted, immediately accessible pop album.

## Spiceworld (1997) — `spicegirls-spiceworld`

- **Core genres:** AllMusic categorises it as pop/dance-pop/teen-pop/Euro-pop/Euro-dance/club-dance/adult contemporary. Albumism describes the deliberate expansion of the pop/R&B formula.
- **Styles in specific songs:** the Albumism analysis explicitly assigns jazz to “The Lady Is a Vamp”, doo-wop to “Too Much”, Motown/soul to “Stop”, disco to “Never Give Up on the Good Times”, synth-funk/new jack swing/freestyle to selected R&B tracks, and Spanish folk to “Viva Forever.” AllMusic calls the neo-Motown material blue-eyed soul and names Latin/blues elements. Slate calls “Spice Up Your Life” Latin pop and the jazz number big-band. These are **track-scope labels, not equal-weight album genres**.
- **Scales:** energy 4 and groove 4 follow the propulsive dance tracks; tempo 3 reflects midtempo Motown and a prominent slow ballad. Electronic 3 because extensive programming coexists with separately credited guitar, bass, brass, woodwind and strings. Mood 4 is mostly exuberant, with nostalgic “Viva Forever.” Theatrical 4 follows show-tune/big-band presentation and enlarged pop arrangements; experimental 2 reflects stylised genre quotation without avant-garde structure. Polish 5 reflects densely controlled major-label arrangements (and is described negatively by the 1997 LA Times review).
- **Voices:** a retrospective specialist explicitly describes two-, three-, four- and five-part harmonies, hence `layeredvocals` in the broad *vocal use* sense. A stronger lead voice is heard in songs singled out by AllMusic for improvement, but the rating is an editorial judgement; no specific digital vocal processor is credited.
- **Instruments:** original-CD personnel (Bondegezou transcription, supported by MusicBrainz/Qobuz): Matt Rowe keyboards; Milton McDonald and Shawn Lee guitar; Kick Horns brass; Steve Lewinson / Paul Williams bass; Snake Davis flute; Pure Stringz strings; John Themis acoustic guitar. **A string arrangement credit is not necessarily evidence of an orchestra playing in full.** No synthesiser model or DAW is asserted.
- **Production:** documented keyboard/programming credits do not by themselves establish any machine token. `layeredvocals` denotes the sourced complex harmony textures; no `sampling` because no album-specific source confirmed it.
- **Producers:** only Richard Stannard, Matt Rowe, and the duo credited as `Absolute` (Andy Watkins and Paul Wilson). Engineering, orchestration, and mixing personnel excluded.
- **Themes:** friendship and agency (identity/social), romance, ending/remembering (“Viva Forever,” nostalgia), dance-party energy.
- **Critic disagreement:** Stephen Thomas Erlewine (AllMusic) describes a stronger, more consistent second album, whereas Natalie Nichols (LA Times, 1997) calls it overprocessed and lacking energy. The latter is not interpreted as proof of technical methods; both evaluations are recorded.
- **Influence caution:** En Vogue/TLC/Bananarama is based on Quentin Harrison's specialist historical assessment of the group's musical education. It is not an assertion that those artists appear on, sampled by or co-produced the LP. In particular Motown is a stylistic vocabulary here, not an album personnel attribution.

## Forever (2000) — `spicegirls-forever`

- **Core genres:** The group's official biography describes a turn toward R&B as four members; Albumism identifies contemporary R&B-pop, AllMusic teen pop/dance-pop/adult contemporary. The era's studio sound is more syncopated and less bright than the first two records.
- **Secondary genres:** Quentin Harrison calls out vintage synth-funk and downtempo passages; an original-era review reprinted by Tinnitist identifies hip-hop-influenced textures and funk grooves; Pop Rescue identifies R&B/garage in “Holler.” These are differentiated from whole-album tags, and garage is **only documented as a local track feature**. Ballads are a substantial part of the sequence.
- **Scales:** energy 3, tempo 3, groove 3 reflect restrained midtempo R&B and fewer communal party chants alongside upbeat “Holler.” Electronic 4 follows the critic-described synth/drum-machine textures, while an identified acoustic-guitar line on “Holler” keeps it below 5. Mood 3 balances warmth, frustration, love and farewells. Theatrical 2 is a more subdued shared delivery. Experimental 2 denotes contemporary genre refresh rather than radical formal experiment. Polish 5 follows high-control, superstar R&B studio production as discussed by period critics.
- **Voices:** Pop Rescue describes unusually restrained delivery and occasional Mel B rap; warm harmonies remain. Not tagged `autotune` or `vocoder` based solely on a critic's description of a “robotic voice” in one song.
- **Instruments:** `synth` is explicitly mentioned in Darryl Sterdan's original-era review reprinted in 2025. `acousticguitar` on “Holler” is specified by the Pop Rescue album track review. The liner transcription lists Jam/Lewis on all instruments for two tracks but **does not say which instruments**, so no generic keyboard or specific drum machine is inferred from that credit. The “Goodbye” string-arrangement credit is not enough to assert a whole album's played string section.
- **Production:** liner-note personnel transcription credits Harvey Mason Jr. with Pro Tools on “Holler,” “Let Love Lead the Way” and “Weekend Love,” so `protools` is assigned with **medium confidence until an original booklet is independently checked**. LaShawn Daniels is credited with vocal production, not inserted into an album producer-only field; vocal producers/arrangers with separate roles remain distinguished.
- **Producers:** MusicBrainz track credits confirm Rodney Jerkins, Fred Jerkins III, Harvey Mason Jr., Robert Smith, Jimmy Jam, Terry Lewis, Richard Stannard and Matt Rowe. **Robert Smith here is the credited album collaborator, not an assertion that The Cure frontman produced this record.** Eliot Kennedy and Sue Drake have *additional vocal production* credits on “Right Back at Ya” but no unequivocal full producer credit in the MusicBrainz data examined; do not silently promote them to album producers.
- **Lyrical themes:** love, breakups/uncertainty, reflection and identity as a four-piece. “Goodbye” was initially released as a single in 1998 but belongs to the standard 2000 album; there is no new standalone row.
- **Influence caution:** the period-review voice reprinted in Tinnitist explicitly references TLC and Missy Elliott in describing the contemporary musical model. They are not on the credits. The field is **critic-supported interpretation**, not a confirmed group interview; candidates for later human review.
- **Critic disagreement:** contemporary LA Times sees a restrained but credible adult R&B makeover; AllMusic and later Pop Rescue view it as overcalculated and less distinctive. A specialised Albumism 2020 retrospective argues the group continued its earlier R&B trajectory. Values are evidence-guided editorial averages rather than a consensus of critical quality.

## General cautions

- Editions change track ordering/bonus content. Use original core studio track lists, **not anniversary bonus tracks, remixes, single edits, or movie tie-ins** as extra album rows.
- The `Spiceworld` album and the *Spice World* film are not distinct full studio album rows merely because the record accompanies the film.
- AllMusic pages are discoverable in search but some automated fetches return 403; citations identify precise album pages for normal browser access. The Bondegezou original-CD transcription returned 502 in automated open, although its detailed search result displayed credits. Cross-check its information against MusicBrainz/Qobuz in the original CD before treating it as an independent primary document.
- Albumism is an album-focused retrospective by a named critic, not a contemporary primary production record. Editorial resemblance (`sounds_like`) and documented/critic-inferred influence (`influenced_by`) are separate fields.
