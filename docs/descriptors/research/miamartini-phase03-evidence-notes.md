# Mia Martini: phase 03 evidence notes

**Corpus / date.** Read-only Fandome `descriptors` branch checked 30 October 2026. The exact artist ID is `miamartini`. GitHub's current `docs/descriptors/miamartini.csv` contains only the four records from 1971–1974. The phase 02 albums are already in the catalogue but are **not included** here, whether or not they have been imported by Claude. This phase adds only the four chronological studio albums listed below. The 1983 *Miei compagni di viaggio* was recorded in concert and is excluded.

**Interpretation of scales.** Fandome scales are editorial album-wide 0–5 judgments, calibrated against the published guide's reference albums. They are not numerical source ratings and do not transfer automatically from artist to album. Every uncertain instrument or piece of equipment is kept empty rather than inferred from musical genre.

## `miamartini-danza` — *Danza* (1978; 11 tracks)

**Edition and conflicting dates.** Warner Bros. T 56610, original ℗ 1978 (WEA reproduction and Hit Parade Italia). Some later catalogues and reissues call this a **1979** album. Fandome's existing title/year are **Danza (1978)**, so preserve them. 1978 *Vola* and the 1979 *Danza/Canto alla luna* single should not become separate album rows.

**Genres.** `pop`: original WEA/LP described in contemporary Italian pop context. `songwriter`: Fossati wrote and composed the complete eleven-track cycle (Rockol and original song credits) and its arrangements; the authorial character is central, not a guest credit. `ballad`: OndaRock's Ivano Fossati monograph explicitly describes **La costruzione di un amore** as a ballad first recorded on Martini's *Danza*; it is not an assertion of an entire LP of ballads. `musicaleggera`: contemporary Italian melodic-pop song context, with several short intimate songs. This is a broad, partly overlapping term: no rock token is extrapolated from OndaRock's evocative *rock bambino* phrase. Pop-rock and folk instruments are audible in the reviews but not securely labelled **album-wide** with an exact genre name. The four genres here prioritize supported labels, at the cost of a narrower genre spread. The dissonance between two ballads and livelier group pieces matters for the scales.

**Personnel.** Original sleeve transcription at Hit Parade Italia names Guglielminetti (bass/percussion), Testolina and Dall'Aglio (drums), Fossati (acoustic and classical guitars, mandovox, cetra, flute, piccolo, vibraphone, percussion and vocals), Puzzolu (electric and classical guitar), Zanaboni (piano, electric piano/Rhodes, Hammond organ, ARP Omni, accordion and backing vocals), Vaona (keyboards on *Vola*), Bianchi (sax), and other identified players. Hence the long, deliberately **main-first** instrument list, which includes `mandovox` and `cetra` as directly credited, genuinely new instrument tokens. Vibraphone maps to `mallets`; piccolo is already an instrument token. Production token `arpomni` is an exact fit to the sleeve's *Omni ARP*; the band practiced together in a studio, but Fossati's account does not say the master was a one-take live recording. No invented tape format, synth model other than ARP Omni, or stereo processing.

**Production dispute — needs review, not concealed.** OndaRock, Fossati's participant interview and the original musical-arrangement credit establish **Ivano Fossati** as the album's artistic leader/producer. Hit Parade's top-line "Production: Piero Colasanti" and a WEA CD reproduction also say **Piero Colasanti**, while the detailed original LP transcription identifies him specifically as **executive producer**, a role the owner's rules exclude. The row currently lists `Ivano Fossati` only to avoid including an executive credit. An original Warner gatefold photograph could settle whether Colasanti also has a separate ordinary producer credit. *Vola* is excepted from Fossati's arranging/realization credit: Danilo Vaona arranged it, Natale Massara realised it; neither is assumed to be producer without unambiguous source credit.

**Eight scales.** Energy **3**, tempo **3**: band-led and sometimes brisk *Danza*, *Ci si muove* versus slower *La costruzione di un amore*, *Buonanotte dolce notte*. Electronic **2**: keyboards, Fender Rhodes and ARP Omni appear but live musicians dominate. Mood **2**: tender and troubled love songs exceed playful moments. Theatrical **3**: expressive vocal peaks, especially *La costruzione*, with a more intimate band format than grand orchestra. Experimental **3**: Fossati describes replacing normal session bookings with a fixed creative ensemble, jointly building arrangements and experimenting, plus distinct unusual plucked instruments. Polish **3**: professional studio sound without evidence of unusually glossy processing. Groove **2**: some danceable group-led songs, not dance-floor production.

**Other fields.** `voice=powerful` reflects OndaRock's description of the dramatic album interpretation. Themes love, heartbreak and intimacy are explicit in Fossati's songs and review. `language=it`. No sonic comparisons, producer-named artistic influences, or musical quotations that qualify for either linkage column were documented. `landmark=1` because independent OndaRock explicitly calls this a *svolta*, a lasting change in the singer's approach, and Fossati calls the album a career-important band experiment. **Confidence medium** because producer roles and the full genre tail remain contentious.

## `miamartini-mimi` — *Mimì* (1981; 10 tracks)

**Identity.** DDD ZPLDR 34139 original release, 1981; some digital releases are later remasters. Album rather than 1982 Sanremo single *E non finisce mica il cielo*, which is **not** on its original ten-song tracklist.

**Genres.** `pop` from Apple Music's original 1981 album. `songwriter` because Mia substantially wrote the LP herself: OndaRock, Radio Margherita and original credits consistently describe this change in authorship. `musicaleggera` is supported by the original LP's Italian-pop classification and retrospective accounts of her melodic-song return. `ballad` applies only to the slower, lyrically intimate songs and remains a **medium-strength stylistic designation**, not a documented whole-album sound. The corpus repeatedly mentions **new wave**, but the specific album-level attribution could not be confirmed from an independent open original source (Wikipedia alone is insufficient), so `newwave` is **not** entered. Claude should revisit the last genre if an explicit 1981 press review is located.

**Instruments and machines.** Original DDD liner-transcription credits Joel Di Bartolo (bass), Paul Leim and Ralph Humphrey (drums and percussion), Mitch Holder (guitars), Dick Halligan (keyboards, **Oberheim**, flute, **vocoder** on *Ancora grande*), Garey Milke (Oberheim), Tom Peterson (sax), Vera Halligan string section, and Mia herself (flute, backing vocals). That supports `synth` (Oberheim instruments) and `vocoder` as an instrument, and exact production tokens `oberheim|vocoder`. **No precise Oberheim model is named**; do not substitute OB-X, OB-Xa, OB-8 etc. Do not infer sequencers or drum machines from the synthesizer credit. Background vocal credits are not automatically `choirvocals`.

**Producer:** Dick Halligan, unequivocally credited. Arrangers/engineers and album's DDD label are not producer names. Her vocal type `raspy` is supported by coverage of changes to her singing voice on returning with this very album; it is not an extrapolation from later live recordings.

**Eight scales.** Energy **3**, tempo **3**: mostly middle-energy self-penned pop, slower passages and some propelled choruses. Electronic **2**: Oberheim synthesizers recurrent across the tracks but with a conventional rhythm section and string ensemble. Mood **2**: reflective, wounded yet capable of warm songs. Theatrical **3**: emotional singing but not operatic or huge orchestration. Experimental **2**: vocoder on one song and increased creative self-direction inside conventional pop writing. Polish **3**: several studios and polished arranged session performance, no documented extreme processing. Groove **2**: a few rhythmic songs, not club music.

**Themes/language.** Introspection, personal identity and love/heartbreak are well documented through self-penned songs; other narrative/social topics appear on selected songs. All Italian. The original album did not establish a new independent genre or receive clear independent *artist-wide defining milestone* commentary beyond being her first songwriter-led LP: `landmark=0` for conservative scoring. No song covers become influences; both linkage fields remain blank. **Confidence high** for original personnel and production; ballad tail is a review item.

## `miamartini-quantevoltehocontatolestelle` — *Quante volte... ho contato le stelle* (1982; 9 tracks)

**Genres.** `pop` per Apple Music original album; `musicaleggera` explicitly used of this **1982 album** by Mauro Decandia; `songwriter` follows the central autobiographical compositions and covers other contemporary cantautori (Calabrese and OndaRock); `ballad` is a **weaker track-level stylistic classification** based on the slower emotionally expansive repertoire, not an explicit source's whole-album genre. The album's lead single *Quante volte* is described in some retrospective online accounts as *soft-funk*, but the immediately discoverable text closely resembles unsourced Wikipedia history. **No `funk`, `softfunk`, `newwave` or `progressivepop` token is inserted on that basis alone.** A stronger original 1982 press/musicological account could justify an additional genre. User requirement to avoid inventing values takes precedence over genre variety.

**Instrument provenance.** Hit Parade Italia reproduces names from original DDD credits: Chris Whitten drums, Phil Cranham bass, Maurizio Preti percussion, Giorgio Cocilovo guitar, Karl Wallinger keyboards, and Bruno Filippini harmonica on *Vecchio sole di pietra*. Thus harmonica is last. Other internet summaries list more synthesizer/vocoder players for the LP, but they were not independently authenticated against an open original pressing; **leave `synth` and production machines blank** rather than guess. No distinct production process from Shapiro or engineers could be verified, so `production` is deliberately empty.

**Producer:** Shel Shapiro (original DDD credit, corroborated by two different independent reviews). Writer credits include Mogol, Gianni Bella, Ivano Fossati, Riccardo Cocciante and Mimmo Cavallo, but these are **composers**, not separately credited producers or automatic influences. The lyrics *Stelle* about isolation after concerts and lyrics of *Quante volte* support introspection, identity, heartbreak and social commentary. `voice=powerful` is tied to an album-specific retrospective assessment of her emotive delivery, not the singer's reputation alone.

**Eight scales.** Energy **3** (varied pop song energy); electronic **1** (electric group plus keyboards, no verified extensive synth rig); mood **2** (loneliness and uncertainty balanced by uplift); theatrical **3** (expansive but not staged); experimental **2** (modern arranging and some nonstandard word rhythms); polish **4** (independent critic emphasizes unusually forward production and modern sound); tempo **2** (many measured melodic songs); groove **2** (some driven pop numbers, without album-wide dance focus).

**Landmark 0:** Reviews call the album strong or underrated but do not explicitly show genre-defining or lasting career-changing impact. No sounds-like comparisons or artist/producer-documented album influences can be safely encoded. **Confidence high for personnel/production/year, medium for final genre and subjective scales; row confidence medium may be more cautious if Claude agrees.**

## `miamartini-martinimia` — *Martini Mia...* (1989; 10 tracks)

**Identity/issue.** Original Fonit Cetra TLPX 219, 1989; do not confuse the LP with a reissue, the 1989 Sanremo single, or 1990 *La mia razza*. The label credit on the first pressing names **Promovideo** as producer and **Gianni Sanjust** only as executive producer. Because the owner explicitly excludes executive producers, `producers=Promovideo` and Sanjust is omitted. Maurizio Fabrizio and Renato Serio are credited for different groups of arrangements; arranger is not automatically producer. The original LP has original tracks and a **new** recording of *Agapimu* from 1974.

**Genres.** `pop` from Apple Music; `ballad` is explicitly how contemporary media describe *Almeno tu nell'universo*, with retrospective reviews focusing on *Notturno*. `poprock` reflects Qobuz label's Pop/Rock album categorization; *rock* alone would be misleading. `neapolitansong` is **partial**, because Gragnaniello contributed three modern Naples-rooted songs: *Statte vicino a me* includes a small Neapolitan refrain but is mostly Italian; don't pretend the album is traditional Neapolitan folk. `songwriter` on the album is partial, not an assertion every song is self-penned: Gragnaniello, Bruno Lauzi, Maurizio Fabrizio and some Mia Martini writing are credited. `musicaleggera` describes the Italian song-centered context in contemporary La Repubblica accounts. Avoid extra `jazz`, `blues` and `gospel`: a 1990 industry article says these occur **in her repertoire generally**, not that this particular LP has those styles.

**Instruments and production.** Gragnaniello is explicitly credited for `guitar` on the Fonit original LP as indexed by musicbase, although the index page blocks some automated opens. No independently verified original sleeve instrument listing for rhythm section, piano, strings or specific synthesizers was available. **Only guitar is entered**; blank `production` is correct because arranger credits and a recording studio alone do not prove a string arrangement, mixing trick or studio machine. This is deliberately thinner than earlier albums rather than fabricating instrumentation. Claude can add from a readable Fonit LP scan.

**Language.** Italian is primary; *Agapimu* is rerecorded in Greek, so `it|el` despite being just one track. The few dialect lines of *Statte vicino a me* are not a separate primary sung language. The Greek song was originally on *È proprio come vivere* (1974), but that is a **self-re-recording**, not a `sounds_like` artist or musical influence.

**Eight scales.** Energy **2**, tempo **2**, groove **1**: slow-to-mid-tempo vocal-led material and soft reflective passages dominate over the livelier social *Donna*. Electronic **empty**: instrument/production evidence insufficient. Mood **2**: darker and vulnerable songs plus some playful self-written tracks. Theatrical **4**: contemporary 1989 reviewers explicitly emphasize the power and emotional intensity of *Almeno tu*; arranged songs are dramatic without being operatic. Experimental **1**: traditional accessible songs rather than new production methods. Polish **3**: a professional studio record arranged by Fabrizio/Serio; no verified digital machinery or wall-of-sound layering.

**Social/lyrical evidence.** *Donna* treats violence and marginalization of women; love and heartbreak recur across *Almeno tu*, *Notturno* and *Statte vicino a me*. Voice `powerful` explicitly corroborated by contemporary La Repubblica critic Gino Castaldo. `landmark=1`: an **independent** contemporary account and retrospective OndaRock identify the album as the major career relaunch after extended disappearance, anchored by its Sanremo performance, and Hit Parade Italia independently documents the album's substantial 1989 chart run. This is a career-changing watershed rather than merely a critics' favourite.

**Influences and comparisons.** The 1989 album's songwriter contributors are **not** influences. A critic praising expressive resemblance or a musicologist describing the Neapolitan tradition is not enough to assign a specific `sounds_like` artist. Both linkage columns remain empty. A guest guitar credit is not a musical influence. **Confidence medium** because equipment and ensemble credits are sparse.
