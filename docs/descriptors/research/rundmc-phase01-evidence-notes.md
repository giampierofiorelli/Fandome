# Run-D.M.C. — phase 01: evidence notes

Sources are cross-indexed in `rundmc_phase01_sources.csv` and `rundmc_phase01_genre_evidence.csv`. All eight numerical scores are editorial, 0–5, and require second-person review. `high` confidence refers to source support for the factual fields, not experimental listening measurements.

## Run-D.M.C. (1984) — `rundmc-rundmc`

- **Genre boundary:** The core is early New York hardcore/old-school hip-hop. `raprock` is anchored in “Rock Box”, not the other eight tracks; `electro` is tied especially to “Hard Times”. Avoid treating the record as a rock album or an album of disco-funk simply because it rejected early commercial rap arrangements.
- **Instruments:** In a firsthand Okayplayer interview, D.M.C. expressly identifies an Oberheim DMX drum machine, live bass, guitars, keyboards, synthesizer, tambourines and cowbells as the sound sources for the debut. Apple Music, Albumism, and Eddie Martinez's track credit establish hard-rock electric guitar and DJ turntables; keyboards/percussion appear in personnel credits. These are record-specific, not inferred from rap generally.
- **Production:** In his Brian Coleman interview, original producer Larry Smith says the Oberheim DMX and Prophet-5 were the foundation of the *whole first album*, hence `oberheimdmx|prophet5`. Do not confuse the DMX with the Roland TR-808. **No `sampling` asserted:** scratching records is documented, but an album-wide sampler technique is not shown as clearly as on the later records.
- **Voices and themes:** Run/D.M.C.'s shouted call-and-response rhymes yield `rap|powerful`; `social` is grounded in “It's Like That”/“Hard Times”; `identity` in self-assertion; `party` in performances and DJ celebrations. `spoken` not added for skits on later reissues.
- **Influences versus comparisons:** Producer Larry Smith explicitly modeled the musical approach of “It's Like That” on Afrika Bambaataa & Soulsonic Force's “Planet Rock”. D.M.C. names Kurtis Blow and Grandmaster Flash as formative hip-hop models; Pitchfork's Melle Mel/The Message observation belongs in `sounds_like`. Because “Planet Rock” debuted as a single, no later compilation title is invented as the original studio-album reference.
- **Personnel ambiguity:** Wikipedia's secondary personnel block calls Rod Hui a producer, but MusicBrainz and original-label credits mark him assistant producer. He is excluded from `producers` by the task's rule.

## King of Rock (1985) — `rundmc-kingofrock`

- **Genre boundary:** On repeated songs AllMusic, Apple Music, and Pitchfork describe heavy-guitar rap-rock. Hard-rock textures apply to the electric-guitar tracks. Yellowman's guest appearance on **one** song, “Roots, Rap, Reggae”, supports tail genres `dancehall|reggae` only, rather than reclassifying the whole album.
- **Instruments:** AllMusic lists Eddie Martinez/Davy D/Larry Smith on guitar, Jam Master Jay on keyboards and percussion; Apple documents Larry Smith's synth on “Darryl and Joe”; Albumism and contemporary reviews detail drum-machine beats and Jay's scratching. `electricguitar` is supported by descriptions of distorted metal-style electric riffs. No speculative bass/sampler model added.
- **Production:** Albumism singles out drum programming on “It's Not Funny” and manipulated/cut vocal fragments, justifying `drumprogramming|sampling`. Historical album writeups connect Orange Krush's Oberheim DMX to the sessions; `oberheimdmx` is included, not a guessed TR-808.
- **Lyrics and voice:** Boastful choruses/identity; “You're Blind” is socially critical; “You Talk Too Much” is ironic; Yellowman guest is neither an influence nor a producer.
- **`sounds_like`:** Pitchfork compares “King of Rock” with AC/DC and “Can You Rock It Like This” with Duran Duran; Apple's review explicitly hears Run the Jewels in “Darryl and Joe”, which is a retrospective resemblance, **not** an impossible 1985 influence.
- **`influenced_by` empty:** There is no sufficiently explicit *album-specific* statement by the artists/producers attributing this album's style to a particular recording. The group's formative influences appear in CLAUDE_IMPORT, not invented as this album's direct drivers.

## Raising Hell (1986) — `rundmc-raisinghell`

- **Genres:** Its spine is hardcore hip-hop and New York rap, with well-documented rap-rock tracks. A later Albumism retrospective describes “Is It Live” as go-go-influenced: the token `gogo` therefore comes last and is track-specific, not evidence of a full go-go record.
- **Instrumentation:** MusicBrainz and label credits identify Jason Mizell on keyboards/percussion, Rick Rubin on guitar, Joe Perry's guitar on “Walk This Way”, Daniel Shulman's bass, Sam Sever's programmed drums; Library of Congress documents live drums on “Perfection”, vinyl scratching on “Hit It Run”, bells on “Peter Piper”, piano and sax on “You Be Illin”. The various instruments are verified for specific songs, not necessarily sustained throughout the album.
- **Production:** `sampling` is established by “Peter Piper”'s Bob James break and other identifiable reuses; `drumprogramming` by Sam Sever's credited work; `onetake|liverecording` by the one-take live-drum account for “Perfection” in the Library of Congress historical essay. **Not a fully live album**; those two tokens apply to a particular track.
- **Producer distinction:** Original recording producers Rick Rubin and Russell Simmons; Joseph Simmons and Jason Mizell are explicitly credited **co-producers**, included as requested. Sam Sever is a drum programmer, not a credited producer. This differs from unreliable credits that treat every engineer as producer.
- **Influences:** In a Library of Congress interview *about this album*, D.M.C. explicitly names Cold Crush Brothers, Beatles, Rolling Stones, Led Zeppelin, Harry Chapin, and Jim Croce as period musical influences. An Albumism account attributes “Perfection”'s sparse approach to Doug E. Fresh/Slick Rick's “La Di Da Di” via D.M.C.'s recollection. These are artist-acknowledged, unlike mere sonic comparisons. D.M.C. also retrospectively lists Public Enemy in the same response, but no pre-1986 chronological support was established for that claim; **Public Enemy is intentionally excluded from the 1986 row** and is documented for 1988 instead.
- **`sounds_like`:** Library of Congress explicitly compares “You Be Illin” to the Coasters' comedy records. Pitchfork/Library of Congress describe the Knack melodic/riff motif in “It's Tricky” and an unmistakable resemblance, but it is actually a musical quotation; the Knack is placed in `sounds_like` here for perceptible similarity, **not** `influenced_by`. A quotation may be treated as a sample rather than an independent comparison by Claude; remove `The Knack` if applying the no-quotation rule to both columns.
- **Critical disagreement:** Pitchfork's 2005 retrospective criticizes filler and “Walk This Way”, while the Library of Congress and AllMusic stress wide musical reach and major impact. This affects perceived quality, not basic instrument or production facts.

## Tougher Than Leather (1988) — `rundmc-tougherthanleather`

- **Genres:** Hardcore East Coast hip-hop with rap-rock songs. D.M.C. says “Miss Elaine” was an experiment in pop-rock, hence `poprock` at the tail. No `dancehall` from the prior record. The film tie-in does **not** make this studio LP's type `soundtrack`; it is in the core album discography as a studio album.
- **Instruments:** Apple confirms drum-machine beats; Albumism describes Jam Master Jay scratching on “Beats to the Rhyme” and “They Call Us Run-D.M.C.”. MusicBrainz, Qobuz and AllMusic credits show Davy D, Daniel Shulman, Andreas Straub, Jon Sierra, Bobby Walker, and Uptown Horns on bass, guitar, piano, drums, horns; Albumism identifies the saxophone solo on “Ragtime”. Guitar is expressly electric on some rap-rock cuts.
- **Production:** Documentary samples from Soul Searchers, James Brown and the Monkees justify `sampling`. Albumism reports a *greater reliance on sampled beats*, whereas Apple Music emphasizes the group's surviving drum-machine attack. Both can be true across tracks; do not reduce the record to a drum-machine-only or sampler-only album. No machine model like SP-1200 is credited by the sources, so none is assigned.
- **Producers:** MusicBrainz and Qobuz identify Run-D.M.C. plus Davy D for most cuts and Rick Rubin for “Mary, Mary”. Russell Simmons is **executive producer**, excluded. Some listings truncate the credit to Davy D alone, but original track credits retain co-production by the group.
- **Influences:** In AllHipHop's firsthand track-by-track, D.M.C. explicitly says “Run's House” and “Soul to Rock and Roll” were informed by Public Enemy. He also connects the comedic “Ragtime” with Slick Rick and “La Di Da Di” (Doug E. Fresh & Slick Rick). The latter was initially a single; no fabricated original studio-album reference is added. The group’s own earlier songs are not counted as third-party influences.
- **`sounds_like`:** Pitchfork's breakbeat comparison with Marley Marl, D.M.C.'s Mantronix drum comparison, and Albumism's observation of Slick Rick/Dana Dane-like narrative style. Critics disagree: Pitchfork calls the album behind the times, whereas Albumism argues it successfully explores a denser sample-led aesthetic. `landmark=0` reflects its generally lesser role than the first three albums, not a verdict on artistic merit.
