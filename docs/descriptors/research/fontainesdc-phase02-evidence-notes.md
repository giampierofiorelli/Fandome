# Fontaines D.C. — Phase 02 evidence notes

Research cutoff: **26 October 2026**. This phase covers **Romance (2024)** and **Dopamine Chamber (2026)** only. Each row contains exactly 27 columns. Evaluations are editorial and provisional (`draft`); discrete gear and credits are sourced, and source-level uncertainty is described rather than guessed. See `fontainesdc_phase02_sources.csv` for URLs and exact support descriptions.

## Romance (2024) — `fontainesdc-romance`

**Release, lineup, production credit.** Fourth studio album, 23 August 2024, XL Recordings (official releases; AllMusic; Official Charts). Primary and track-by-track MusicBrainz credits consistently identify **James Ford** as the *producer and mixer*. No arranger, instrumentalist, engineer or co-writer is added to `producers` without production credit. Recording credits identify **La Frette, France** for most songs and **The Church, London** for *Motorcycle Boy*; strings were engineered separately at Orbb Studios (MusicBrainz). The 2025 deluxe edition adds tracks and is not a separate original studio album.

**Genre prioritisation.** The backbone is indie/alternative rock with retained post-punk heritage: AllMusic specifically gives Indie Rock, Alternative/Indie Rock and New Wave/Post-Punk Revival. Pitchfork expressly cites Britpop, gothic Americana and 1990s alternative rock; AOTY additionally calls it Post-Britpop, Gothic Rock, Dream Pop and Jangle Pop, but those latter subgenre tags are less authoritative and ranked below the core. The Guardian points to shoegaze-y guitars on *Sundowner* and a lighter nu-metal tinge on *Here's the Thing*. `numetal` is a **new genre token**; the band and critics describe its musical touches rather than claiming a full nu-metal album. `boombap` belongs to the rhythmic break on *Starburster* only, supported by Pitchfork; `electronica` is textural (Vogue). `janglepop` is likewise a **new genre token**. All album-wide vs track-only scopes are explicit in the genre evidence CSV. No cinematic or film inspirations are added as genres.

**Eight scores (album-wide editorial judgements, not measured BPM).** `energy=3`: fast, urgent *Starburster* and *Here's the Thing* balance slower *In the Modern World*, *Motorcycle Boy* and *Horseness Is the Whatness*. `electronic=3`: Mellotrons, synths, guitars processed with fuzz/chorus and electronic beats become prominent but a live guitar/bass/drum core remains. `mood=2`: romance and fleeting uplift surrounded by alienation, numbness and apprehension. `theatrical=4`: dramatic contrasts, swelling string arrangements and staged vocal personas. `experimental=3`: unusual hybrid combinations and breaths used rhythmically within standard songs, not a fully avant-garde record. `polish=4`: Ford's richly layered and carefully arranged studio sound. `tempo=3`: driving, mid-tempo and slow tracks. `groove=3`: *Starburster* and *Here's the Thing* introduce danceable beats without a consistently dance-led album. Primary listening descriptions: Pitchfork, Guardian, Guitar.com, Crack and AllMusic.

**Vocals, language, words.** The album retains Chatten's rough-edged declamation and soft croon; Pitchfork documents expanded upper register and falsetto on *Here's the Thing* and breathy vocal dynamics. `falsetto` is a *production/vocal-use tag*, not a new lead-voice class; `layeredvocals` corresponds to the harmonies noted on *Sundowner* and track-level backing voice credits, without claiming a specific harmonizer. English throughout. The Guardian and Pitchfork document desire, love, anxiety, emotional numbness, identity and nostalgia, while *Starburster* specifically uses a panic attack experienced by Chatten. It is not reduced to romance alone.

**Instruments and production.** MusicBrainz's release-level track credits substantiate **guitars, bass, drums, synths (including Yamaha Reface CP), Mellotron, piano, keyboards, organ, percussion and string quintet personnel** (Emma Smith, Jennymay Logan, Richard Jones, Laura Moody, Marriane Schofield). Guitar.com directly documents Conor Curley's Jazzmaster guitar, Keeley Loomer fuzz and Boss Chorus on *Here's the Thing*. We therefore tag `distortion`, but refrain from inventing a specific Boss Chorus model. Crack quotes Deegan on **layering multiple bass performances**, supporting `overdubbing`; its accounts of the quartet and MusicBrainz credits support `stringarrangements`. Pitchfork reports an actual fetal heartbeat recording incorporated into *Horseness Is the Whatness*, supporting `sampling`, but not `fieldrecordings` (the heartbeat was not documented as captured in the field). Production model `minimoog` is explicitly listed in MusicBrainz on several tracks; the **Yamaha Reface CP** is credited, retained as new model token `yamaharefacecp`; this is a product model label rather than a claim that it uses analog synthesis. MusicBrainz marks **ring modulation** on *Bug*, so `ringmodulation` is a newly proposed production-effect token. James Ford is a credited producer; additional string players/arrangers are not. `programming` is supported by track-level production credits and liner notes. We do not tag drum machines simply because Tom Coll said he tried to sound *like* a drum machine; the recorded instrument is not automatically a drum machine.

**Verified musical influences vs resemblance.** *Romance*-period statements explicitly name **Korn** (Grian / Carlos), **Deftones** and **Alice in Chains** (Guardian interview), **Outkast** (Conor Deegan in Soundofbrit), **Lana Del Rey** (Chatten on arranging *In the Modern World*, Vogue), **True Widow**, **Smashing Pumpkins**, **Nirvana** (bass-layering technique), **Shygirl** and **Sega Bodega** (Crack). These are album-relevant and therefore in `influenced_by`. **The Cure, Felt, Pixies, The Verve, Blur** are used as audible comparisons (`sounds_like`) when reviewers identify similarities; they are not automatically promoted to historical influence. **Film, literary and manga inspirations are excluded** from musical `influenced_by`.

**Reception disagreements.** Critics broadly applaud the expanded melodic craft but differ over how far it remains post-punk. Pitchfork emphasises 1990s alt rock and disorientation; the Guardian calls it more accessible without losing grit. AOTY's fine-grained genre labels are medium evidence, not evidence that each style dominates. `landmark=1`: clear career expansion and widely acclaimed album; `confidence=high` because release credits, multiple interviews and numerous independent reviews converge on the main sonic direction.

## Dopamine Chamber (2026) — `fontainesdc-dopaminechamber`

**Release, editions, billing.** Fifth studio album issued **16 October 2026** via XL Recordings; 11 tracks in the original edition (official discography; label; MusicBrainz; Official Charts). The official site has at times retained *pre-order* wording after release, but original release date and actual digital release are independently confirmed. One song, *Where Is Gone?*, is credited **Fontaines D.C. with ROSALÍA** on MusicBrainz. **Do not add a co-billed ALBUM** in `coBilled.ts`: Rosalía is a guest on a track, not an album-level co-artist. The vocal parts on that track are **in English**, so album `language=en`, not `en|es` (NME lyrics / independent transcript); national origin of a guest is not evidence of a language.

**Genre and scope.** The record combines **alternative/indie rock** (release metadata / Pitchfork) and more extensive electronics, with **synth-pop** on *Mimesis* (NME), **goth pop** (RTÉ), **trip-hop** beats on *Tongue* (NME), **electroclash** on *Mimesis* (RTÉ), **electronica** on *Demolition* (NME), **dream pop** on *Japan* (Pitchfork), and **grunge** on *Demolition* (Pitchfork). `industrial` marks the specific percussion/texture described by RTÉ rather than an assertion that the whole record is an industrial album. Orchestral balladry is important but not lazily classified as a soundtrack. Most genre labels here are track-scoped because the record changes sonic character from track to track.

**Eight scores, editorial.** `energy=3`: forceful opening synth rhythms, *Demolition* and climax contrasts with several long subdued ballads. `electronic=3`: guitars are absent on some songs, while synthesizers and electronic production are foregrounded, but a full live rhythm section and substantial *played* strings coexist (RTÉ launch press statement and MusicBrainz). `mood=1`: persistent societal dread, numbness, desire and fractured intimacy with occasional beauty. `theatrical=4`: cinematic crescendos, dramatic string writing and changing vocal roles. `experimental=3`: unusually synthetic soundscapes for the band but recognisable hooks and rock/pop forms. `polish=4`: detailed, multi-layered 2026 studio production. `tempo=2`: abundant deliberate mid-to-slow pacing, with some more urgent tracks. `groove=2`: *Mimesis* and *Tongue* groove, but most tracks are not dancefloor-directed. Compare NME / Pitchfork / Guardian / RTÉ with the dissenting Financial Times assessment.

**Sourced instruments, techniques, machines.** MusicBrainz offers track-level players and instrumental detail: electric guitar, bass, live drums, synths, piano, acoustic guitar, cello, viola, violin, double bass, orchestral strings, tambourine; aggregated metadata further lists a drum machine, synth bass and Mellotron (Tracknack, **medium** because source collapses release and track detail). The label's press statement states samples and triggers in the finished songs, supporting `sampling`. MusicBrainz explicitly credits **programming** on multiple tracks (including Tom Coll and Carlos O'Connell on the opener), but this does **not** imply any named drum-machine make, `tr808`, `moog`, `ableton`, or autotune. Named models are thus left out. Grian Chatten's **makeshift vocal booth in a Palermo Airbnb for One Man**, with ambient sound of children playing outside the cathedral heard at the song's ending, is documented first-hand in Vogue. That specific ambient capture supports cautious `fieldrecordings`; we do not assert an exact microphone, DAW, hardware module, or recording medium. Track-level string arrangement credits identify Grian Chatten, Elysian Collective, Richard Jones and Laura Moody, giving `stringarrangements`. RTÉ describes distorted electronics, supporting `distortion` as a *texture*; not necessarily digitally clipped vocals.

**Crucial: Mina is a DIRECT DOCUMENTED musical influence.** In Ann Powers' **NPR interview published 12 October 2026**, Grian Chatten identifies the Italian singer **Mina**, describing her music as informing the humanity and emotional feeling he intentionally brought to **this album**. This is a primary, album-specific, unmistakably musical statement, so `Mina` goes in **`influenced_by`**, and is also a legitimate (editorial, localised) human/orchestral point of comparison in `sounds_like` without treating the entire synthetic album as sonically identical to Mina. In the same answer, he contrasts Mina with **John Maus**, **Depeche Mode**, **New Order** and The Cure's *Disintegration*, describing the darker, industrial, dystopian electronic spectrum shaping the album. Those artists are also documented `influenced_by`. The NPR transcript on one affiliate renders the name John Maus phonetically as 'John Mouse'; the intended artist is clear from another affiliate transcript and context. **No Mina sample, cover, duet, production credit or actual guest recording is claimed.** The 1960s Italian-pop *strings* in the label press statement provide additional independent context for Mina's influence, but the influence is established by Chatten's own testimony, not by speculative resemblance.

**Themes, voices, guest and language.** The Vogue and NPR interviews explicitly establish digital distraction, AI-era anxiety, ecological collapse, political violence, false or corrupted pleasure, loss of authenticity and intimate relationships. English `en` only, including Rosalía's *Where Is Gone?*. Chatten's nasal/rough spoken chant, quieter baritone and more forceful melody justify `raspy|soft|powerful|spoken`. Neither Rosalía's guest performance nor her nationality turns the band into a Spanish-language album. `sounds_like` (Depeche Mode, New Order, Cure, Massive Attack, Nick Cave etc.) represents qualified critical comparisons, not additional undocumented album influences.

**Producer credits discrepancy.** XL's official press and MusicBrainz's **11 track-level release credits** identify **James Ford** as producer of the actual master; hence `producers=James Ford`. The aggregator Tracknack additionally lists **Marta Salogni**, **Carlos O'Connell** and **Tom Coll** as 'Producer' at album level, but MusicBrainz identifies Marta on engineering and synth, and Carlos/Tom on programming or instruments, *not as produced-by on original track credits*. Do **not** turn engineer, programmer, or musician credits into co-producer credits until an authoritative album liner-note correction substantiates them. This conflict is explicitly flagged for Claude/human review; it is not silently merged.

**Critical disagreement / confidence.** NME, The Guardian, Rolling Stone UK and RTÉ praise the cinematic synth expansion; **Financial Times gives a sharply negative review** for ponderous tempos and repetitive gloom, while Pitchfork scores it **7.8** with more reserved praise. We do not infer a consensus from one enthusiastic review; `confidence=medium` for a ten-day-old album, despite high confidence in release dates, official James Ford credit and Mina's explicit influence. `landmark=1` is a provisional judgement: major fifth LP with a distinct, documented stylistic pivot, not a claim of established long-term critical canon.

**Exclusion tests.** No live albums, greatest-hits records, deluxe reissues, standalone singles, EP padding, and no duplicate Phase 01 rows. *Mina* is a documented Italian singer influence, not a Fontaines D.C. member; *Rosalía* is a featured track vocalist, not an album producer or formal co-billed album artist. `notes` for both rows contain <=15 words.
