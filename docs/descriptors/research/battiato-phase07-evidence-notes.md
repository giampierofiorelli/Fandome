# Franco Battiato — Phase 07: evidence notes (26 October 2026)

**Status:** editorial draft; not independently audio-measured. Sources reflect original releases wherever possible. The scales are comparative Fandome editor judgements from album-specific descriptions, not measurements. Empty cells mean insufficient evidence. Genre tokens are ordered by importance, but track-specific style labels remain towards the end. `sounds_like` is editorial resemblance only; it never establishes direct influence.

## `battiato-fleurs` — *Fleurs* (1999)

- **Edition/type/landmark:** Original Mercury/Universal issue dated 22 October 1999 (MusicBrainz, Rockol). Studio-made repertoire interpretations, 10 covers and two new songs, not a compilation of original recordings. Battiato won the subsequent Tenco interpreter award (official Universal). `landmark=1` describes its major role in the artist's later songbook identity, not an innovation claim. AllMusic's top-of-page 2001 date refers to a later distribution; its release list includes the 1999 issue.
- **Genres:** AllMusic explicitly lists *Classical Pop*, *Neo-Classical*, *Easy Listening* and *Euro-Pop*, while Qobuz categorizes the original as pop. OndaRock's album-specific analysis describes a chamber-style collection of songs by Italian singer-songwriters and French chansonniers. `chanson` is limited to Brel/Aznavour/Trenet material and `canzonedautore` to the Italian singer-songwriter tradition. Styles apply to different parts of a repertory album; they do not imply it was a French original. No unverified 'ambient'/'synthpop' just because Battiato made those genres earlier.
- **Instruments:** Original album-specific AllMusic credits list Michele Fedrigotti (piano and keyboards), Alessandro Simoncini (violin), Demetrio Comuzzi (viola), Luca Simoncini (cello), Angelo Privitera (keyboards). Thus `piano|strings|keyboards`, not guitars, synthesizers, and drums inferred from his older studio albums. The piano-and-quartet structure is independently confirmed by OndaRock.
- **Production/producers:** Battiato sole **Producer** in Qobuz original label credits and AllMusic; Fedrigotti's arrangement credits are not a producer credit. `stringarrangements` reflects named chamber arrangements; no source warrants claiming a particular DAW, drum machine or synth model.
- **Voice/lyrics/languages:** Soft, clear lead voice and strongly intimate delivery; original repertoire emphasizes lost love, distance and nostalgia, alongside the spiritual or inward-looking `Invito al viaggio`. Qobuz/MusicBrainz document Italian, French (`J’entends siffler le train`) and English (`Ruby Tuesday`). `Era de maggio` uses Neapolitan and is included as `nap`, not ordinary Italian.
- **Scales:** energy 1 and groove 0: mostly ballad-like chamber settings, not driven by rhythms; electronic 1 from mostly acoustic violin/cello/piano with minor keyboards; mood 1 on Rockol's nostalgia/melancholy; theatrical 2 for restrained interpretation; experimental 2 for cross-repertoire chamber reinterpretation rather than avant-garde; polish 4 for studied studio arrangements; tempo 1 for mostly slow songs. **These are qualitative interpretations** of Rockol, OndaRock and credits, not sourced BPMs.
- **Influenced_by versus cover repertoire:** Battiato's original selection is described by Rockol as music from his youth with deep personal importance. De André, Endrigo, Brel, Rolling Stones are documented repertoire and can be treated as *material shaping this album's choices*, **not** as proof each artist influenced Battiato's entire career. If Claude's standard requires an explicit artist-quoted 'X influenced me', move all these names to a covered-artists field rather than asserting stronger biography. The name set is deliberately smaller than the complete composer list. `sounds_like` is editorial sonic comparison, not sourced influence.
- **Disagreements:** AllMusic's reissue header shows 2001 vs confirmed 1999 first issue; AllMusic lists Euro-Pop but OndaRock strongly stresses classical chamber treatment. Both tags are preserved with a genre-evidence scope and weight distinction.

## `battiato-campimagnetici` — *Campi magnetici* (2000)

- **Identity/type:** Sony Classical CD for Paco Decina's ballet, premiered at the Maggio Musicale Fiorentino in June 2000. Official artist page calls it explicitly music for ballet; therefore `soundtrack` as type (not `studio`), with actual genres present. Apple Classical dates the original album 9 June 2000 and names eight tracks (seven movements plus `La mer`). This is NOT the 2002 pop album.
- **Genres:** `experimentalelectronic|electronic|experimental|avantgarde` describes the central experimental electronic score according to OndaRock, SUONO and a contemporary Battiato interview. Rolling Stone identifies **occasional** almost-techno beat and near-industrial chaos; `techno` = some tracks and `industrial` = texture; `ambient` = long suspended sections described by the same critic, not a dance-oriented overall claim. `classical` was **not** added merely because Sony Classical released it. SUONO invokes trance in a descriptive way; no unsupported entire-album `trance` tag.
- **Instruments and production:** Original release credit transcription identifies Fedrigotti piano/keyboards and Dell'Ira trumpet; OndaRock and SUONO explicitly describe sampling, rhythmic electronics, rapid sound transformations, and spoken-word passages. Pischetola has a named 'Rhythmic' credit; `drumprogramming` is cautious attribution supported by such context and electronic pulses (review original booklet if strict hardware credit needed). We **do not** list a physical sampler instrument, an uncredited synth model, or an invented DAW. `sampling|sonicmanipulation` summarises source-confirmed sampled collage and computerized transformations. The original booklet 'Rhythmic' credit does not prove literal drum programming, so that token is omitted.
- **Vocals/language/themes:** Manlio Sgalambro recites; Simone Bartolini is credited as male soprano. Thus `spoken|operatic` describes episodic voice, not a conventional Battiato lead-vocal album. Narrated science/philosophy (Lucretius, Einstein) is documented by the official page; `lyrics` left empty because these are heterogeneous textual recitations and not coherent song lyrics. `it|fr|en` comes from the source booklet/song titles, `La mer` spoken French plus English passage title; **English recitation is not independently confirmed**; remove `en` pending lyric evidence. **Final row set to Italian and French only**.
- **Scales:** electronic 5, experimental 5, theatrical 4 reflect electronics, aggressive contrasts, narrated/dramatic movement changes. Energy 3 and mood 1 balance explosive/abrasive episodes against expansive quiet sections. Polish 4 reflects deliberate digital composition and production. Groove 1 because occasional pulse does not make the full work danceable. `tempo` blank because extremely different movement speeds do not yield a defensible typical speed. Note that ballet music is not necessarily dancefloor music.
- **Credits/influence:** Apple lists **Battiato and Saro Cosentino** in production; release transcription specifies 'additional' for Cosentino (retain as co-producer/additional producer, not engineer only). Pischetola is recording/mixing, not automatically producer. Michael Nyman and Philip Glass appear as **comparisons in SUONO**, entered solely in `sounds_like`, NEVER `influenced_by`.
- **Disagreements:** Official credits describe textual excerpts while critics sometimes refer to science spoken-word pieces as lyrics; scopes differ. Genre labels vary dramatically across critics; primary 'electronic avant-garde' remains stable.

## `battiato-ferrobattuto` — *Ferro battuto* (2001)

- **Type/producers:** Original Columbia/Sony studio album, 2001. Original sleeve transcription and AllMusic credit **Franco Battiato and Saro Cosentino as Producers**; Francesco Cattini is executive producer (excluded). Original release quotes Jim Kerr guest singing, Natacha Atlas guests, London strings. No co-billed album.
- **Genres:** A real conflict of emphasis. Original release metadata says electronic/rock/pop, art rock/pop rock; the 26 April 2001 Rockol critic disputes the idea of straightforward pop, noting world-flavoured `Hey Joe`, jazz-sampled `Scherzo in minore`, and cross-style composition. Official artist page says sampled dance grooves. Genre list therefore holds poprock, artrock, rock, pop, electronic across the album and dance, jazz, worldmusic only late in the list, track-local. `worldmusic` here is a critic's description of a cover, not proof of an authentic ethnic tradition or entire album.
- **Instruments:** On original 2001 CD, Gussoni, Rhodes and Cosentino guitars; Paolo Costa electric bass; Lele Melotti drums; Michele Fedrigotti piano and orchestra director; Battiato keyboards and guitar. Credited orchestra/strings and violin Gavin Wright justify `strings`, but the full ensemble isn't to be inferred as every track. `electricguitar` is mapped from the credited electric guitar sound; if Claude requires credit to literally specify electric vs acoustic, convert token to `guitar` (present in repository) until booklet checks.
- **Production:** Official artist notes say sampled dance textures; period critic specifically identifies sampled jazz. Original press credits Pino 'Pinaxa' Pischetola with rhythm programming; Versari digital editing. `stringarrangements` maps explicitly credited orchestration by Fedrigotti. We do not assert Moog or any specific drum machine.
- **Voice/lyrics/language:** Mostly clear lead with introspection and other guests, and some spoken inserts. German `Bist du bei mir`, French `Personalità empirica`, English `Running against the grain`, Sicilian `Il cammino interminabile`, and principally Italian elsewhere are sourced on the official page. `Hey Joe` and Sgalambro material require separating covers from original songwriter themes.
- **Scales:** medium energy 3, electronic 3 and tempo 3 are inferences from mixed electronic pulses, acoustic band/strings, mid-tempo pop and slower tracks; mood 2 introspective; theatrical 3 and experimental 3 for cross-genre arrangements; polish 4 for expensive international studio personnel and electronic post-production; groove 2 for dance beats only in parts, not a club album.
- **Documented influences:** Original artist site explicitly calls `Hey Joe` an homage to Jimi Hendrix; OndaRock also confirms. `Jimi Hendrix` qualifies as work-specific influence, though the composition is credited to Billy Roberts, and Battiato changes the harmonic setting. Tchaikovsky is **quoted** in `Personalità empirica` per Rockol, but we exclude artist-wide influence unless the sample/quotation is verified in official credits. Peter Gabriel and David Sylvian comparisons remain subjective `sounds_like` only.
- **Critical split:** The Rockol review regards the many ideas as unfocused. Official artist biography promotes genre experimentation. Our matrix conveys the observable mixture without endorsing either evaluative verdict.

## `battiato-fleurs3` — *Fleurs 3* (2002)

- **Release/type:** Second installment of cover sequence, originally Columbia/Sony 2002, not the third chronological volume. Original MusicBrainz Italian CD says 20 Aug 2002; official Battiato artist biography states 30 Aug 2002; an AllMusic October date belongs to an issue/release. `year=2002` is secure, exact day disputed. It is a *newly recorded studio covers album*, not compilation. `Fleurs 2` is a separate later record (2008), NOT part of this phase.
- **Genres:** Official site says songbook homage to Genoese cantautori Bruno Lauzi, Gino Paoli and others, with French chanson (Léo Ferré), Strauss's German art song, PFM etc. The main fabric is Italian pop and its chamber/orchestrated reworkings. OndaRock notes drums/electronics brought into a string-centered model; `classicalpop` is deliberately omitted without an explicit album-specific classification, while `lieder` is one track (`Beim Schlafengehen`), not entire album. No conventional `rock` just because PFM's song is covered.
- **Instruments:** Album-specific AllMusic credits: Guaitoli piano, Simoncini violin, Comuzzi viola, Simoncini cello, Battiato *chitarrone/guitar* and keyboards, Paolo Valli drums, Mauro Parodi trombone. `strings|piano|keyboards|drums|guitar|trombone`. We avoid specifying 'electric' for guitar, because AllMusic's literal credited string instrument is unspecific. `stringarrangements` from Guaitoli/Fedrigotti, `drumprogramming` from Pischetola's named Rhythm Programming. Battiato `Franco Battiato` producer in Sony metadata; note Wikipedia lists Francesco Cattini as producer without corresponding original-song credit; **do not silently adopt that claim**. Original physical booklet review could further settle if he was only executive producer.
- **Voice/language/themes:** Battiato leads, Alice co-vocal on original `Come un sigillo`, a soprano appears on Strauss/Hesse `Beim Schlafengehen` (Rockol). Main Italian lyric language, one German art song. Songs of affection, loss, nostalgia and longing predominate, with biblical language in the new duet. Voice token `operatic` is track-local, not overall opera.
- **Scales:** energy 2; electronic 2 rather than *Fleurs*'s 1 because critic documents more electronics and drums; mood 2 nostalgic; theatrical 3 because some grand/vocal classical passages; experimental 1 relatively traditional cover architecture; polish 4 professional studio strings; tempo 2/mid-slow; groove 1 with rhythmic songs, not dance purpose. `confidence=medium` reflects disagreement on arranger/producer and album-level crossover genre categorisation, despite solid credits.
- **Influence/repertoire distinction:** PFM, Lauzi, Paoli and Strauss are names directly represented by covers or performance; they are *the documented material reworked on this disc*, not necessarily biographical inspirations of the artist. If strict Fandome `influenced_by` requires direct testimony instead of demonstrable borrowing, move them out of `influenced_by` during review and rely on `sounds_like`/evidence notes. The period review differentiates Strauss lied from the many classic popular songs, and makes no claim that being covered means shaping the author's historical taste.
- **Critical disagreement:** Rockol's 2002 review finds inventive rearrangements and a strong Alice duet but describes some material as less successful; OndaRock judges the sequel less imaginative/considerably more commercial than 1999. The descriptor registers a pop-leaning, more rhythm-driven production rather than averaging reviewers' evaluations.
