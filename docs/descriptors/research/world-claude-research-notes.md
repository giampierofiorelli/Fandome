# Evidence notes (world music)

## Why these three
- **Buena Vista Social Club (1997, Cuba)**: the strongest case on every measure.
  - Rolling Stone 500 (2003): no. 260; also in 1001 Albums.
  - Grammy 1998; Library of Congress National Recording Registry (2022).
  - Guinness: best-selling world music album (8M+ copies).
  - 5 stars from AllMusic, Uncut and the Encyclopedia of Popular Music.
- **King Sunny Adé – Juju Music (1982, Nigeria)**: chosen for its historical role.
  - The NYT and others credit it (and the tour) with launching and popularising world beat in the US and the West.
  - It was Island's first major push of African music that wasn't reggae.
  - Pitchfork 10, AllMusic 4.5, NME top ten of 1982, and in The Wire's list of 100 records.
  - It bears directly on Talking Heads' Naked, which critics compare to Adé.
  - It is not in 1001 Albums (I checked the unofficial full list).
- **Youssou N'Dour – Immigrés (1984, Senegal)**: the record that put him on the international map.
  - In 1001 Albums; NME no. 18 of 1984.
  - It got Peter Gabriel "hooked on African rhythm".
  - Its tradition (Wolof mbalax) is different from jùjú.
  - Its weak point is critical standing: AllMusic gives 3/5 and Christgau B+.
- **Not chosen**:
  - **Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan**: Mustt Mustt is strong (AllMusic 4.5; Alternative Press top 100 of the 90s), and 1001 Albums lists his Devotional Songs. He is dropped because his discography runs to hundreds of cassettes, which Wikipedia itself says would be "a nightmare" to put in order, so the catalogue's full studio discography could not be built.
  - **Ali Farka Touré – Talking Timbuktu**: in 1001 Albums and won a Grammy, but it is billed jointly with Ry Cooder (who already produced BVSC) and AllMusic gives it only 3/5.
  - **Mory Kanté – Akwaba Beach**: a big hit ("Yé ké yé ké"), but I found no all-time list that includes it.
  - **Youssou N'Dour – Egypt (2004, Grammy)**: an alternative to Immigrés if a later, more acclaimed record is preferred.

## Juju Music
- **Recording**: according to Meissonnier (RBMA), the band recorded live in Lomé, Togo, in two days; synths were added later in London and Godwin Logie mixed it. Hence liverecording|overdubbing. Meissonnier added "heavy echo and reverb", hence heavyreverb. Wikipedia and Linkiesta both mention dub mixing techniques, so `dub` goes last in genres, as a texture.
- **Linn drums**: Wikipedia says Meissonnier introduced synthesizers and Linn drums, but doesn't say which model (LM-1 or LinnDrum). So linndrum is left out. drummachine is not added either, since the credits list live drummers.
- **Genres**:
  - Jùjú and worldbeat come from the Wikipedia infobox.
  - afropop is medium strength: Wikipedia says the album opened doors for "Afropop performers", and lists Adé's genre as "African pop".
  - Ben Ratliff (NYT 2000) compares Adé's music to reggae, salsa, country, calypso, the Grateful Dead and jazz. That was a concert review about his music in general, so none of these is used as an album genre or in sounds_like.
- **Scales**:
  - groove 4: Palmer calls it "compulsively danceable"; the NYT called it "the year's freshest dance-music album".
  - mood 4: "sweet and cool"; "communal ritual and peace".
  - electronic 2: synths are used sparingly and move in and out; the core is acoustic percussion.
  - experimental 3: the dub spaces and long jams were unusual for Western pop, though the record stays accessible.
- **voice**: soft (RBMA: "gentle tenor"; NYT: "gentle singing") | choral (high harmonies; eight credited vocalists).
- **lyrics**: Palmer says the proverbs espouse traditional values; Adé says "all the songs are more or less a proverb"; he links Ja Funmi to God. Hence spiritual|identity|social.
- **sounds_like**: Ebenezer Obey. Wikipedia's jùjú article names him with Adé as a modern jùjú artist. An editorial pick, low to medium strength.

## Immigrés
- **Edition**: first released 1984 on Celluloid (France). The 1988 Earthworks/Virgin international edition was remixed, according to Christgau. The row uses 1984.
- **Genres**:
  - mbalax and World come from the Wikipedia infobox.
  - soukous comes from Christgau's description (low strength, placed last).
  - Trouser Press also hears Cuban and Arabic elements. These are not added, because the source doesn't name them as genres.
- **Instruments**: all from the Wikipedia personnel list.
- **DX7**: Pan African Music says the DX7 is the keyboard mbalax musicians prefer, but not that it was used on this album, so it is left out of production.
- **Production is empty**: the only facts on how it was made are Christgau's "recorded in Paris" and the later remix (an edition detail). Pan African's "analogue warmth" is too vague to support analogtape.
- **Scales**:
  - polish 2: Trouser Press calls it "less polished".
  - groove 4: the title track is made for the dance floor and the mbalax rhythm is danceable, though Pitche Mi is a ballad.
  - electronic 2: AllMusic criticises the keyboards/synths, but the album is mostly live percussion.
- **language wo**: fr.wikipedia classifies him as a Wolof-language singer. No source is specific to this album, so confidence is medium.
- **lyrics**: social, identity and nostalgia for the diaspora and the theme of return (a homesick "letter home"); storytelling for the Pitche Mi fable.

## Buena Vista Social Club
- **Recording**:
  - Eastwood (2016): no headphones or booths, microphone bleed, a Studer reel-to-reel. Hence liverecording|analogtape.
  - NPR (quoting Nick Gold) and Stereophile describe the room's natural reverb. Both pages were blocked and I only saw search snippets, so roomambience is medium strength.
- **electronic 1**: an acoustic ensemble; only Cooder's electric slide guitar adds amplified sound.
- **Genres**:
  - From the Wikipedia infobox and text.
  - `cuban` and `latin` are added as broader tokens, based on the Grammy's "Tropical Latin" category and the Billboard Latin charts.
  - `jazz` goes last (it is in the infobox; descarga is a jam form).
- **lyrics**: Chan Chan tells a rural story (storytelling); Candela is full of innuendo (sensuality); the boleros are love songs.
- **sounds_like**: Afro-Cuban All Stars – A Toda Cuba le Gusta, recorded at the same sessions with largely the same players (Wikipedia; Stereophile via search snippet).
- **influenced_by is empty**: under the rules, covered repertoire (Arcaño, Cachao) is not an influence.

## Access limits
Pitchfork, AllMusic, Discogs, RYM, Stereophile and Britannica returned 403 or were refused, so their scores are cited via Wikipedia. I did not read the Pitchfork review directly.
