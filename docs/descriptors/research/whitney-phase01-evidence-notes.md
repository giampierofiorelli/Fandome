# Whitney Houston — Fandome Phase 01: evidence notes

Research cut-off: 2026-10-25. Draft editorial values; no direct catalog or repository writes. Evidence applies to **original albums**, not retrospective remixes, deluxe extras or live bonuses.

## 1. Whitney Houston (1985) — `whitney-whitneyhouston`

**Genres and arrangement.** Stephen Thomas Erlewine's AllMusic review and styles list explicitly place this album in R&B, contemporary R&B and quiet storm while identifying the very different dance/R&B behavior of *How Will I Know* and *Thinking About You*. Album of the Year separately tags pop soul, dance-pop and adult contemporary (https://www.allmusic.com/album/whitney-houston-mw0000650265; https://www.albumoftheyear.org/album/20685-whitney-houston-whitney-houston.php). Consequently quiet storm and dance-pop are included as substantial song-group styles, not claimed uniformly for all ten tracks. *Pop* is additionally corroborated by the contemporary pop crossover discussion. Avoid overtagging gospel: Whitney's training does not make every studio song gospel.

**Instruments.** The reproduced session credits list Kashif's keyboards, real kit players, bassists including Nathan East and Freddie Washington, session **guitar** players, percussionists, Ernie Watts on saxophone, and backing vocalists. MusicBrainz credits for *How Will I Know* identify Rustici's **guitar synthesizer** and Preston Glass's synthesizer. String-orchestration and string parts occur in Masser ballads; sax and other instruments are not present on every track (https://sessiondays.com/2016/08/09/1985-whitney-houston-whitney-houston/; https://musicbrainz.org/release/4d4f9889-88f0-43db-913c-1f3c7596083d). The generic `guitar` token is already supported by the repository's `instruments.ts`; it avoids inventing acoustic versus electric on credits that name only 'guitar'.

**Production.** No specific hardware or album-level studio process is demonstrably named in the consulted 1985 credits beyond routine keyboards and vocal sessions. Hence production is **empty**; do not tag `dx7`, `linndrum`, `analogtape`, `layeredvocals` or `heavyreverb` by period or style inference. Backing singers alone do not establish a documented vocal-stacking production method.

**Producers.** Kashif, Jermaine Jackson, Michael Masser and Narada Michael Walden are credited as individual *recording producers*. Some official promotional album pages compress the executive role into a simplified 'Producer: Clive Davis', but detailed recording credits and the executive-versus-track distinction must take precedence. No writer, arranger, engineer or executive producer was converted into a track producer.

**Scales, themes and resemblance.** Energy 3 balances brisk singles against quiet-storm ballads. Electronic 2 reflects live rhythm players plus some synthesizers, while groove 2 and tempo 2 reflect the original LP's slow material rather than extended club remixes. Mood 4 balances optimism/romance with yearning; theatrical 4 captures large gospel-trained climaxes; polish 5 and experimental 0 describe immaculate conventional crossover pop. *Greatest Love of All* supports `identity`, with romantic themes across the rest. `sounds_like` names are editorial sonic comparisons, **not** proof of influence. Influenced-by deliberately blank.

**Critical distinction.** Erlewine values the track diversity and light dance numbers as much as grand ballad singing, so reducing this to an all-ballad album would distort recommendations.

## 2. Whitney (1987) — `whitney-whitney`

**Genres and scope.** Ron Wynn's AllMusic styles include contemporary R&B, dance-pop, pop and adult contemporary; The Washington Post discusses quiet-storm-type ballads and a song from *Chess*. `musicaltheatre` applies only to the closing *I Know Him So Well* duet with Cissy Houston, not the overall record. The late-1980s pop/dance/ballad alternation is central (https://www.allmusic.com/album/whitney-mw0000190356; https://www.washingtonpost.com/archive/lifestyle/1987/07/10/her-second-billboards-first/397b36ab-3801-4f26-86d2-b66073ec0a0a/).

**Instrumentation; equipment-level traceability.** The transcribed original *Whitney* liner (Arista ARCD 8405) names Narada Michael Walden's kit drums, Walter Afanasieff synths, Randy Jackson synth bass, programmed percussion and synth horns on *I Wanna Dance with Somebody*; Frank Martin's DX7 and Kenny G sax on *Just the Lonely Talking Again*; Robbie Buchanan's Rhodes piano and Nathan East's bass on *Didn't We Almost Have It All*. *Love Is a Contact Sport* explicitly credits **DX7, Prophet 2002, Oberheim, Matrix 12** and Shambhu's **acoustic guitar**. *For the Love of You* credits **Moog Synth, Linn, Roland 808 programming, DX7 and Oberheim Matrix 12**. *So Emotional* explicitly credits **drum sampling**, and *Love Will Save the Day* drum programming. Strings, brass, saxophone and vibes occur on named tracks. Source: https://albumlinernotes.com/Whitney.html. Sessiondays and MusicBrainz also credit **Roy Ayers on live vibraphone** in *Love Will Save the Day*, supporting `mallets` beyond the emulated DX7 vibe timbre.

**Production tags.** `sampling` is grounded in the *So Emotional* credit; `tr808` in 'Roland 808 Programming' from *For the Love of You*; `dx7`, `oberheim`, `moog`, `prophet2002` in explicit model credits. `prophet2002` is a **new token**, and specifically **must not** be silently mapped to `prophet5`, a different machine. The liner says generic 'Linn', not unambiguously a *LinnDrum*, so `linndrum` is omitted. `drummachine` is supported by the 808/programming credits. `electricpiano` derives from named Fender Rhodes. `mallets` is supported by Roy Ayers’s **acoustic vibraphone** credited for *Love Will Save the Day* (https://musicbrainz.org/release/9774f312-e988-48d4-9624-b6ba1344682b); the separate DX7 vibes sound is synthetic. Similarly `synth` covers named synth-horn timbres, rather than asserting actual brass on those tracks; a separate credited horn section establishes real `brass` on others.

**Producers.** Walden is the default producer for all tracks unless separately noted; Masser produces *Didn't We Almost Have It All* and *You're Still My Man*, Jellybean *Love Will Save the Day*, Kashif *Where You Are*. Clive Davis executive only (https://albumlinernotes.com/Whitney.html). No need to add performer Whitney as producer on this album.

**Scales.** Electronic 3 (extensive synths, drum programming, strong acoustic ballad core); energy 3, tempo 3 and groove 3 (dance singles versus lengthy ballads); mood 4 (ecstatic singles with heartbreak counterweight); theatrical 4, polish 5, experimental 0. Lyrics range from love and longing to celebration and social feeling (*Love Will Save the Day*). The last musical-theatre song is a duet, but the album is not choral throughout.

**Critics disagree.** The Washington Post reviewer argues the album develops Whitney's personality and variety. Robert Hilburn's LA Times review criticizes an overly planned formula but hears a convincing Cyndi Lauper-like exuberance in *I Wanna Dance with Somebody*. Those judgments coexist; the `sounds_like=Cyndi Lauper` link is a critical sonic comparison, not recorded direct influence (https://www.washingtonpost.com/archive/lifestyle/1987/07/10/her-second-billboards-first/397b36ab-3801-4f26-86d2-b66073ec0a0a/; https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-1987-06-01-ca-5509-story.html).

## 3. I’m Your Baby Tonight (1990) — `whitney-imyourbabytonight`

**Genre split.** Contemporary R&B and pop remain the broad frame; new jack swing is directly documented by the genre index, a period Washington Post review (*My Name Is Not Susan*), and retrospective assessment. Pop soul, dance-pop and adult contemporary are separately named by the genre index. `funk` is limited to a rhythmic *texture*, since the Whitney Houston Estate cites Rolling Stone's original funk-and-dance-driven characterization (https://www.albumoftheyear.org/album/20686-whitney-houston-im-your-baby-tonight.php; https://www.washingtonpost.com/archive/lifestyle/style/1990/11/11/whitney-houstons-im-your-baby-strong-voice-weak-material/2c7d702a-a965-4600-b2e6-002a50f98c1f/; https://poprescue.com/2021/10/29/review-im-your-baby-tonight-by-whitney-houston-cd-1990/; https://www.whitneyhouston.com/news/whitney-houstons-im-your-baby-tonight-album-was-released-in-november-1990/). No spurious house or hip-hop album tag merely because the record has programmed dance beats.

**Instruments.** Song credits identify Babyface on bass/keys, L.A. Reid on drums/percussion, Walden's drums, Afanasieff and Biancaniello keyboards, Gerald Albright/Kenny G/Tom Scott saxophone, Frank Martin vibraphone on *Lover for Life*, Neil Larsen's Hammond organ and Wayne Linsey's piano plus trumpets/horn ensemble on *I'm Knockin'*. Track 8 names a *Linn* drum contribution and a synthesizer, but again fails to identify a specific LinnDrum unit, hence no `linndrum` production token. The generic `guitar` tag keeps instrumentation literal when credits fail to specify electric/acoustic (https://sessiondays.com/2024/01/22/1990-whitney-houston-im-your-baby-tonight/).

**Production.** L.A. Reid recounts Whitney recording and **doubling/tripling** her lead and backing harmonies on the title track; `layeredvocals` is therefore unusually strong album-specific firsthand evidence. Programmed drums are mentioned, but no source establishes a specific 808, MPC, or Fairlight; none is included (https://www.whitneyhouston.com/track/im-your-baby-tonight/).

**Producer distinctions.** The original song-by-song credit transcript lists **L.A. Reid, Babyface** (1,2,5,6); **Narada Michael Walden** (3,4,7); **Luther Vandross** (8); **Stevie Wonder** (9); **Michael Masser**, co-produced with **Mark Spier** (10); **Whitney Houston** and **Rickey Minor** (11); and co-producer **Yvonne Turner** (1,2,5). Whitney also acted as executive producer on the album; the list includes Whitney exclusively because she is also explicitly credited as a *song* producer on track 11. Clive Davis executive only. Some international mixes/reissues differ, so confirm Yvonne Turner's role for the exact source edition on import (https://sessiondays.com/2024/01/22/1990-whitney-houston-im-your-baby-tonight/).

**Scales and topics.** Higher percussive density than the 1985/1987 releases but still multiple slow ballads: energy 3, electronic 3, tempo 3, groove 3, mood 3, theatrical 4, polish 5, experimental 0. Themes span romance, infidelity/misrecognition (*My Name Is Not Susan*), adult sensuality and personal assertion. `sounds_like` is editorial; Janet Jackson/Bobby Brown represent era's dance/R&B timbre, Vandross and Anita Baker its quieter side, with no claim that they directly influenced the album.

**Disagreement.** The 1990 Los Angeles Times praises the harder dance sound but criticizes much songwriting as anonymous. The Washington Post praises the new producer roster and some funk/rhythm work while questioning Whitney's interpretation of individual songs. These evaluations should not force `mood` or `experimental` upward (https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-1990-11-04-ca-5441-story.html; https://www.washingtonpost.com/archive/lifestyle/style/1990/11/11/whitney-houstons-im-your-baby-strong-voice-weak-material/2c7d702a-a965-4600-b2e6-002a50f98c1f/).

## 4. The Bodyguard: Original Soundtrack Album (1992) — `whitney-thebodyguardoriginalsoundtrackalbum`

**Identity and listening unit.** The original soundtrack's six Whitney songs are followed by tracks featuring **other artists** plus an instrumental film cue. Rate the **whole original 13-track soundtrack**, not just the six Whitney cuts. The estate's current listing shows **12 tracks**, omitting instrumental Kenny G's *Waiting for You*; original 13-track Sessiondays record includes it. This affects `voice=instrumental` and the sonic diversity. Avoid conflating the 2017 *I Wish You Love* recordings with the original master (https://www.whitneyhouston.com/music/bodyguard-original-soundtrack-album/; https://sessiondays.com/2022/09/29/1992-soundtrack-the-bodyguard/).

**Genres with scope.** The Recording Academy identifies Whitney's first-half romantic power-pop ballads, dance/R&B version of *I'm Every Woman*, rock-flavored *Queen of the Night*, and the pop/gospel *Jesus Loves Me*. LA Times independently hears neo-disco and hard rock on two contrasting tracks and notes the guest half is a stylistic mixed bag. `soundtrack` occupies the **last position** because it describes media context, not sonics. `powerballad` is a standardized genre taxonomy for Academy's *power pop ballads* (scope some songs); it is not an album-wide genre. `disco`, `rock`, `gospel`, `dancepop` are localized, never assigned to every cut (https://www.grammy.com/news/bodyguard-soundtrack-25-years-after-whitney-houstons-masterpiece/; https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-1992-11-22-ca-1891-story.html).

**Instrumentation and production.** Session musician records credit David Foster's keyboards/synth bass, Ricky Lawson's drums, bass players, Kirk Whalum's sax on *I Will Always Love You*, Simon Franglen's **Synclavier** on *I Have Nothing* and *Run to You*, Dean Parks's specifically credited **acoustic guitar** on the latter, Vernon Reid's guitar solo and Babyface's organ/programmed drums on *Queen of the Night*, and full-kit/keys/piano/sax on the Aaron Neville/Kenny G contribution. One 1992 LA Times review directly describes Whitney vocals being **multitracked** on *Queen of the Night*: `layeredvocals`. Gerrick Kennedy’s Library of Congress essay specifically discusses Whitney’s **belting** in her 1992 recording and describes its dynamic vocal crescendo; add `belting`. These three production tags are track-level evidence, not omnipresent. The score cue is instrumental; Aaron Neville/Joe Cocker and others make this a multi-voice collection. There is no evidence the album was a live recording (https://sessiondays.com/2022/09/29/1992-soundtrack-the-bodyguard/; https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-1992-11-22-ca-1891-story.html). String arrangements are specifically credited to Jeremy Lubbock/William Ross etc. on *I Have Nothing* and *Run to You*, supporting `strings`; this does not require unverified `orchestra`.

**Producers.** The production-credits list is an **album union**, including contributing guest tracks. By first-seen song: **David Foster** (tracks 1,2,4 and 7), **Narada Michael Walden** (3), **L.A. Reid and Babyface** (5), **BeBe Winans and Whitney Houston** (6), **Walter Afanasieff** (7), **Andy Morris/Ian Devaney** (8), **Robert Clivillés/David Cole** (9), **Danny Kortchmar** (10), **Kenny G** (11), **Charlie Midnight** (12), **Alan Silvestri** (13). **Duran Ramos** additionally receives *rap vocal producer* credit on track 9; as a component-specific producer, include but audit if Fandome prefers top-level track producers only. **Whitney and Clive Davis** are also co-executive producers, but Whitney qualifies in the production list **through her track-6 credit only**, whereas Clive does not. **Ricky Crespo** marked assistant producer, excluded. These roles should be revisited against a physical release if edition differs (https://sessiondays.com/2022/09/29/1992-soundtrack-the-bodyguard/).

**Influence, not simple song authorship.** Dolly Parton wrote and originally recorded *I Will Always Love You*. This alone is **not** evidence she directly shaped Whitney's recorded *arrangement*. The Library of Congress essay documents David Foster providing a **Linda Ronstadt recording as the reference** for Whitney's preparation; therefore `influenced_by=Linda Ronstadt` is **track-specific and documented**, not a claim about the full soundtrack. Similarly, Chaka Khan's recorded original is a documented cover model for *I'm Every Woman*, but avoid asserting a separate album-wide stylistic influence without direct production testimony (https://lcweb2.loc.gov/static/programs/national-recording-preservation-board/documents/I-Will-Always-Love-You_Kennedy.pdf; https://www.grammy.com/news/deep-10-whitney-houstons-the-bodyguard-original-soundtrack-album/).

**Scales and lyrics.** The familiar, polished film power ballads predominate (tempo 2, groove 2, electronic 2), balanced by a couple dance and hard-rock cuts (energy 3). Very prominent vocal staging (theatrical 5), dark/warm/celebratory alternation (mood 3), commercial songwriting (experimental 0), studio gloss (polish 5). Lyrics include romantic devotion and loss, spiritual gospel subject and *Peace, Love and Understanding* social appeal. Celine Dion, Chaka Khan and En Vogue mark musical proximity only; the album is not a standalone Whitney studio LP.

**Critic disagreements.** The Grammy retrospective celebrates vocal range and stylistic breadth. Chris Willman at LA Times admires some vocal delivery but criticizes the big-ballad finale, rock crossover and guest half. Both opinions are maintained without conflating negative critical judgments with low technical polish (https://www.grammy.com/news/bodyguard-soundtrack-25-years-after-whitney-houstons-masterpiece/; https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-1992-11-22-ca-1891-story.html).

## Cross-album data quality / do not infer

- `influenced_by` stays blank for the first three releases because no album-specific direct influence was established. It is never populated from `sounds_like` or from the artist's early-life influences.
- `guitar` is an **existing Fandome instrument token** even though the latest user-supplied abbreviated list does not name it: generic guitar track credits do not justify electric/acoustic guessing. The exact `instruments.ts` includes `guitar`.
- The instruments lists are **unions of evidenced original-album track credits**, ordered by prominence, not claims each plays on every track. `production` is also a union but requires a credited technique or machine, never genre deduction.
- `soundtrack` is included as a context tag only at the end of the primary sonic genres for *The Bodyguard*.
- Dates for *I’m Your Baby Tonight* conflict in some retailer metadata (October or November); original Whitney estate discography and multiple retrospectives support **November 6, 1990**. The 1990 single released earlier, do not conflate single and album release dates.
- The latest Fandome validator will reject the new **`prophet2002`** production token until Claude adds it to `src/data/production.ts`, as explicitly requested by the user. It will also reject the new soundtrack album until Claude creates its catalog entry. Those two steps are part of expected import, not failures in the draft data.
