# The Chemical Brothers — Phase 01: evidence notes

Source review date: 2026-10-25. Original editions only. Rows are editorial judgements, not lab-measured values. `instrument` entries describe credited or specifically reviewed instruments heard; a guitar audible in a sample is not necessarily a freshly recorded guitar. Evidence strength in `sources.csv` grades **support of its claims**, not equal certainty of all fields in the descriptor.

## Fourteenth Century Sky (1994) — `chemicalbrothers-fourteenthcenturysky`

- **Identity**: EP originally **credited to The Dust Brothers**. This is the same UK duo, who subsequently became The Chemical Brothers. Keep the artist field as the Fandome room name; credited producer remains `The Dust Brothers`. A US production team also used this name; do not merge their credits. AOTY identifies the four tracks as Chemical Beats / One Too Many Mornings / Dope Coil / Her Jazz. Some archival titles render this `14th Century Sky` or append `E.P.`; retain canonical `Fourteenth Century Sky` pending catalogue metadata check.
- **Genre and scope**: Album of the Year lists big beat, breakbeat, acid house, downtempo for the EP. Ed Simons confirms an early EP track closest to trip-hop (The Quietus). The more subdued track belongs under `downtempo`/`triphop`, not every cut.
- **Instrumentation**: Stereogum describes Chemical Beats as combining a TB-303-like acid line, funk breaks, cowbell, guitar and a multitracked vocal sound. `synth`, `electricguitar`, `percussion` reflect those **heard textures**; source does not prove the sampled guitar was recorded during the EP sessions. `tb303` is a critic's device attribution, not a manufacturer session sheet. Do not claim sampler/drum-machine models beyond this.
- **Production**: `sampling` includes documented borrowed sound fragments, and `layeredvocals` the explicitly multitracked utterance. These are mostly **track-level** claims about Chemical Beats, not a complete EP equipment log.
- **Scales (energy 4; electronic 5; mood 3; theatrical 1; experimental 3; polish 2; tempo 3; groove 4)**: Energy and groove reflect the aggressive Chemical Beats riff and breaks, tempered by One Too Many Mornings. Electronic sources predominate; no evidence for theatrical singing. Its lean, rough assembly warrants relatively low polish versus later albums, but exact technical fidelity is not independently measured. Tempo = mixture of breaks and downtempo.
- **Voice/language/lyrics**: Predominantly instrumental. Fragmentary sampled exclamations do not establish lead-singing register or a main album language; empty lyrics/language deliberate.
- **Influences**: Early DJ inspirations from The Quietus are **general pre-album context**, not proof of an influence on a specific EP track. Do not fill `influenced_by` from sample credits or same-era resemblance.
- **Comparisons**: `sounds_like` is editorial sonic similarity (break-led rave) not a claim of influence.
- **Counterevidence**: AOTY genre tagging is database metadata with thin critic coverage; EP warrants `medium` confidence despite the track-level high-quality retrospective. `landmark=1` for early defining Chemical Beats recording, not a claim that the entire EP was a commercial blockbuster.

## Exit Planet Dust (1995) — `chemicalbrothers-exitplanetdust`

- **Genres**: AllMusic assigns acid house, big beat, electronica, funky breaks, house, techno, acid techno, trip-hop and the broad club/dance category; Guardian/Muzik interview describes hip-hop and funk ingredients. `hiphop` and `funk` are **some-tracks/texture** cases rather than full-album dominance. Retain `funkybreaks` and `clubdance` exactly for AllMusic labels, flagging them as proposed tokens to Claude. `electronic` is AllMusic's overarching genre.
- **Instruments**: MusicBrainz release credits identify Segs Jennings on dirty bass in Leave Home and original track samplings. AllMusic hears screeching sampled electric-guitar sounds; Stereogum describes 303-like acid line and cowbell on Chemical Beats (an edited version appears on LP). The Quietus first-person account of drum-machine use refers to early material around the debut; `drummachine` is cautiously included at **medium support**, not an identified make/model. `electricguitar` is a heard sampled timbre, not a documented studio guitarist on all tracks.
- **Production**: `sampling` is evidenced by multiple release sample relationships, including Kraftwerk on Leave Home. `tb303` is tied specifically to Chemical Beats, **not** a claim that all songs use a TB-303. No unverified 808/909/AKAI/Moog tags.
- **Voice/lyrics**: Guardian's contemporary interview identifies guest lead vocals from Tim Burgess (Life Is Sweet) and Beth Orton (Alive Alone); song-based assessment yields `instrumental|soft|clear`. English vocals exist, but specific album-wide lyric themes have insufficient coverage.
- **Scales (4/5/3/2/3/3/3/4)**: AllMusic emphasizes large, abrasive sampled dance tracks; the 1995 duo interview says the LP is sequenced with a sustained dance opening and more home-listening material later. Thus energy and danceability high but not maximum; mood is mixed and tempo varies. The experimental juxtaposition and 1995 studio production are substantial without the later full-album maximal polish.
- **Producers**: MusicBrainz lists the producer for album recordings as The Chemical Brothers. Engineers Steve Dub, Dan Zamani, and Tim Holmes are not automatically co-producers.
- **Influence**: A sampled Kraftwerk recording on Leave Home is an **interpolation/sample relationship**, not necessarily a documented compositional inspiration for the album. `influenced_by` intentionally blank. `sounds_like` is editorial.
- **Release year**: UK original June 1995; August 1995 dates refer to North American issues.

## Dig Your Own Hole (1997) — `chemicalbrothers-digyourownhole`

- **Genres**: AllMusic labels big beat, electronica, funky breaks, techno, trip-hop, acid house and house. Its `Club/Dance` is kept as `clubdance`. Apple Music emphasizes breakbeats and the album's jazzy downtempo interludes, whereas AllMusic describes hip-hop colliding with hardcore techno **on Block Rockin Beats**. The SHFL assigns neo-psychedelia and tech house; those are weaker, narrower descriptions than big beat, and put late. `hardcoretechno` is **track-specific** not the album's overall sound.
- **Instruments**: AllMusic specifically notes synths and layered drum machines; MusicBrainz credits Alistair B. H. Friend with bass and bass synthesizer on the title track and Segs on bass. WIRED interviews the duo about making the LP with samplers and digital editing. Apple identifies sampled guitar licks. Accordingly `sampler` and `sequencer` are actual production-device evidence; `electricguitar` can be sampled rather than newly played.
- **Production**: WIRED (December 1997, with Simons) names Steinberg Cubase and ReCycle as software used when making Dig Your Own Hole, and sampling as central. AllMusic explicitly says they distorted loops, rhythms and samples; therefore `sampling|distortion|cubase|recycle`. Do not tag `liverecording` because they performed/remixed sequences live in production; do not tag a named AKAI model without album-specific support.
- **Scales (5/5/2/3/4/4/4/5)**: Apple describes massed, pulsing sampled rave tracks with slower jazzy passages; AllMusic hears pounding, maximal arrangements. Tom Rowlands, interviewed by DJ Mag, contrasts Dig's darker, disorientating psychedelia with Surrender's brighter atmosphere, supporting mood 2. High groove and fast-driving tempo reflect the album's dominant, but not universal, sections.
- **Voice/lyrics**: Recorded guest vocals from Noel Gallagher (Setting Sun) and Beth Orton (Where Do I Begin); much of the LP remains instrumental, with short sampled speech. The sources do not justify a uniform lyrical theme.
- **Influenced by vs sounds like**: AllMusic explicitly calls Setting Sun an homage to The Beatles' Tomorrow Never Knows; this qualifies `The Beatles` in influenced_by at **some-tracks scope**. Sample credits for Schoolly D and Quincy Jones do **not** prove influence. Similarity to The Prodigy etc. remains editorial.
- **Editions**: The 2022 25th anniversary version includes previously unreleased session tracks, demos, and alternate mixes: do not score these as the 1997 studio album.

## Surrender (1999) — `chemicalbrothers-surrender`

- **Genre disagreements**: AllMusic centers big beat, electronica, house, progressive house and funky breaks. DJ Mag, with Rowlands interviewed, stresses trance/acid house and psychedelia. Apple calls it breakbeat and identifies psychedelic leanings. Pitchfork hears a Krautrock-like feel in **Music: Response**: `krautrock` is explicitly **texture**, not a whole-record classification. These perceptions can coexist; the genre evidence CSV preserves differing scopes. `psychedelia` is a new token because it is not the same as existing neopsychedelia/psychedelicrock.
- **Instruments**: Pitchfork explicitly describes analog synth bleeps on Music:Response and layered synth patterns throughout; MusicBrainz credits Bernard Sumner with guitar on Out of Control, Jonathan Donahue with acoustic guitar and piano on Dream On. The list intentionally omits flute: Pitchfork describes flute-*like* timbre, not necessarily a flute performance. Likewise no unconfirmed 808, 909, vocoder or specific synth brand.
- **Production**: MusicBrainz lists borrowed clips (e.g. Nicole Wray/Missy Elliott on Music:Response and Rock Master Scott & the Dynamic Three on Hey Boy Hey Girl), supporting `sampling`. No evidence conclusively establishes album-wide `vocoder` or `layeredvocals`. Cheeky Paul's editorial credit and Steve Dub's engineering are not co-producer status.
- **Voice/language/lyrics**: Guest voices include Bernard Sumner, Bobby Gillespie, Noel Gallagher, Hope Sandoval and Jonathan Donahue (MusicBrainz, DJ Mag, Apple). `instrumental|soft|clear|spoken` describes instrumentals, intimate Sandoval singing, other clear vocals and repeated samples; language English. Lyrics themes remain blank rather than equating club functionality with `party` lyric content.
- **Scales (4/4/4/3/3/5/3/4)**: DJ Mag reports deliberately shining/glossy production from Rowlands; confirms high polish. Tracks combine euphoric four-on-the-floor club cuts with gentler Asleep from Day, Dream On and the opening of Sunshine Underground, supporting energy/groove 4 and tempo 3, brighter mood 4. Acoustic guitar/piano mean electronic 4 rather than automatic 5; this is an editorial **relative** scale.
- **Influence**: Pitchfork identifies the Beatles-like rhythmic homage of Let Forever Be (Tomorrow Never Knows), supporting `The Beatles` as track-specific influence. The resemblance to Kraftwerk and New Order in critics' prose belongs under sounds_like; Bernard Sumner appearing on Out of Control is a collaboration, **not by itself proof** of New Order influencing the record.
- **Edition**: 1999 studio tracks only. Bonus material in the 2019 20th-anniversary reissue, including Secret Psychedelic Mixes, excluded from ratings.

## Cross-album cautions

- All genres marked `texture` or `some tracks` legitimately appear at the **end** of a genre field; leading tokens weight broad stylistic identity. AllMusic's `Club/Dance` is a broad database category, not a tightly delimited scene; Claude may decide how to model it, but should not silently substitute `dance`.
- The exact alias/original credit for the 1994 EP needs preservation; original producer field uses The Dust Brothers, while the canonical Fandome artist field uses The Chemical Brothers.
- `cubase`, `recycle` are proposed production tokens (software), and `funkybreaks`, `clubdance`, `hardcoretechno`, `psychedelia` are proposed genre tokens. These must be added to the appropriate Fandome enumerations **and the validator** before any final validation.
- No unverified speculative hardware attributions. Album-to-album similarities are not treated as influence evidence.
