# Gala — Fandome Phase 01 evidence notes

Research cut-off: 25 October 2026. Evidence is for **Gala Rizzatto**, not the GALA festival and not other artists named Gala. The verified catalogue artist ID is `gala`.

## Methods and scope

This is a deliberately two-row exception to the usual 3–4-row phase. Independent catalogue and release databases substantiate **two original studio albums**; the intervening 2007 anniversary album repeats existing songs, and later EPs are built mainly from singles and remixes. The 2009 original album is distinguished from the EP preview and the 2012 deluxe expansion. Do not import 2007/2012 reissues as separate main albums without a new landmark rationale. The unresolved `L’Album` claim is recorded in the audit, not silently converted into an invented album.

* Scales are original whole-record assessments tied to track-by-track criticism, not averages of genre presets.
* Genre evidence is per token and includes a `scope`; texture or some-track assignments belong at the end of the list and have low influence.
* `sources.csv` separates label credits, firsthand interviews, reviews and lesser-strength databases. Source URL strings are linkable, not invented.
* Never upgrade an inferred piece of gear to a credited instrument. In particular, **no TR-909, 808, software DAW, sampler brand, sidechain, or autotune** is evidenced for either album.
* `sounds_like` records an editorial sonic comparison; `influenced_by` is reserved for artists personally or publicly named as inspirations in relation to the period.

## Come Into My Life (1997) — `gala-comeintomylife`

**Album and year.** Fandome catalog.ts already lists 1997. Apple Music/Do It Yourself identifies the original 1997 phonogram, while AllMusic indexes a 1998 import. A 10-track streaming version and an 11-track CD edition differ by a remix; no extra album. MusicBrainz and the label release identify Molella and Phil Jay as musical producers. Gala is credited with arrangement and mixing, **not** production.

**Genre, relative weights.** `eurodance` is attached explicitly to its signature recording, “Freed From Desire”; `dancepop` and `dance` describe the wider accessible electronic pop direction. `house`/`eurohouse` and `italodance` are based on critic/record-label master-release characterisations of particular versions. `latinpop` is an **AllMusic categorisation that is not borne out as the dominant style**: retain only as a low-weight texture associated with the flamenco-adjacent instrumental “Summer Eclipse”. `acidtechno` similarly describes a critic's historical sonic association, **not** a statement that every track is acid techno. Other genre names mentioned in secondary track commentary have not been promoted without stronger evidence.

**Musicians and sounds.** Pop Rescue identifies synths and synth bass throughout tracks including “Keep the Secret”, “Come Into My Life”, “Suddenly” and “Freed From Desire”; keyboards, organ and piano appear on “10 O’Clock”; handclaps and drums are specifically mentioned for “Freed From Desire”. The acoustic guitar on “Summer Eclipse” is attributed to **Tonino Baliardo** by AllMusic and the album review. Percussion features in both the slow “Freed From Desire” and “Summer Eclipse”. This list covers **documented main or noticeable** instruments without inventing model-specific synthesisers.

**Production.** `layeredvocals` is traceable to the review's description of Gala adding her own background vocals on “Suddenly”. It is a track-specific example, not an assertion of stacking across the album. Apple credits Phil Jay and Molella as producers, engineers and multi-instrumental performers, while Gala handles mixing/arrangement. Some critics object to her flat, nasal voice; guide tokens cannot represent “nasal”. `clear|soft` therefore describes relatively restrained, unforced vocal presentations, including the slow version, without claiming a powerful belting style.

**Energy 3 / electronic 4 / mood 3 / theatrical 2 / experimental 1 / polish 4 / tempo 3 / groove 4.** The major singles are strongly dance-driven, but “Summer Eclipse”, “10 O’Clock”, and the slow version of “Freed From Desire” lower the whole-album averages. Sparse delivery and largely conventional structures limit theatrical and experimental ratings. Production is carefully assembled but not uniformly maximalist.

**Lyrics and influences.** Gala told Soundwall that “Freed From Desire” was written from a Buddhist-prayer idea and addresses liberation from money/status; she described “Let A Boy Cry” as a message about gender stereotypes and permission for boys to express feelings. Accordingly `identity|social|spiritual` is warranted beside love/dance themes. General early admiration for Prince and the Beatles is documented, but no interview securely pins either artist to these **particular recorded arrangements**, so `influenced_by` is blank; use `sounds_like` only for contextual musical proximity. AllMusic compares her singles to **Dubstar**.

**Disagreements.** AllMusic downrates the vocal technique and calls the album Latin Pop despite dominant dance music. Pop Rescue likewise finds softer tracks uneven and says the famous dance single misrepresents the whole album; the evidence notes preserve that divergence rather than rating the entire album as euphoric high-energy Eurodance.

## Tough Love (2009) — `gala-toughlove`

**Release/version.** Artist label Matriarchy's 12-track album was first issued September 6, 2009 (Apple/Spotify). A 2009 **EP** anticipated the record; contemporary reviews of the EP are used only for songs subsequently present on the album. The 2012 deluxe release includes additional mixes; it does not change the source album's date or number of entries in Fandome.

**Genre weights.** Wildy’s World describes the collision of alternate/alternative rock guitars with dance beats. NeuFutur explicitly identifies rock, dance, pop, R&B and funk on specific tracks; the disco mention is a **limited reference to a reinterpretation of “Freed From Desire”**. RockWired calls the later NYC material grittier electropop. `poprock|dancepop` are therefore the centre; `rnb|funk|disco` go last, limited to named songs and textures. It would be inaccurate to replicate the 1997 `eurodance` weighting just because the album revisits that song.

**Musicians and instruments.** Apple credits **Deantoni Parks (drums), Itamar Ziegler (bass), and Joseph Friedman (guitar)** on the album-era “Faraway”, “Different Kind Of Love” and “I'm The World”. The reviewers independently mention the alternative guitar on “Different Kind Of Love” and rock backing on “You and Me”. We use the existing catalogue token `guitar` rather than guessing acoustic vs electric; the exact guitar technology or synthesizer hardware is **not documented** in the retrieved sources. Despite dance beats, `drummachine` is not asserted from style alone.

**Production.** This field is intentionally **blank**, meaning no sufficiently corroborated studio process/device token. The “Un-Remix” is a new **arrangement**, not proof of sampled audio, hence no `sampling` token. A team of musicians and producers is supported; how they recorded the mixes is not separately sourced.

**Producer attribution.** In his RockWired interview with Gala, Brian Lush asks specifically who produced the album; Gala calls **Marcus Bell** the principal producer. Wise Music confirms he worked with her to create Tough Love. Apple’s song-level “Faraway” credits additionally identify **Gala and Ali-Dee** as producers with Marcus Bell. Kevin Rudolf is described in Wise Music and artist biographies as a collaborator and producer on “Faraway”, but Apple’s displayed credits for a version of that song omit him: **version-specific conflict, retain at medium confidence**. Album of the Year lists **Tamir Muskat and Mike Daly** as producers (with Gala, Ali Dee, Rudolf), though Gala’s first-person account emphasises Marcus Bell, whom that aggregator omits. Do not treat Tamir's composing credit as equivalent to production: supporting source for him as a producer is the release-index credits. Deantoni Parks is explicitly a **drummer**, not a producer. Credit order privileges the principal producer verified by Gala.

**Energy 3 / electronic 2 / mood 3 / theatrical 3 / experimental 1 / polish 4 / tempo 3 / groove 3.** Contemporary reviews identify several guitar/band-based mid-tempo songs alongside dance-pop beats. Compared with Come Into My Life there is more guitar, some alternative grit, and more confessional material. No firsthand evidence permits exact analog/digital production methods or unusually experimental construction.

**Voice.** Reviewers find a more assertive, sometimes sharp and wavering/rough delivery than her 1997 recordings, balanced by softer sections. `clear|raspy|soft` only approximates the guide's limited eight tokens; the “raspy” assignment is the least secure of these three and not an assertion of a permanently gravelly voice.

**Themes and documented inspirations.** RockWired interviews Gala about the loneliness and relationships that motivated songwriting and directly elicits her admiration for **Prince**, **Pat Benatar**, **Blondie** and **the Beatles**. These are evidenced influences she discusses during the *Tough Love* promotion, though not a claim of session-by-session copying. A syndicated publicity bio additionally names Chrissie Hynde, Joan Jett, Debbie Harry and the B-52's, but this uncredited text is **insufficient on its own** for the stricter `influenced_by` standard. By contrast comparisons to **Grace Jones** on “Do It” and **the Go-Go’s** on “You and Me” are sonic analogies; they remain only in `sounds_like`.

**Critical disagreement.** Wildy’s World praises the rock/dance crossover but dislikes the affected verse voice on the title track and cautions that the rock turn sometimes feels imposed. NeuFutur reviews the same EP-era material very enthusiastically and praises the vocals and new genre mixture. The incompatible judgments should not be presented as a unanimous critical consensus.
