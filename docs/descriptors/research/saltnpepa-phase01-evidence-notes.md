# Salt-N-Pepa — Fandome Phase 01: Evidence and editorial decisions

Scope: four original studio albums, 1986–1993. All descriptors are `draft` editorial judgments (0–5 scales), not automatic measurements. Evidence references are in the accompanying sources CSV. The four albums share a New York rap origin, but their sonic changes are substantial.

## Hot, Cool & Vicious (1986) — `saltnpepa-hotcoolvicious`

**Edition:** original US LP has 8 tracks and **does not contain Push It**. That single appeared in 1987; its Cameron Paul remix was later added to reissues. Do not claim the 1986 debut already contained the hit. `electro` is deliberately fifth because Rolling Stone discusses Push It's electro-bass, and the later version is much better known. The genre evidence file explicitly scopes it to the later-added track. The main sound of the original record remains pop-rap/hip-hop; Rolling Stone also calls production go-go inspired. `eastcoasthiphop` reflects Album of the Year's album-level exact classification; this is a new genre token for Claude to add, not a rename of `hiphop`.

**Production and instruments:** the Christgau review identifies sampled hooks and DJ scratching, grounding `sampling` and `turntables`; Chris Parker has a documented live drum credit; the later inserted song's original electronic hook grounds synth/synth-bass/keyboards (specific later-edition scope). *No brand/model of drum machine is claimed.* The scale `electronic 3` is an editorial balance of this studio and electronic material, not a claim that all tracks use synths. `rap|spoken` captures sharply rhythmic, spoken interjections, without inventing an R&B lead vocal.

**Credits:** Hurby Azor leads production; Stephen Keitt is credited specifically on I Desire, retained because named album producer by AllMusic and 1986 pressings. Vocals/backing and live drums appear in credit sources; not assumed for every track. Lyrics emphasize challenging sexist treatment, relationships, swagger, party atmosphere. `influenced_by=Roxanne Shanté` is directly grounded in Salt's interview saying Shanté's answer-record approach inspired the group; Doug E. Fresh is the *subject of their answer record*, not thereby an album influence. The sounds_like set is a broad editorial sonic judgment and is not represented as a sourced influence. `landmark=1` for first successful album and chart crossover; Christgau's A− is separate from commercial performance. Production polish 3: clear production without 1990/1993 layering; experimental 2: clever, not structurally radical.

## A Salt with a Deadly Pepa (1988) — `saltnpepa-asaltwithadeadlypepa`

**Genres:** `hiphop|poprap` are AllMusic categories. International LP/CD pressing descriptions also identify electro; E.U. supply go-go on the notable single Shake Your Thang. Soul is included as an album pressing style, with scope deliberately qualified by vintage covers and E.U.'s R&B approach. No claim that the *whole LP* is go-go or soul. `remixing` relates to Let the Rhythm Run (Remix), not a blanket characterization. `sampler` reflects documented audio samples on Shake Your Thang; a specific hardware sampler is not known. Turntable work corresponds to Spinderella's credited DJ role. E.U.'s go-go feature does not establish any particular percussion instrument, so none is added. Do not invent a drum-machine model. If Fandome demands explicit instrument-per-track credits, Claude should inspect original sleeve before treating the DJ as a verified instrument credit.

**Credits:** 1988 international original pressings list Hurby Luv Bug and **The Invincibles** as producers; retain both even though some US issues shorten the sleeve credit. `Push It` appears as a remix bonus on some editions; not new material originally composed for this LP. Songs incorporate reworkings/covers of Joe Tex, the Isley Brothers, and Twist and Shout; covers and samples **must not** be inserted in influenced_by. The contemporary Christgau review praises E.U.'s song but finds much of the set scattered; Steve Huey's AllMusic review is also reserved. Lower album landmark=0, confidence medium because sparse instrument credits. Scales reflect club-ready rhythms and a relatively accessible, unevenly polished sound.

## Blacks' Magic (1990) — `saltnpepa-blacksmagic`

**Genre/rhythm:** AllMusic identifies a distinct **funkier R&B foundation**, while retaining hip-hop/pop-rap. Album of the Year tags East Coast Hip Hop. The record's stronger pop hooks are documented by Mark Kemp in Trouser Press. Don't equate the album's sampling of funk records with a sole funk genre: here multiple reviewers also describe the *new overall musical base*. `keyboards` documented for Stanley Brown; `sampler` for Doper Than Dope's Honey Drippers sample and other documented reused records, not a branded sampler. Spinderella is credited as the group's DJ; turntables retained as a performance role, with precise track scratching not universally documented. Voice `rap|spoken|clear` is the album's brash duo interplay and melodic sung hooks, not a claim Salt was the sole singer.

**Production:** multiple track-level producers, not just Hurby Azor: Invincibles; Salt (Expression, Start the Party, Independent, also I Like to Party), Steevee-O Boy Wonder, Excalibur, Spinderella, Quicksilver. List excludes purely guest performers and mixers. Some sources differ on team vs individual credits; MusicBrainz and original liner personnel used for the producer column. Salt's original production on Expression represents a meaningful shift toward creative control. 'You Showed Me' is a cover, Coltrane is sampled; **neither automatically counts as an influence**. Lyrics include identity, sexism, race, sexual responsibility (Let's Talk About Sex) and independence. Mood 3 reflects bright hooks alongside a serious theme; 4 polish reflects fuller, more sophisticated arrangement vs earlier LPs. `landmark=1` for the recognized creative turn, not just chart position. Christgau A−; AllMusic favorable; some skepticism about crossover sheen in Trouser Press.

## Very Necessary (1993) — `saltnpepa-verynecessary`

**Genres:** 1993 album as a whole is rap/pop-rap; AllMusic describes a *contemporary R&B edge* and Trouser Press identifies **dancehall elements** alongside smooth R&B; the major single Shoop is explicitly described as funky. These textural/minority genres are positioned after the main rap styles. This is not an all-over dancehall album. `rap|spoken|clear` accounts for spoken asides and some clearer melodic and ensemble singing, notably with guest En Vogue; do not add `choral` merely from a guest vocal group. En Vogue is a contributor, not a source of influence.

**Recording/instruments:** original CD sleeve says tracks 1–7, 9 and 12 recorded/mixed analog; track 11 recorded/mixed digitally and edited with sound tools. Thus `analogtape` is typical and `digitalrecording|digitalediting` are track-11-only production tail descriptors. Keyboardists Avon Marshall and Harold Edgar are individually documented; a retrospective listening review identifies synthesized textures on No One Does It Better. `sampler` tracks are grounded in Pitchfork's sample discussion (e.g. the J.B.s sax sample on Break of Dawn); no claim of live guest sax without a credit. Production by Hurby Azor is extensive **but not exclusive**. The album's track-level credits also name Salt, Pepa, Spinderella, Mark Sparks, Steve Azor, DJ Wynn and Tommy Shannon (co-producer) in original sources. Co-producers are explicitly allowed in Fandome producer column; executive producers, engineers, guest singers and other recording staff are excluded. Other credits may differ between editions; confirmed sources in sources CSV.

**Lyrics and reviews:** female autonomy/sexual boundaries (None of Your Business), relationships (Whatta Man, Shoop) and AIDS public-awareness PSA (social) plus major single fun. `political` relates to the public-health/social-rights stance and should be treated as *editorial*, not an explicit party-political manifesto; Claude can remove if too broad. Pitchfork (Claire Lobenfeld, 2017) and AllMusic (Jason Birchmeier) praise its political/creative force; the contemporary Los Angeles Times review (Dennis Hunt, Oct 1993) criticises the beats and construction. The disagreement is retained, not averaged away. As part of the artist's best-known work, landmark 1. Varying sources give different numbers of tracks for original vs 2023 anniversary edition; use 1993 13-track recording as album basis and do not import later remixes as new albums.

## General interpretation

- Unproven drum-machine models, synth models, hardware samplers, and album-wide vocal effects remain blank / omitted, not inferred from era.
- `sounds_like` lists are editorial sonic neighbours; *not* claims of influence. `influenced_by` remains empty when evidence is insufficient for a specific album.
- `eastcoasthiphop` is the only **new proposed genre token**; it must get a parents entry. All other genre/instrument/production tokens are already present in the GitHub branch checked 2026-10-29.
- Intro, remixes, anniversary bonus tracks and compilations do not become separate descriptor rows.
