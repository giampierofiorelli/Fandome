# Subsonica — phase 01: audit-ready evidence notes

Date of audit: 27 October 2026. Each line below is an editorial judgement anchored to **this album**, not an inference from the band's general style. Ratings refer to the whole album, rather than a single hit or bonus edition. For all short genre names see the separate one-genre-per-row evidence sheet. Source identifiers below refer to the sources CSV and the linked outlets.

## Subsonica (1997) — `subsonica-subsonica`

- **Genres:** Paolo Ciro (OndaRock, *Pietre Miliari*) uses the composite label *electro-dub-pop*. Rolling Stone explicitly hears pop-rock, funk, reggae and club elements. OndaRock describes British trip-hop, jungle and big beat as stylistic threads; these are placed **last** because they are reference colours and do not each govern the whole LP. `electrorock` and `electropop` also occur as *rock elettronico* and *elettropop* in the secondary discographic metadata, but that secondary source is weak: retain for Claude review. The `pop` and `dub` tokens split a critic's literal composite category, rather than falsely translating it into a single existing near-synonym.
- **Eight scales:** energy **4** (urgent rhythms and vocal intensity), electronic **4** (synth, samplers and band rhythm section), mood **2** (urban anxiety, political tension, melancholy notwithstanding more buoyant dance tracks), theatrical **2** (direct, street-level delivery), experimental **3** (electronic, reggae/dub and sampling hybrid), polish **3** (Casacci's controlled studio sound with deliberate rough effects), tempo **4** (often propulsive), groove **4** (danceable bass and club rhythms). OndaRock and Rolling Stone each explicitly describe these characteristics.
- **Voice:** Samuel's principally clear melodic singing; isolated speech-like/hip-hop phrasing warrants `rap` as a secondary type, not a claim that he raps throughout.
- **Instruments:** Paolo Ciro expressly describes **synth**, **samplers**, Casacci **guitar** tremolo, Pierfunk **bass** and Ninja **drums**. The personnel transcription gives Boosta **keyboards**, **Fender Rhodes** (`electricpiano`), session **cello**, **violin**, **trumpet**, **trombone** and **saxophone**. The latter instruments are supporting performances, placed last; original packaging remains a worthwhile cross-check. This field includes performance evidence, not sonic analogy (e.g. synthetic violins do not count as real violins).
- **Production:** `sampling` is supported by source-described samplers and a transcribed sampled-drum credit; `programming` is explicitly credited to Casacci/Boosta; `lofi` refers to deliberately applied low-fi **effects**, not proof of an entirely low-fidelity recording; `tremolo` is explicitly heard on Casacci's guitar (OndaRock). No machine make/model is specified, so none was entered.
- **Producers:** contemporary credits list **Max Casacci** and **Subsonica** as artistic producers; transcribed album credits separately list **Boosta** as coproducer. Valerio Soave has an ambiguous generic “produzione” line and is omitted pending sleeve verification because a label/executive function is possible. Carlo U. Rossi is a mixer, not credited as producer in the data used. Original personnel transcription is a secondary account.
- **Lyrics:** Rolling Stone identifies independent political militancy, occasional nostalgia and love; OndaRock connects the album to a pressured urban environment. `it` main language.
- **Influences vs similarity:** OndaRock's qualified criticism connects the debut's dub/trip-hop blueprint directly to **Casino Royale's 1995 *Sempre più vicini*** and to **Massive Attack**-style Bristol trip-hop. The bracket in `Casino Royale (Sempre più vicini)` indicates the named album, not guest personnel. `Africa Unite` is a similarity judgement based on reggae/dub, **not** an `influenced_by` entry (Casacci's old band and a former collaboration).
- **Landmark:** `1`: independently called a *Pietra Miliare* by OndaRock, and a top-three career album by Rolling Stone. **High** confidence for the sound and album's importance; secondary credit details should be validated against sleeve imagery.

## Microchip emozionale (1999) — `subsonica-microchipemozionale`

- **Genres:** Rockol's **contemporary** review explicitly identifies drum’n’bass patterns with funky guitars and an intent to make people dance. The electronic-rock classification is corroborated by discographic material. Rolling Stone calls it the group's key hybrid breakthrough. `triphop` is a **texture** borrowed from OndaRock's overview of this early phase, rather than a central contemporary genre verdict.
- **Eight scales:** energy **4** (urgent dance tracks but also contemplative moments); electronic **4** (programmed and sampled interplay with live drums); mood **2** (social anxiety, dystopia and wistful love), theatrical **3** (dance-floor chorus delivery), experimental **3** (sophisticated drum’n’bass/funk/electro fusion), polish **4** (developed Casasonica production), tempo **4** (busy rhythms), groove **5** (multiple club/dance-floor anthems). Anchors based on Rockol 1999, OndaRock and Rolling Stone, not on *Tutti i miei sbagli* alone.
- **Instruments:** credits transcribe Casacci **guitar** and **programming**, Boosta **keyboards**, **sampler** and **programming**, Pierfunk **bass**, Ninja **drums**. `sampler` plus `sampling|programming` are direct credit derivations. Physical violin and orchestral arrangement credits reported for “track 6” are **ambiguous between the original 1999 sequence and 2000 reissue**, so **neither violin nor string-arrangement techniques are asserted** for the original.
- **Producers:** **Max Casacci|Subsonica**, album production credits. Guest performances by **Morgan**, **Daniele Silvestri**, **Claudio Coccoluto** are not producer, co-billing or album influence evidence.
- **Lyrics:** Rockol explicitly singles out dark urban stories (`social|political`), liberation/identity, depression/introspection, sentiment. Italian-language main record.
- **Landmark:** `1`: Rockol's period review and Rolling Stone's retrospective both identify it as a breakthrough. **High** sonic confidence, medium confidence for unsighted original liner personnel details.
- **Edition caveat:** the **1999 original** is the object of this row; 2000 reissue adds tracks including “Tutti i miei sbagli” and “Albe meccaniche.” Their distinct sound/guest arrangements must not be projected backwards.

## Amorematico (2002) — `subsonica-amorematico`

- **Genres:** OndaRock calls it **pop-rock** and describes a mostly song-based dark album, with an explicitly **deep-house closing suite** (four *Atmosferico* pieces by DJ Roger Rama) and **disco** pulse on “Nuova ossessione” (Krisma guest feature). The deep-house and disco entries are hence **some tracks**, low in order. Rockol's firsthand January 2002 interview documents techno-dance, `technopop` (especially “Nuova ossessione”) and 1960s electronic references; Rolling Stone identifies electro-pop as dominant texture. `triphop` is used only for a described episode, last.
- **Eight scales:** energy **3** (moderate songs versus a dance suite), electronic **4** (programmed club/electropop prominence), mood **1** (band describes album as troubled and introspective after 2001), theatrical **2** (understated even when charged), experimental **2** (more classic pop forms, with unusual finale), polish **4** (deliberately arranged and finished), tempo **3** (mid-tempo songs interspersed with faster disco), groove **4** (strong *Nuova ossessione* and long house finale). Contemporaneous self-description supports the low mood even though portions are upbeat.
- **Voice & language:** Samuel sings mainly in **Italian**, Moroccan rapper **Rachid** delivers a guest verse **in Arabic** on “Gente tranquilla.” Hence `clear|rap`, `it|ar`; minority language does not replace the main language.
- **Instruments:** band credits identify **keyboards**, **guitar**, **bass**, **drums**; the album-specific transcription identifies cello (Marco Robino), viola (Tamara Bairo), violin (Efix Puleo and Elena Gallafrio), tracks 4/7/8. These strings are supporting instruments (at the end); no specific synthesizer hardware is established by credit, so no model is added.
- **Production:** `sonicmanipulation` stems from Rockol's direct description of DJ Roger Rama's sound manipulations. No specifically proven analog/digital machine, DAW or vocal plug-in is assumed.
- **Producers:** listed as **Max Casacci|Subsonica|Roger Rama** (secondary discographic producer credits). OndaRock documents Rama's responsibility for the closing pieces, rather than assuming he produced the whole record. The participation of **Krisma** is a **feature**, not a producer credit, and guest authorship is not sufficient to assign production.
- **Documented album influence:** **Ennio Morricone** because Max Casacci explicitly tells Rockol that 1960s soundtrack arrangements and electronic textures associated with Morricone informed the record. No soundtrack `type` or `soundtrack` genre is added: this is an original studio LP. Krisma remain a **guest**, not an influence entry. Musical science-fiction cinema is cultural imagery, excluded from the music influence column.
- **Critical disagreement:** OndaRock scores it **5/10** and foregrounds uneven songwriting; Rolling Stone values its transition to a mainstream electropop identity. `landmark=1` reflects its place in the popular career arc, not universal acclaim. **High** confidence on direct interviews and track-specific genres; source-transcribed credits still need a physical-sleeve check.

## Terrestre (2005) — `subsonica-terrestre`

- **Genres:** Rockol's contemporary review explicitly foregrounds **rock** and the guitar-led shift. Rolling Stone calls it a pop-rock turn. MusiCulturA Online documents distinct **punk-rock** (“Abitudine”), **hard rock** (“L’odore”), **reggae** (“Salto nel vuoto”), **synth pop** (“Amantide”), and electronic rock (“Ratto”). These are *track-level*, entered last, not pretended whole-album labels. OndaRock also discusses trip-hop on selective cuts. A retrospective mentions *nu-metal* tendency but this is disputed as album classification; **`numetal` left out** pending better track-specific consensus.
- **Eight scales:** energy **4** (driving/abrasive guitars), electronic **2** (keyboards/synths remain, but rock ensemble dominant), mood **2** (confrontation and melancholy, with some warm songs), theatrical **3** (stronger rock delivery), experimental **2** (more conventional band-based compositions), polish **4** (studio-constructed rock and later mix), tempo **4** (fast rock, interrupted by ballads), groove **2** (largely riff/drum-driven rather than dancefloor, though one extended club cut remains). Differences among critics concern success, not the basic sonic shift.
- **Instruments:** band credits reproduced in MusiCulturA: **C-Max guitar**, **Bass Vicio bass**, **Ninja drums**, **Boosta keyboards/synth**, guest **electric piano** (“Abitudine” per critic), **Samuel acoustic guitar** on track 14, **C-Max lap steel guitar** on track 14, **Edodea Ensemble strings** tracks 6 and 13 and explicitly **violins** on “Le serpi”. `electricpiano`, `acousticguitar`, `steelguitar`, `strings` and `violin` are therefore secondary/tail instruments, not universal ones. The credited double-neck guitar remains generic `guitar`, not an invented extra token.
- **Production:** Ale Bavo's **programming** is credited specifically. He is **assistant to production**, NOT a producer under project rules. Max Casacci and Subsonica hold **artistic production** credit. The band recounts reversing its process: composing from played instruments rather than beginning in a computer; this is **not** evidence for `liverecording` (which would assert tracking simultaneously live in studio). No DAW or compressor model is reported.
- **Voice/language & lyrics:** Samuel alternates gentle melodic and forceful rock vocals; `clear|powerful` has track-level criticism. “Gasoline” is in **English**, all others principally Italian: `it|en`. Lyrical examples: interpersonal conflict (“Abitudine”), introspection and life confrontation (“Corpo a corpo”), love (“Incantevole”). No unsupported broad political tag.
- **Similarity:** reviewer explicitly compares the club track “Alba a quattro corsie” to **Faithless**, included in `sounds_like` only; Afterhours is an editorial rock-scene comparison, not a claimed influence. `influenced_by` empty.
- **Landmark:** `0` narrowly because Rolling Stone's later ranking puts the three earlier albums above this 2005 phase and considers its rocker turn less central, though Rockol admires the stylistic gamble. This is a documented judgement call, not a claim of unimportance. **High** confidence sound and musician credits.

## Cross-album exclusions and reproducibility

- No `EP`, standalone remix, compilation or live row in this phase. Early *U.F.O.*, *Preso blu*, *Colpo di pistola*, *Nuvole rapide*, *Mammifero* and *L'errore* are listed by the band as EPs but do not have sufficient separate album-level landmark evidence for this project. *Controllo del livello di rombo* (2003) is live, but landmark status for including it is not established: exclude. *Subsonica Special Edition* (1998) combines debut and live material, not a separate studio work. The 2001 film *Santa Maradona* featuring “Nuvole rapide” is not a Subsonica solo score album; do not make the 2002 LP a soundtrack.
- The ID scheme follows `artist-id + '-' + alphanumeric title slug`: no accents, spaces or punctuation. For existing album IDs preserve catalogue slug; no collisions here.
- `sounds_like` is an **editorial comparison**, not documented artistic influence. Guests, covers, former band memberships and lyrical/cinematic themes never populate `influenced_by` on their own.
- The user-supplied header and repository validator agree: **27 columns**. Header ordering is preserved without extras.
- The present 2026 discography contains *Terre rare* (2026); excluded here because phase strictly ends with the 2005 album. See the catalogue audit for seven missing studio/special releases, and the import file for subsequent phases.
