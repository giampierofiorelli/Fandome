# Pink Floyd — Phase 01: evidence and reasoning

These are album-level editorial judgments grounded in the cited sources. Instruments, effects and genre labels are not inferred from artist-era stereotypes. The strength column in the sources CSV reflects attribution quality rather than certainty of the 0–5 scales.

## The Piper at the Gates of Dawn (1967) — `pinkfloyd-thepiperatthegatesofdawn`

### Sources and sonic profile
- Official album page confirms the original **11-track UK sequence**, 1967 date and **Norman Smith** as the credited producer; the 1967 US edition and 2007 reissues may contain a different sequence. The single *See Emily Play* is excluded from the baseline original UK album.
- AllMusic and Pitchfork identify a split between brief fairytale/acid-pop songs and long psychedelic instrumental improvisation, with Barrett's dreamlike delivery and Richard Wright's organ; this supports psychedelic pop **and** extended space-rock strands, not an electronic-dance classification.
- Genre order privileges psychedelic rock, then Barrett's short-form psychedelic pop, before specific sounds on particular songs. AllMusic's label *British Psychedelia* is recorded as `psychedelia` with the geographic qualifier in the evidence text. `acidpop` and `toytownpop` are literal source genres to add; neither should be silently mapped to `pop`.
- `energy=3`: comparatively driving Interstellar Overdrive / Take Up Thy Stethoscope and Walk counterbalance shorter restrained songs. `electronic=1`: organ and studio effects are present, but band instruments dominate; this is not synthesizer-led electronic music. `mood=3`: bright surreal miniatures coexist with anxious dissonance. `theatrical=4`: story-voices, sound effects and play-acting. `experimental=4`: long improvisations, odd harmony and effects. `polish=3`: Norman Smith professional Abbey Road production without later high-gloss sheen. `tempo=3`, `groove=2`: mixed pace; not dance-oriented.
- `voice=soft|clear|spoken` is grounded in Pitchfork's languid vocal assessment and MusicBrainz's sung/vocalized credits; no claim of rapping. `lyrics=storytelling|irony`: character sketches and playful, sometimes absurd British fantasy noted by AllMusic/Pitchfork.
- Instrument provenance (MusicBrainz track relationships): guitars, bass, drums, Farfisa/Hammond/Lowrey organs, piano; vibrational/mallets including vibraphone, celesta, harmonium, cello, slide whistle on particular tracks. No speculative EMS VCS3 or period-model synth token; `production` left empty because album-specific technique attribution at the required precision is insufficient.
- `sounds_like` is a **non-sourced editorial resemblance**, not a documented influence, and carries no producer or influence claim. `influenced_by` blank because comparisons are not evidence of causal musical influence. `landmark=1`: well-documented Syd Barrett-era creative defining album.
- **Critical difference:** Pitchfork concentrates on Barrett, songwriting and the original mono mix; AllMusic emphasises the dissonant, experimental side alongside melody. These are complementary emphases, not a reason to collapse genres.

## A Saucerful of Secrets (1968) — `pinkfloyd-asaucerfulofsecrets`

### Sources and sonic profile
- Official Pink Floyd discography and AllMusic identify this as a **transition** album; Norman Smith produced. Barrett appears on certain recordings only, while David Gilmour joins. The band's own account identifies the title suite's structured, diagram-based development.
- The album leans psychedelic/progressive/space-rock, with instrumental suites outweighing its Barrett-era whimsy. `avantgarde` is restricted to the **title composition** (AllMusic song styles); not a claim that all seven songs are avant-garde. AllMusic's generic British Psychedelia maps to `psychedelia` while preserving the source's original wording.
- `energy=3`: rhythmic propulsion in Let There Be More Light balances quieter material and abstract movements. `electronic=1`: electric band and electric organs/Mellotron, not synth-based electronic production. `mood=2`: darker hypnotic pieces tempered by pastoral See-Saw. `theatrical=4`: suite's dynamics and Jugband Blues character ending. `experimental=4`: long abstract ensemble structure and dissonant textures. `polish=3`: professional EMI studio recordings. `tempo=2` and `groove=1`: deliberate/repetitive pulses rather than dancing.
- MusicBrainz documents Farfisa and Hammond organs, Mellotron, piano, vibraphone/xylophone (`mallets`), electric and acoustic/slide guitars, drums, bass, brass, kazoo and tin whistle. Secondary instruments are track-local: no assertion that all tracks have orchestral backing. Not enough precise verified technique evidence to add a production token.
- `voice=clear|soft|spoken` covers several lead singers plus wordless/whispered vocals; this is not an all-instrumental release. `lyrics=introspection|storytelling` narrowly reflects Barrett's personal farewell and Corporal Clegg's narrative; other pieces are instrumental. `influenced_by` left blank. `landmark=0` in the strict sense of universally defining Pink Floyd albums; a historically pivotal record nonetheless.
- **Source nuance:** the official band page says Barrett features on three pieces; some older summaries misleadingly imply *Jugband Blues* is his only surviving appearance. Original mono and stereo editions both appeared in 1968; don't blend edition-specific music into the row.

## More (1969) — `pinkfloyd-more`

### Sources and sonic profile
- The band's official album page establishes *More* as the first specifically composed **film soundtrack**, released in June 1969 and self-produced by Pink Floyd. `type=soundtrack` rather than using soundtrack as the only genre.
- AllMusic review explicitly contrasts pastoral folk-like songs (Green Is the Colour, Cymbaline), harsher guitar material (The Nile Song, Ibiza Bar), and atmospheric score cues (Quicksilver). AOTY identifies `psychedelicrock`, `experimentalrock` and the specific `psychedelicfolk` token. Genre scope records whole-album versus selected tracks.
- `energy=3`: aggressive segments against much quieter cues. `electronic=1`: organ, guitar, bass, drums and tape-based effects; not a synth-pop album. `mood=2`: frequently wistful/unsettling though not uniformly dark. `theatrical=2`: primarily cinematic understatement rather than stage delivery. `experimental=3`: sound-design tracks but accessible songs. `polish=3`: quick commissioned studio production of variable texture. `tempo=2`, `groove=1`: ballads and incidental instrumentals dominate despite rock tracks.
- MusicBrainz original-track relationships explicitly credit acoustic/electric guitars, Farfisa/Hammond organs, piano, bass guitar, drum kit, bongos, vibraphone (`mallets`) and Lindy Mason's **tin whistle** (`whistle`), on *Green Is the Colour* and *Party Sequence*. `tapeeffects` is specifically documented for *Up the Khyber*, and tape/gong/effects for *Quicksilver*; **do not use fieldrecordings** without proof of source recording.
- Several tracks are instrumental, others have David Gilmour on lead vocal: `voice=soft|clear|instrumental`. The full album lacks sourced, consistent lyrical themes, so `lyrics` is deliberately blank. `influenced_by` blank. `landmark=0` under the strict canonical-defining rule.
- **Caveat:** the song styles vary sharply; do not use *The Nile Song* alone to rank the entire record as heavy rock. Some streaming platforms treat it as the third studio LP; the original programme is a film score and should be a `soundtrack` row.

## Ummagumma (1969) — `pinkfloyd-ummagumma`

### Sources and sonic profile
- The original 1969 **double-LP contains one concert LP and one studio LP**, verified by the official band page, original record-side credits and AllMusic. `type=studio` is a required **schema compromise**, since Fandome permits no `mixed` type: it does *not* claim that the whole issue is studio-recorded. A future `hybrid` format model could supersede this.
- Producers require **separate credit scope**: Norman Smith produced the studio LP; Pink Floyd produced the live LP. Do not credit the engineers (Peter Mew and Brian Humphries) as producers. Both names appear in `producers`, with source-scope note.
- MusicBrainz and AllMusic document the live electric-band recordings plus Wright's instrumental keyboard suite, Waters's pastoral acoustic song and tape-based vocal collage, Gilmour's multi-instrumental suite and Mason's percussion experiment. `musiqueconcrete` source genre is relevant to Waters's manipulated voices **only**; do not describe recordings of real animals or outdoor field recording. In a 1970 interview Waters confirms he made those voices and slaps himself.
- `energy=3` reflects highly dynamic live performances alongside diffuse studio experiments. `electronic=2`: organ/synth and manipulated tape but still largely acoustic/electric instruments; electronic score is not 5. `mood=1`: often tense and austere, interrupted by peaceful Grantchester Meadows. `theatrical=3`: contrasting instrumental suites, screams, character voice effects. `experimental=5`: tape speed changes, avant-garde sound collage and individual freeform tracks. `polish=2`: rougher exploratory collage and more direct concert recordings. `tempo=2`, `groove=1`: extended and abstract material rather than dance beats.
- `concertrecording` applies **only to the live disc**. `tapeeffects` applies **only to specific studio pieces**, documented by MusicBrainz and Roger Waters testimony. No named synth machine, DAW, or fieldrecording token is warranted.
- `voice=instrumental|clear|soft|spoken|powerful` summarises mostly long instrumentals, quiet song vocals, spoken/processed imitations and forceful live outbursts. `lyrics=nostalgia|storytelling` are limited to Grantchester Meadows and the mock-rhetorical passage; no whole-set lyrical programme. `landmark=0`: historically interesting but not usually cited as one of the group's defining masterworks.
- **Critical disagreement:** AllMusic praises the live performances for drive while reporting much of the studio LP as esoteric; the specialist magazine Louder contextualises those studio pieces as experimentation and highlights individual roles. Scoring avoids treating the live side as representative of the entire original album.
