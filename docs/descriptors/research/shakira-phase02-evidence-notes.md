# SHAKIRA — PHASE 02: EVIDENCE NOTES

Source identifiers below point to exact URLs in `shakira_phase02_sources.csv`; consult each album's source rows for provenance. All eight numeric scales are **editorial judgments of the complete album** anchored to `docs/album-descriptors-guide.md`, not measurements from audio analysis.

## 1. Laundry Service (2001) — `shakira-laundryservice`

**Original edition:** the 2001 studio album, not the later Washed and Dried release or concert footage. Primarily English with several Spanish tracks. Review disagreements matter: Alex Henderson (AllMusic) finds the cross-language eclecticism convincing, Isabelia Herrera's Pitchfork retrospective contextualises both its imaginative fusion and its uneasily received English songwriting; Christian Hoard (Metro Times) hears idiosyncratic and sometimes excessive vocals. These are not mutually exclusive accounts.

**Styles/genres:** AllMusic labels the album Latin Pop, Dance-Pop, Pop and Adult Contemporary and describes pop-rock, Argentine tango (Objection), Andean music (Whenever Wherever) and Middle Eastern music (Eyes Like Yours). Pitchfork identifies a disco-inflected beat on Ready for the Good Times and post-grunge moments. The latter five flavours are **specific songs or textures**, not uniform album properties. Do not interpret `andeanmusic`/`middleeasternmusic` as claims that every track belongs to those regional traditions.

**Scales:** energy **3**, from driving singles balanced with slower romantic songs; electronic **2**, because multitrack programming sits alongside documented live guitars, bass, drums, percussion and horns; mood **3**, contrasting playful tracks with heartbreak; theatrical **3**, emphatic but not operatic; experimental **2**, distinct mixes in familiar song structures; polish **4**, carefully produced international pop; tempo **3**, ballad-to-dance mixture; groove **3**, danceable tracks and numerous ballads. Pitchfork and AllMusic support this mix.

**Voice:** `powerful` reflects critical accounts of her emphatic delivery and warbling vibrato. The exact vibrato is more precise than these limited voice tags; **neither yodel nor vibrato exists in the allowed vocabulary**, so don't invent voice tokens.

**Instruments:** MusicBrainz original edition lists electric/acoustic guitars, drums, electric and acoustic bass, percussion, mandolin, keyboards, harmonica, saxophones, horns, bandoneon, strings and Andean charango/quena/rondador; AllMusic roster corroborates. `bandoneon`, `charango`, `quena`, `rondador` are new instruments requiring controlled-list additions. These are documented instruments and are ordered by prevalence; some appear only on a single song. `strings|violin|cello` reflects credited ensemble and individual players.

**Production:** credited programming (Tim Mitchell, George Noriega), horn arrangements (Lester Mendez) and string arrangements (David Campbell). No drum machine model, DAW, Auto-Tune or tape format has been asserted. **Producers:** Shakira (principal), Lester Mendez, Tim Mitchell, George Noriega, Luis Fernando Ochoa, Javier Garza and Pablo Flores (credits/production roles reported across MusicBrainz and AllMusic). Emilio Estefan is **executive**, not admitted. Pablo Flores/Javier Garza's credits may attach to particular album versions or material; inspect original booklet at import if strict original-only producer list is required.

**Lyrics / scene / similarity:** romantic frustration, desire, female self-definition and anti-drug criticism in Poem to a Horse are detailed by Pitchfork. Sonic comparisons to B-52's and Nirvana are made there; Alanis Morissette is supported by contemporary critical comparison, not a verified album-specific influence declaration. **`influenced_by` deliberately blank**.

## 2. Fijación Oral, Vol. 1 (2005) — `shakira-fijacionoralvol1`

**Styles:** Stephen Thomas Erlewine's AllMusic review places Latin pop at the centre, with bossa nova in Obtener un Sí, ballads and dance-pop. David Ponce (Emol) directly calls La Tortura reggaeton, highlights pop electrónico, tango-flavoured bandoneon and rock on Escondite inglés. Genres are scoped individually in the genre-evidence CSV. `electropop` translates the critic's phrase 'pop electrónico', rather than inferring from keyboards; `tango` refers only to a texture.

**Scales:** energy **3** (mid-tempo mix), electronic **2** (organic arrangements and explicit programming coexist), mood **3** (sensual dance tunes and sad ballads), theatrical **3** (dramatic vocals with quiet passages), experimental **2** (eclectic inside accessible pop), polish **4** (AllMusic specifically stresses glossy finish), tempo **3**, groove **3** (La Tortura plus several ballads). No genre label alone determines the scales.

**Voice and language:** `powerful|soft` reflects Ponce's contrast between trademark dramatic breaks and newly gentle passages. Primarily Spanish, with French-language opening fragments on En tus pupilas (`es|fr`), which are verified in the original lyrics. These fragments should not imply a substantial French-language album.

**Instruments:** label-derived Qobuz/AllMusic personnel identify keyboards, guitars, bass, drum kit, percussion, accordions (La Tortura), bandoneon, flutes, trumpet, trombone, flugelhorn and strings. Scope differs: accordion/bandoneon/horns are not on all tracks. **Production:** MusicBrainz and Qobuz detail programming and string arrangements, without a defensible specific hardware model.

**Producers:** Shakira; Lester Mendez, Gustavo Cerati and Luis F. Ochoa credited on select tracks; José 'Gocho' Torres as an actual *La Tortura* producer, not merely remixer. Rick Rubin repeatedly appears as **executive producer only** and must be excluded despite shorthand reviews describing him as producing the album. Alejandro Sanz is a guest, not a producer or musical influence. `sounds_like` notes the critic's Gwen Stefani/Julieta Venegas comparison and Eurythmics-type synth pop texture; none is a documented album-specific influence.

## 3. Oral Fixation, Vol. 2 (2005) — `shakira-oralfixationvol2`

**Edition caution:** the initial **11-song 2005 album** is the reference. The later 2006 version added Hips Don't Lie featuring Wyclef Jean. Avoid importing that 2006 addition as an album-wide rhythmic, instrument or producer identity; its credits are shown in expanded Qobuz releases but are edition-specific.

**Genre disagreements:** AllMusic's reviewer Stephen Thomas Erlewine sees serious **arena-scale pop-rock**, Euro-disco, rock & roll and Britpop elements, with neo-disco concentrated in politically pointed Timor; its retail taxonomy also includes Latin Pop, Pop, Dance-Pop and Adult Contemporary. Slant's Sal Cinquemani hears resemblance to Natalie Merchant/Jewel and No Doubt on some songs, but criticises parts of the radio-ready polish. This is **not a uniformly dancefloor album**. `neodisco` must be added as its own token: source literally says neo-disco; do not relabel as `nudisco`.

**Scales:** energy **3**, many strong choruses but several ballads; electronic **2**, prominent live guitars/drums/bass and some programming; mood **2**, critical accounts emphasise melancholy, religious/political questioning, though a few bright tracks intervene; theatrical **4**, dramatic arena-rock arrangements and opening choir; experimental **3**, surprising stylistic shifts within mainstream song forms; polish **4**, international studio finish; tempo **3**, variety of pulse; groove **2**, only some disco/dance cuts.

**Voice/languages:** Shakira's forceful and textured vocals lead, while gospel-like and choral ensembles supplement How Do You Do; `choral` is intentionally excluded from `voice` because the album's **lead** remains Shakira, not a choir. According to Slant, the original is predominantly English with brief French, Italian and Arabic; use `en|fr|it|ar`. These fragments are not co-equal languages.

**Instruments:** MusicBrainz and Qobuz list guitars, bass, drums, keyboards, percussion, strings/orchestra and horn parts. **Production:** explicit programming credits and string arrangements; no inferred synthesizer model or undocumented effects. **Producers:** Shakira, Lester Mendez, Gustavo Cerati, Tim Mitchell and Luis Fernando Ochoa are all credited as producers or co-producers across the initial songs. The Matrix is only marked pre-production in MusicBrainz despite alternative producer display in some sources; **excluded pending clear full-producer proof**. Rick Rubin is executive only; Carlos Santana is a guitar guest, not a producer.

**Influence/similarity:** Erlewine likens Illegal to Alanis Morissette and How Do You Do to Like a Prayer, while Slant compares some songwriting to Jewel, Natalie Merchant, and a No Doubt style on Hey You. These are **critical sonic references**, not artist testimony or independently verified musical inspirations; all are confined to `sounds_like`, and `influenced_by` stays blank. `Timor` shares political themes but the Peter Gabriel songwriting credit is NOT evidence of artistic influence.

## 4. She Wolf (2009) — `shakira-shewolf`

**Editional caution:** use the original 2009 album's recorded music and original language variants. Some US/digital **expanded editions** add Give It Up to Me with Timbaland / Lil Wayne, and other editions reprogramme the track sequence. Do **not** add Timbaland as an original-edition producer because of a bonus track. Spanish-language alternate takes are not new independent studio albums.

**Genres:** Erlewine identifies album-wide electro-disco, while Sal Cinquemani (Slant) calls out electropop, hi-NRG/Italo disco, dancehall rhythms, and a disco title track. Andy Gill (Independent) identifies the cumbia/dancehall pattern on Long Time; NME sees flamenco and post-punk riff touches. Guardian hears R&B on Why Wait, and Daily Nexus a new-wave flavour on Mon Amour. These are intentionally tagged **some tracks** or **texture** in the genre evidence CSV. The Latin pop token is based on AllMusic's broader album taxonomy rather than pretending the album sounds like the artist's previous Latin pop records.

**Scales:** energy **4**, club pulses dominate; electronic **4**, Shakira explicitly names synthesizers as primary sound sources, while reviews/credits document some acoustic strings, guitars, accordion and percussion; mood **4**, playful/sensual even when lyrics are angry; theatrical **3**, club-pop vocal character and some outsized hooks; experimental **3**, electronic stylistic experiments relative to her back catalogue; polish **4**, tightly produced pop; tempo **4**, consistently upbeat; groove **5**, AllMusic describes a cohesive dancefloor concept. This is not fully electronic = 5 because organic instruments remain.

**Instruments:** Shakira's 2009 interview explicitly says she used many synths on every track. Daily Nexus documents loud guitars, handclaps, accordion, synths; NME names Arabian strings and gongs; Independent details a clarinet break. Qobuz credits Miami Symphonic Strings on Why Wait. `gong` is a **new instrument token** and appears only on some music, not as an album-defining component.

**Production:** AllMusic explicitly credits digital editing, horn and string arrangements. Slant mentions 'robot effects' on vocals but does not identify a **specific** vocoder, Auto-Tune, talkbox or named piece of hardware: all are therefore absent. Do not fill `drummachine` because beats sound synthetic. `synthprogramming` would be an inference from synth presence and is not entered.

**Producers:** song-level credited Shakira, John Hill, The Neptunes (as a production act rather than automatically Pharrell alone), Wyclef Jean, Jerry Duplessis, Amanda Ghost, Lukas Burton, Future Cut. Qobuz expanded-edition credits and AllMusic names align for these tracks. Note John Hill is also composer on some songs, but his inclusion specifically relies on a producer credit. Hossam Ramzy's Arabic/percussion arrangement is NOT a producer credit.

**Influence:** Lucas Villa (Daily Nexus) explicitly calls Men in This Town **Crystal Castles-inspired**. Shakira described John Hill introducing her to electronic artists including Crystal Castles during album development (reported in 2009 press and consistent with the critic). That specific link qualifies for `influenced_by=Crystal Castles`; unlike an ordinary producer collaboration, this is an artist/critic-supported musical inspiration. Santigold and Britney Spears are **sonic editorial comparisons**, not documented influences.

**Reception disagreement:** AllMusic enthusiastically judges the focused electro-disco concept, Slant calls it musically uneven, and Andy Gill thinks some mainstream electronics are generic. Album values seek a sound description, not to average review scores. Robert Christgau discusses additional-track version changes, which are not imported into the original edition.

## Cross-album integrity

- `instruments` and `production` never come from genre assumptions; all appear in contemporary criticism, credits or artist statements, with track-level scope and caveats above.
- `sounds_like` is editorial. `influenced_by` is blank on three albums and narrowly supported on She Wolf.
- The `genres` sequence orders characteristic album genres first, localized song styles and textures later.
- Rick Rubin is an executive producer only; Emilio Estefan's involvement on Laundry Service does not prove full album producer status.
- Neither MTV Unplugged nor any greatest-hits collection is a descriptor row, regardless of commercial/award importance.
- No content from Phase 01 has been recopied into the Phase 02 rows file.
