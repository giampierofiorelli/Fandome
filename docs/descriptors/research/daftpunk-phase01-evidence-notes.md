# Daft Punk — Phase 01 evidence and decision notes

Evidence standards: album-specific direct interviews and original label metadata outrank inferred sound; critical review supports editorial scales. Inherited remixed equipment is not presumed. Full URLs, types, strength and 1:1 genre provenance live in the CSVs.

## Homework (1997) — `daftpunk-homework`

**Core source disagreement.** Sean Cooper at AllMusic calls it a compelling funk-house record. Larry Fitzmaurice's 2018 Pitchfork retrospective stresses its intentionally raw and sometimes brutalist house groove rather than generic late-1990s electronica; Apple Music draws attention to filtered disco and acid techno in specific tracks. Accordingly the row leads with French touch/house, then Chicago house/techno, with `gfunk` last because Pitchfork's comparison concerns a small number of tracks, particularly `Da Funk`. `electronica` is documented as the broader AllMusic tag, not a claim that every track is downtempo or big beat. The more specific `chicagohouse` requires catalogue expansion.

**Instruments, machines and process.** The 1999 Japanese artist-interview equipment list reprinted by MusicTech and independently discussed by MusicRadar enumerates Roland TR-707/808/909, TB-303, Juno-106, LinnDrum, E-mu SP-1200, Roland S-760, sequencers, analog synthesizers and Alesis 3630. The documented DAT path is mixer/compressor to DAT, followed by edits on an S-760 sampler and DAT output. `sampling|datrecording|tr909|tr808|tr707|tb303|juno106|linndrum|sp1200|rolands760|alesis3630` reflect *this album's* session/production gear, **not** proof that a particular named machine played a specific lead sound. Bangalter himself described an analog synth-bass and a talk box in `Around the World` (Wax Poetics), supporting `synthbass` and `talkbox` in the appropriate columns. A separate online re-creation may call an effect a Korg MS-20, but the verified original equipment list does **not** include one. Avoid that attribution.

**Scales** (editorial, guide anchors): energy 4 for club dynamics offset by atmospheric pauses (`Fresh`); electronic 5 from original equipment list; mood 3 because euphoria and abrasion coexist; theatrical 1 because understated/improvised vocal staging; experimental 3 for aggressive loops, distortion and formal endurance despite conventional house meter; polish 2 because of lean home recording; tempo 4 for sustained up-tempo club programming; groove 5 because extended DJ-oriented dance tracks dominate. Do not assign theoretical BPM values.

**Voice and themes.** Instrumentals predominate; `Teachers` delivers a spoken/named roll call; `Around the World` uses repetitively processed vocals. `social` is supported by Pitchfork's interpretation of `Revolution 909` as commentary on police disruption of dance culture, while `identity` covers the music's explicit lineage testimony and `party` its club-centered text. The list in `influenced_by` is drawn from the self-identification of formative artists in `Teachers`, corroborated by Apple Music and Pitchfork, not an arbitrary list of stylistic lookalikes. `sounds_like` is a separate editorial comparison.

**Producer rule.** MusicBrainz release/track-credit data identifies both Daft Punk members; no extra sound engineer was elevated to producer. Defining album -> landmark 1; documentary evidence high, notwithstanding album-genre scope ambiguity.

## Discovery (2001) — `daftpunk-discovery`

**Genre order.** GRAMMY's anniversary article describes a bright, maximal pop transformation of house, bringing '80s pop, funk and rock. AllMusic's artist history explicitly calls out disco, synth-pop, house, garage and R&B. The 2001 *Mix* interview discusses rock, disco and jazz/R&B passages; Pitchfork's contemporary review was lukewarm about the sentimental vocal tracks while later accounts regard the sonic synthesis as transformative. `softrock`, `ambient`, `downtempo`, `garage`, `rnb` and `rock` in the CSV are limited to documented subsets/texture, not falsely treated as uniform album-wide styles. Soft-rock references the original interview/reviews on the ballads and is not equated to a heavy guitar record.

**Instruments.** In the original *Mix* 2001 interview, de Homem-Christo describes playing guitars, vintage keyboards and bass, recording his own parts and resampling them; the interview names Fender Stratocaster, Wurlitzer electric piano (especially `Digital Love`), Roland and Moog synths and samplers. GRAMMY corroborates these instruments and drum machines in their actual recording sessions. `electricpiano` maps to Wurlitzer; it is not a generic assumption from the album's genre. `vocoder` also appears as a pitched vocal/instrument in credits. A separate contemporary *Remix* interview quoted in Equipboard specifies Roland SVC-350, Auto-Tune and DigiTech vocal effects. `vocoder`, `autotune`, `harmonizer` describe identifiable different methods and must not be conflated. `sampling` means both outside source material and recursive sampling of their own performances; `distortion` stems from the artists' original Mix remarks. The listed TR-808, TR-909, MPC, Juno-106, Roland S-760 and SP-1200 are supported by the documented Discovery-specific studio inventory, not merely borrowed from Homework's gear.

**Disagreement and uncertainty.** There is disagreement about the exact count of external samples. The original 2001 interview says four and later research uncovered further sources, including retrospectively cleared material. The row asserts a general `sampling` technique without asserting that all instrumental timbres came from records. `Digital Love` evokes Supertramp in the duo's direct comments; they explicitly said its bridge was made with a Wurlitzer, **not** a sample of Supertramp. Track writing/production credits are distinct: DJ Sneak is a credited co-writer of `Digital Love`, not a producer, while Romanthony and Todd Edwards have production credits on `Too Long` and `Face to Face`, respectively, confirmed by Qobuz, MusicBrainz and the original Virgin CD-credit transcription. That justifies the producer list, not a blanket extra-producer claim for all songs.

**Documented influences.** The Independent reproduces a contemporary Daft Punk statement naming Jimi Hendrix, AC/DC, John Williams, Stevie Wonder, Dr Dre, Basement Jaxx, The Who, the Beatles, Herbie Hancock and Supertramp as inspiring the sonic exploration. Contemporary remarks additionally name the Buggles (and the 2021 reviewer hears them). These belong in `influenced_by`, not as contributors or sampled performers.

**Scales**: energy 4 because tracks alternate club anthems and slower interludes; electronic 4 because guitars/electric piano/bass are heavily used and processed rather than purely synthetic; mood 4 for euphoric tracks set against melancholy slow songs; theatrical 3 for overtly stylised robot vocals; experimental 3 for collage and manipulated timbre within pop structure; polish 5 for tightly detailed effects/arrangement; tempo 3 due to variety; groove 4 because danceable majority but intentional ballads and ambient transitions. Vocals are melodic/soft and accompanied by instrumentals; distorted/synthetic vocal timbre is captured in production, not by inventing a voice token. Themes: joy, romantic love, nostalgia, tenderness; English lyrics occur on vocal tracks.

## Daft Club (2003) — `daftpunk-daftclub`

**Why eligible.** The band's official site explicitly calls this a remix album and documents work by The Neptunes, Cosmo Vitelli, Basement Jaxx, Boris Dlugosch, Jess & Crabbe, Demon, Laidback Luke, Slum Village, Gonzales and Romanthony. This is a documented collaborative remix project, despite not being a defining original *Daft Punk studio* album (landmark 0). Most material comes from Discovery with one Homework remix (`Phoenix`); the other tracks include material outside conventional remix classification.

**Genre and scale rationale.** AllMusic labels the album house, progressive/left-field house and electronica; reviewer John Bush observes Slum Village transforming `Aerodynamic` into a hip-hop track, and the Neptunes' `Harder...` into a lounge-like reconstruction. We assign hip-hop and lounge narrow scope. Critic Bush values some remixes while disliking others; therefore neither perfection nor the original Discovery's bright emotional score should be inherited. Energy 3, electronic 4, mood 3, theatrical 2, experimental 3, polish 4, tempo 3, groove 4 reflect mixed rearrangements; confidence is medium because track-level construction, instruments and vocal effects are heterogenous.

**Instruments and production restrictions.** `sampler|sampling` is supported by John Bush's explicit discussion of Slum Village's sampled version and original remix credits; no synthesiser make/model has been verified *for the mixes in this compilation*. The few surviving original Discovery vocals can be heard in the remixes, but we deliberately do not assign the original album's full sampler/vocoder/studio equipment or gear tokens to a new set of mixers without proof. `rap` and `clear` reflect Slum Village and Romanthony tracks; `instrumental` represents many tracks. Original tracks/versions are not automatically all 'instrumental'.

**Producer distinction.** Daft Punk members are producers of original source tracks; Todd Edwards is specifically credited as producer of `Face to Face` material, Romanthony of `Too Long`; the original LP credits *Gonzales* as producer of his own `Too Long (Gonzales Version)`. No extra record was co-billed to these collaborators. The Neptunes, Basement Jaxx and Slum Village are explicitly labelled **remixer** in authoritative credits, so their remix roles are stated in this research file but not silently converted into `producers`. This limitation is material because a later Claude import can choose to supplement newly verified remix-producer credits.

**Influences** left empty: collaborations do not imply that remixers influenced the original album. `sounds_like` left empty because some remixes genuinely *are* by the named acts. Original tracks' lyrics retained where audible, mainly love/party/selfhood, but variation warrants caution.

## Human After All (2005) — `daftpunk-humanafterall`

**Different compositional premise.** The 2005 record was intentionally stripped-back, built rapidly across approximately six weeks. Le Monde records two guitars, two drum machines, vocoder and limited keyboards/samples. This supports `electricguitar|drummachine|keyboards|vocoder|sampler` with guitar up front; electronic 4 rather than 5 because guitars are core instruments. Pitchfork's contemporary Mark Pytlik review finds little of Discovery's joy and interprets it as cynical; the later French-history account notes an austere, confrontational sound. AllMusic's review provides house/progressive-house framing. A 2005 review archived by Denmark's bibliotek.dk uses electro language and points to almost-metal digital guitar contours on The Brainwasher. We classify electronic and rock separately rather than inventing an unsupported combined genre; the Ableton artist profile names a disco-funk component.

**Production.** The guitar/synth distortion and vocoder are repeatedly reported. `sampling` reflects the handful of samples (not the sample-collage language of Discovery): the album's Robot Rock uses an existing funk recording. Claims about an eight-track machine and a DigiTech synth-wah circulate in secondary accounts, but neither is securely established from album-specific original equipment documentation, so both are **omitted** from the row. An Ableton artist interview links Live to the duo's 2005 workflow, supporting `ableton` without asserting an exact version or sound signature. Gildas Loaëc and Cédric Hervet have production coordination (not necessarily producer) credits; Qobuz names only the duo as music producers. Keep writer, engineer, coordinator, producer roles separate.

**Scales.** Energy 4 for relentless riffs and programmed drums; electronic 4 for hybrid electric guitars/electronic sequencing; mood 1 for anxious/paranoid lyric premise and foreboding tones; theatrical 2 for robotic chant but small arrangements; experimental 2 for surprising severity yet repetition of minimal patterns; polish 2 for rougher textures than Discovery (intentional rather than amateur); tempo 3 due to ranges from more vigorous club cuts to Make Love/Emotion; groove 4 for persistent 4/4 pulse. `spoken|instrumental` reflects mechanically intoned slogans and otherwise instrumental focus. Lyrical themes social/identity/introspection from technology and fear; avoid forcing love on an album because of the *Make Love* song title.

**Critical disagreement.** The Pitchfork critic's severe score and AllMusic's relatively mixed assessment do not settle the `landmark` indicator; the album is a defining sharp sonic pivot and became foundational material for their transformative 2006–07 live arrangements. It is important as an artist-era document, even if not universally critically praised. Influences left blank: names like Kraftwerk and Suicide are **editorial sonic comparisons** unless an album-specific primary statement says they directly shaped these sessions.

## Across the phase: methodological reminders

- Scores 0–5 are researcher editorial judgements calibrated to the published guide, not extracted numeric critic ratings.
- All production devices in CSV are from album-specific direct accounts or track provenance; speculative `digitechsynthwah`/`eighttrack` have been deliberately excluded.
- Do not infer `protools`/`ableton` for 1990s recordings. A 'vocoder-like' robot voice is not a proof of a particular device; the evidence explicitly identifies Auto-Tune, a Roland SVC-350, talk-box and other processing **in different settings**.
- Genres written as compound words use Fandome controlled tokens or new tokens listed in CLAUDE_IMPORT. A narrow track-only genre is deliberately last or marked track scope.
- Some named artists in `sounds_like` are sonic resemblance judgments, while `influenced_by` names must have documentary evidence; for Homework that is the explicit `Teachers` roll-call, and for Discovery a 2001 Daft Punk account.
