## Bella ’mbriana (1982) — `pinodaniele-bellambriana`

**Genre hierarchy:** OndaRock (D’Amato) and Lotari's DeBaser album review agree on blues, jazz-rock, funk, an internationally oriented approach, and traditional Neapolitan writing. OndaRock also identifies `soul` specifically on *Ma che mania*; `tarumbo` and `tarantella` attach principally to *Tarumbò* and therefore rank at the tail. AllMusic's album style is Italian Pop. The album title is Neapolitan folklore, not a literal folk recording: `neapolitansong` is the pre-existing matching token. `jazzrock` is justified as a whole-album idiom, whereas `soul` is a single-track texture. `worldmusic` is independently named by the DeBaser reviewer and echoed as a new international direction by OndaRock.

**Instruments:** Official credits explicitly list Daniele (electric and acoustic guitars), De Piscopo (drums and percussion), Johnson (bass), Jermano (percussion), Shorter (sax) and Amoruso (piano, keyboards). OndaRock names a flute-like synthesizer texture on *Annarè*; that is `synth`, **not** a real `flute` credit. Official credits say Amoruso plays *harmonica*, a detailed pressing-derived transcription says *melodica*, and these differ materially; neither has been asserted in the row pending original sleeve checking. Shorter's sax and Johnson's bass are *guest performances*, not influence links. OndaRock also describes substantial treatments and polished arrangements but supplies no specific processor model, so do not invent effects.

**Production and credits conflict:** Official album site says *computer programming* by **Aldo Bassi**. Vinyl Story (transcribing the 1982 LP) says **Aldo Banfi**, specifically **Synclavier II**. Later digital metadata elsewhere has also appeared under another programmer. The album itself documents programming, and a pressing-derived source names Synclavier II, so `programming|synclavier` records the process/device, but the disputed operator/model attribution must be verified by Claude against a first-press photograph. The site directly credits **Pino Daniele and Willy David** as producers; Allan Goldberg is sound/mix engineer and is not added as producer.

**Scales:** Energy 3 and tempo 3 reflect *Mo basta* and the danceable title-track against the slow *Maggio se ne va* and *E po' che fà*; mood 2 derives from the dark/crepuscular vein explicitly described by two independent critics; groove 3 because funky songs coexist with slow ballads. Electronic 2 means a guitar-led group with additional synth/programming, not an electronic album. Experimental 3 for jazz/blues/folk crossovers and new synthesis, polish 4 for the critic's notably processed, precise Carimate recording; theatrical 2 for dynamic delivery not staged dramatics. No defensible lead `voice` category beyond general expressive descriptions.

**Sound comparisons:** OndaRock says *Mo basta* evokes Talking Heads' **The Great Curve** from *Remain in Light*, a one-track/11 comparison. OndaRock also likens the sung/guitar unison in *Io vivo come te* to **George Benson**, similarly one of 11. Neither is an artist-declared musical influence. Other names in the scene history are not automatically linked.

**Lyrics / landmark:** Neapolitan household folklore and local identity, *Tutta 'nata storia* (American model and social identity), *Tarumbò* (satire of church hierarchy), *Maggio se ne va* (quest for God), and intimate love songs. OndaRock explicitly describes the album as closing Daniele's defining early five-album golden cycle and expanding his recorded sound into an international idiom: `landmark=1` on independent career-defining grounds, not because of high ratings.

## Musicante (1984) — `pinodaniele-musicante`

**Genre disagreements:** Carlo Pasceri's detailed song-by-song examination specifically identifies the album as **world music** with mainly acoustic Mediterranean tonality and says strong jazz/funk characteristics of the predecessor and blues are largely *absent* on Musicante. D'Amato (OndaRock) independently describes a pivot toward African/Latin-world textures and traditional Neapolitan songs, but calls lead single *Keep On Movin'* funky-dance, *Disperazione* classic blues and *Acchiappa acchiappa* fusion (the latter also named by Pasceri). Hence `funk`, `jazzfusion` and `blues` are **late, song-level** tags despite an independent contradictory whole-record judgment. AllMusic supports Italian Pop. `folk` describes the Neapolitan acoustic idiom and traditional battente sound, not an unsupported pan-genre assumption.

**Instruments:** Daniele's official credits: guitar(s), mandoloncello, *electric chitarra battente*; Johnson bass, Zurzolo double bass, Marangolo drums, Vasconcelos percussion, Faraldo's **tammorra**, Amoruso piano/keyboards, Collins alto sax. The 1984 pressing transcription and Pasceri also identify Amoruso's synth on *Acchiappa acchiappa*, a mechanical **carillon/music box** on *Lazzari felici*, and classical acoustic battente on many tracks. `chitarrabattente` (10-string double-course southern Italian instrument) and `tammorra` (regional frame drum) need new instrument tokens; `musicbox` is the already existing acceptable token for carillon. Do not turn the guest saxophonist Mel Collins, Vasconcelos or the biography's Don Cherry into `influenced_by` values; the original album's credited instrumental list does **not** contain Don Cherry/trumpet.

**Production:** Original-sleeve-derived sources specify *computer programming Fairlight* (Dario Massari) and *computer programming PPG* (Roberto Masala). `fairlight|programming` are therefore supported. **PPG is a manufacturer reference with no exact machine model stated**, and the allowed `ppgwave` token represents a *specific* model: do **not** infer or enter `ppgwave` from an unspecified PPG credit. Pasceri describes recorded/self-overdubbed Pino harmony voices on multiple tracks: `layeredvocals|overdubbing`. Producer credits are **Pino Daniele and Willy David**; keyboard arranger Joe Amoruso, computer programmers and engineers are not producers. Pasceri specifically says no electric piano is used, so `electricpiano` is excluded even though it appears on earlier Daniele albums.

**Scales / voice:** Energy 3 and groove 3 balance opening syncopated, percussive pieces against the restrained *Stella nera*, *Lassa che vene* and *Lazzari felici*. Tempo 3 mixed; mood 3 because Pasceri reports considerably brighter sonic light than *Bella 'mbriana* while still singling out melancholy. Electronic 2 for credited Fairlight/PPG and synth within a predominantly acoustic band, polish 3 for the spacious arrangement, experimental 3 for new rhythmic grammar/battente and sampled-era hardware, theatrical 1 because recorded delivery is close and restrained. `raspy` has specific support in OndaRock's description of the lead song's scratchy voice, although this is not asserted as universal on every track.

**Links / landmark:** OndaRock's *Just In Mi* compares its wordless vocalizing sound with **Lucio Dalla**, a [1/10] sonic comparison. Paul Simon's *Graceland* is mentioned retrospectively as later world-music history, **not** an album inspiration or sonic comparison. The artist expressly credits tours, Neapolitan history and African percussion idioms; none names a non-guest specific *artist* as album influence. OndaRock independently describes *Musicante* as opening a distinct world-oriented sonic direction and Pasceri calls it unique even within the subsequent albums. `landmark=1` means the *sound changed*, not that reviewers praised it.

## Ferryboat (1985) — `pinodaniele-ferryboat`

**Genres and atmosphere:** The official 1985 retrospective says this record steers more decisively toward **jazz** and **Latin** sounds, away from reliance on jazz fusion. OndaRock details electronic keyboard arrangements and ensemble horn textures, with tracks ranging from mechanically accented dance-rock to tender ballads. Independent DeBaser review specifically names Mediterranean rock on *Bona jurnata* and Latin-inflected blues on *'A rrobba mia*. `mediterraneanrock` is **already in the project's list**; it is track-specific. OndaRock names `softrock` only for *Che ore so'*, so it ranks last. AllMusic's `italianpop` remains an album-style genre. `pop` describes the whole album's commercial pop direction in OndaRock. Do not assign `jazzfusion` by band-member résumés when the retrospective expressly says the direction is toward jazz rather than fusion.

**Personnel:** The artist credits Pino guitar/voice, Gadd drums, Cinelu percussion, Zurzolo bass and double bass, Richard Tee acoustic piano and “Fender” (ambiguous model), Vitolo keyboards, Lara trumpet, Torres trombone, Nocella/Barbieri/Marco Zurzolo sax, Potter congas, and an orchestra of flute/oboe/strings on *Quaccosa*. OndaRock identifies synths on *Bona Jurnata* and an electric guitar passage on *Sarà*, warranting `synth|electricguitar`. The bare “Fender” credit is **not** confidently a specific Rhodes model; leave `electricpiano` out pending sleeve/model clarification. `strings` and individual strings/woodwinds are present mainly on one final short song, not album-wide orchestration.

**Production credit ambiguity:** Official page prints **“Produced and arranged by Pino Daniele”** and separately **“Recordings produced by: Luciano Torani, Rino Zurzolo and Pino Daniele.”** Because the user explicitly requires *all credited producers*, the draft includes all three in `producers`, while clearly flagging Torani/Zurzolo as *recording-production* credits to inspect before final import. Do not add the explicitly credited string/wind arrangers unless they are credited as producers. `hornarrangements` follows the explicit wind-arrangement credit for Torres and Daniele; `stringarrangements` follows Paolo Raffone's scored strings on *Quaccosa*. Daniele says the *Quaccosa* musicians performed live for the recording and his draft vocal take was used: `liverecording` applies to that track, not to a live-concert album. No evidence for particular synthesizer models or digital multitrack, and none are inserted.

**Scales and voice:** Energy 3/groove 3/tempo 3 for brisk Latin/jazz/electronic numbers offset by many unhurried love ballads; mood 3 for joyful coastal imagery with longing and social frustration; experimental 3 for electro-acoustic and Latin hybrid; electronic 3 from OndaRock's stronger electronic component alongside Gadd and orchestrated tracks; polish 4 for elaborate Bagaria studio multimusician production and precisely written horns/strings, theatrical 2 for larger choruses without theatrical staging. The critic mentions processed voices in *Ferryboat* but no specific technique: neither `vocoder` nor `vocalfilter` is asserted. The sources do not warrant a definite album-wide lead voice tag.

**Sounds-like:** OndaRock compares mechanical synth atmosphere of *Bona Jurnata* to **Public Image Ltd.'s Rise**, subsequently on *Album* (1986); the comparison is retrospective, **not** an influence by the 1985 artist, and is encoded [1/10]. The same critic describes the soft-rock *Che ore so'* as “à la Paul Young,” another [1/10] comparison. The guests **Gato Barbieri/Steve Gadd/Mino Cinelu** are never similarities merely by appearing on the credit list.

**Landmark:** The album is described as a major change of direction and ambitious recording, but OndaRock does not independently demonstrate that it defined an enduring genre, sound or the musician's **whole career** rather than this one phase. Consequently `landmark=0` despite its large production and critical praise.

## Bonne soirée (1987) — `pinodaniele-bonnesoiree`

**Genres:** Daniele himself and his official retrospective expressly state that his Paris tours sparked **Arab rock** and world-music practice; the style name becomes the new proposed token `arabrock`, with `worldmusic` separately named, not substituted. Independent OndaRock also calls the LP a sonic break. DeBaser tags the whole release as `rock|blues|worldmusic`; AllMusic labels its album genre Italian Pop. Track-level OndaRock descriptions specifically name `synthfunk` (*Guardami in Face*), `salsa` (*Baccalà*), `dub` (*Scrack*), `jazz` (*Boys in the Night*, described as speed-jazz) and `ethnometal` (*Mama, E!*). They are all deliberately in the long tail, **not** claims about the album's entire sound. `arabrock` and `ethnometal` are new-token requests. The critic's metaphor of “speed-jazz” is normalized to existing `jazz` because the phrase describes fast jazz playing rather than a separately documented genre; no new model or named subgenre is inferred.

**Personnel and production:** Official credits list Daniele guitar/voice, Palladino bass, Marotta drums, Cinelu percussion, Illiano acoustic piano/keyboards, Collins sax, Nocella sax solo on *Boys in the Night*. OndaRock hears an abstract synthesizer-funk sound on *Guardami in Face*, so generic `synth` is documented without a specific machine model. Producer credits **Pino Daniele and Allan Goldberg**; Emmanuel Guiot is the mixer, Massimo Fregnani the executive producer, neither belongs in `producers`. Recorded in Formia/Carimate, mixed in Paris; **no exact documented effects/hardware/recording technique from allowed tokens**, so the `production` cell is deliberately blank. Do not assume digital recording from the production era or electronics from the genre.

**Languages / versions:** Italian, Neapolitan and English are substantial. *Baccalà* contains brief Spanish phrases and the title track repeats a French greeting, but there are no substantiated full Spanish/French sung verses; do not promote incidental phrases to main `es` or `fr` languages. The **original vinyl album has 10 tracks** and CD/cassette 11 including *Watch Out*, per the official 1987 retrospective. The row describes the 11-track widely available edition and reflects the 1987 album's sonic repertoire; additional-track styles/lyrics should be considered edition-dependent. This does not change the 1987 album identity or add a duplicate row.

**Scales / landmark:** Energy 3, groove 3, tempo 3 reflect rhythm-oriented *Guardami in Face*, *Scrack* and *Boys in the Night* balanced by love songs. Mood 3: anti-war/drug concerns and introspection offset more playful syncopated moments. Electronic 3: synth-funk and conspicuous keyboards inside an otherwise live rhythm-section sound. Experimental 4 for the evidence-backed shift into Arab rock with Latin, dub and synth-funk crossings; polish 4 for layered studio arrangement and multi-studio mixing, **not** uncredited effects. Theatrical 2. Neither a definitive lead-voice style nor track-independent sonic artist comparison is established. `landmark=1` is supported by **independent** OndaRock characterizing a true break with earlier work, and by independent DeBaser discussion of its new musical direction; **not** by Daniele's own promotional claim or an enthusiastic “masterpiece” label alone.

**Future work and exclusions:** *Sciò Live* (1984) is excluded as live, regardless of stature. No greatest hits/compilations are described. *Le vie del Signore sono finite* (film 1987, soundtrack released 1988) could require a distinct **soundtrack** album in later research if a full album release and original primary billing are confirmed; it is **not** inserted into this four-album ZIP. It may change which releases are chronologically next, so the next-phase selection below is provisional pending that audit. In the phase-01 master catalogue-audit, *Sotto 'o sole* is listed as 1991 but the independent OndaRock discography and AllMusic give **1992**; flag this to Claude for release-edition reconciliation, without silently changing any previous rows.
