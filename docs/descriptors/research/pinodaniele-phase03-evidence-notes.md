## `pinodaniele-leviedelsignoresonofinite` — Le vie del Signore sono finite (1988)

- **Inclusion and type:** An actual separately published 1988 Bagaria 6-track 33 rpm mini-LP (catalogue 50 7900981); film premiered in 1987. Official site files it under *Soundtracks*; independent SoundtrackCollector confirms a physical 1988 LP. It is not a retrospective compilation, despite one online retailer displaying an automated “Compilation” flag. It matters as a documented, released score to the Massimo Troisi film and gets `type=soundtrack`. Six tracks run about 9½ to 10 minutes. *Schizzechea* is another 1988 release and follows the mini-LP in both contemporary discographic sequence and its own contemporary descriptions. Official: https://www.pinodaniele.com/music/le-vie-del-signore-sono-finite-1988/; archival film/LP facts: https://www.pinodaniele.com/miscellaneous/1987-2/, https://www.soundtrackcollector.com/title/63501/Vie%2BDel%2BSignore%2BSono%2BFinite%2C%2BLe.
- **Genres (whole vs track-specific):** `soundtrack` is contextual only and is accompanied by sonic descriptors. DeBaser identifies the main **ballad** “Qualcosa arriverà” (also reprised instrumentally), four other very short acoustic cues and a **waltz** on “Chez moi.” Alberto Moneti's musician account describes the **lead song** as a mixture of pop, blues and jazz; those three genres apply **only to the main sung track**, and are intentionally low-weight in the ordered genre string. These genres must NOT be projected onto the whole largely instrumental score. https://www.debaser.it/pino-daniele/le-vie-del-signore-sono-finite/recensione; https://www.accordiespartiti.it/accordi/italiani/daniele-pino/qualcosa-arrivera/.
- **Instrumentation/production:** Original official credit: Daniele guitars/vocal, Kevin McAlea keyboards, Romano Bais “computer”. DeBaser directly hears classical/acoustic guitar on “Tarantarabe” and in instrumental reprise. `acousticguitar|keyboards` only. Bais' bare computer credit does **not** establish a specific synthesizer, sampler, drum machine or production technique; `production` is left **empty**. DeBaser hears an unnamed wind instrument on the instrumental reprise; its woodwind/brass family is not specified, therefore not inserted. Moneti's generic song-account instrumentation conflicts with the specific official miniature-score personnel; do not add bass, drums or saxophones.
- **Producer unknown:** A credited soundtrack *composer* is not automatically a credited album *producer*. No album producer can be confirmed from the opened non-Wikipedia sources, so `producers` is **blank**. Film producers are completely unrelated to the record's producer field.
- **Scales:** energy 1, tempo 1, groove 1 from mainly short slow, intimate cues with only Tarantarabe more rhythmical; mood 2 for wistful/hopeful song and tender instrumentals; theatrical 1, experimental 1 for understated film vignettes, not an experimental concept; electronic 1 because named computer and keyboards occupy a predominantly acoustic score, but model/effect is unknown; polish 3 from recorded, composed film cues, with limited mix detail. `voice=instrumental` reflects five of six pieces. One Neapolitan vocal song gives `language=nap`; instrumental cues have no language. `lyrics=love|introspection|storytelling` applies only to that main song (one of six); don't imply five sets of lyrics.
- **Comparisons/influences/landmark:** The DeBaser comparison with Daniele's own future ballads is a self-comparison, excluded from `sounds_like`. Massimo Troisi is a collaborator/filmmaker, not a musical influence. Film premise is not musician influence. Neither “best song” praise nor this important film collaboration makes the *album* independently demonstrable as genre/career-defining: landmark 0. Confidence medium due sparse production/genre evidence.

## `pinodaniele-schizzecheawithlove` — Schizzechea With Love (1988)

- **Genres:** AllMusic tags whole album `italianpop`; reviewer DeBaser describes melodie italo-napoletane and blues/funk, while original/reissue disc metadata attributes Jazz, Blues, Funk/Soul, **Jazz-Funk**, **Latin Jazz**. OndaRock explicitly calls it a return to cantautorato and more accessible pop. Track-level extras in low-weight tail: `latin` (Cumbà), `newwave` (Cry), `disco` (Tell Me Now funky/disco hybrid per DeBaser), `soulrock` (Al Capone, exact OndaRock description), a *new token*. `soulrock` is specific to one track and is placed after the full-album styles. OndaRock hears **less** Arab influence than on *Bonne soirée*; do not repeat phase-02 `arabrock` as if the whole new LP were Arab rock merely because a website's artist-wide biography says the idiom continues.
- **Instruments:** Official sleeve transcription on artist site lists Daniele guitar/voice, Steve Gadd and Agostino Marangolo drums, Danny Cummings percussion, Chris White sax, Jeremy Meek bass, “Charlie” Intrieri and Danilo Rea piano, Kevin McAlea and Bruno Illiano keyboards, Romano Bais programming, Linda Wesley backing vocals. DeBaser/OndaRock identify acoustic/classical guitars, effected/electric guitar and acoustic string parts in “Gesù Gesù” / “Schizzechea”, so include `guitar|acousticguitar|electricguitar|bass|drums|percussion|saxophone|piano|keyboards|strings`. Daniele himself says he began using samplers *on this record*; `sampler` is supported, not extrapolated from 1980s pop. Do not insert synthesizer models absent from original credits.
- **Production:** `sampling` and `sampler` are directly warranted by his first-person recollection; Bais is credited computer programmer, so `programming`. DeBaser hears effected and chorus-treated guitar on “Tell Me Now” and “Cry” (`processedguitar`). This is the first record Daniele says he mixed abroad, at Advision London. Recording was Bagaria Formia. Neither those place names nor backing singers alone support `digitalrecording`, `choirvocals`, `layeredvocals` or an uncredited reverb unit.
- **Producers:** The official site distinguishes “Produced and arranged by Pino Daniele” from “Recording produced: Greg Jackman.” Per the phase-02 convention of including named *recording producers* alongside general producers, include `Pino Daniele|Gregg Jackman`, flagging that the page uses “Greg” in that credit while artist note says “Gregg”. Gregg Jackman is a named recording producer, not included merely for mixing. Exclude executive Massimo Fragnani. Claude should adjudicate the recording-production distinction consistently across phases.
- **Voice/lyrics/languages:** DeBaser describes strong, sometimes forceful vocal on Tell Me Now and contrast calm/angry performance on Gesù Gesù, `powerful|soft`. Song texts and critic: love, heartbreak, addiction, anticolonial Cuba, anti-Camorra, spiritual/Jesus, introspection and irony. Italian/Neapolitan/English words have substantial sung presence; `nap|it|en`. `energy=3`, `tempo=3`, `groove=3` for alternating ballads and funky/Latin numbers, `mood=2` from addiction/anguish with some buoyant groove, `theatrical=2`, `experimental=3` for samplers/new-wave and crossovers, `electronic=2` for sampled/computer textures but a live band backbone, `polish=4` for Formia/London multitrack multi-instrument studio production. **No album-level sonic peer comparison was actually supplied by critics**; leave `sounds_like` blank. No evidenced album-specific non-guest musician influence, leave `influenced_by` blank.
- **Landmark=0:** Independent commentators call it closing the Formia cycle and paving the way to more pop-friendly writing, but neither establishes *this album alone* as definitively changing/defining the artist/genre. Technical first sampling claim comes from the artist, and artist judgments do not suffice for the landmark flag. Source disagreement: DeBaser describes the album warmly, OndaRock considers it stylistically weaker; do not translate taste into landmark.

## `pinodaniele-mascalzonelatino` — Mascalzone latino (1989)

- **Genres:** AllMusic `italianpop`, critical coverage specifies pop shift and classical-guitar Neapolitan melody. Ballads “Anna verrà”, “Ammore scumbinato”, “Carte e cartuscelle” represent several songs. OndaRock names **flamenco-gypsy** embellishment specifically on “Giungla”, “A speranza è semp' sola”, “Aria strana”; lower-weight `flamenco`, not predominant. Review labels it within cantautori repertoire, `canzonedautore`. The opened 1989 Bagaria original LP seller listing names **Blues, Rhythm & Blues, Soul and Funk**, as does the opened 1994 CGD reissue for R&B/Funk/Soul. These contribute `rnb|soul|funk|blues` as record-style genre labels, not guessed from groove. Their priority is lower than the independent song-level melodic descriptions. No dedicated samba genre merely because one song is titled “Sambaccussì”.
- **Instruments/production:** The artist's own personnel: Daniele guitar, Illiano keyboards, Rosario Iermano percussion, Clive Mayuyu drums; DeBaser specifically identifies **classical acoustic guitar** as effectively replacing electric guitar and an electric-bass part on Anna verrà, so `acousticguitar|keyboards|percussion|drums|bass`. Do not add electric guitar merely because Daniele generally plays it. His artist note says he made the album at home, aiming to merge acoustic/electronic. `programming` is explicitly credited to Bruno Illiano; **no model-specific machine**. Electronics justify `electronic=3`; they do not justify a `synth`, drum machine, `sampling`, `homerecording`, or named software token without specific credit. Only `Pino Daniele` is named producer; Bruno Illiano's co-creative role does **not** turn his keyboard/programming credit into co-production.
- **Languages/lyrics:** Mostly Neapolitan with important Italian “Anna verrà”; `nap|it`. The singer's first-person attribution that “Anna verrà” was his first Italian-language song is about a notable *album track*, not proof that the whole album is in Italian. Themes: love, distance/longing, Naples/local identity, migration, social aggression, San Gennaro spirituality, introspection. `powerful` vocal from DeBaser's explicit description of high-dynamic often forceful delivery (not necessarily every song).
- **Scales:** energy 3 and groove 3 for rhythmic Faccia gialla/A speranza/Giungla contrasting with romantic pieces; tempo 3, mood 3 for idealistic/romantic and combative contexts, theatrical 2 and experimental 2 for accessible songs with flamenco and acoustic-electronic interplay, polish 3 for controlled home/demostudio production (not automatically lo-fi), electronic 3 for Illiano's programmed keyboards with an otherwise acoustic lead. Neither electronic instruments' exact nature nor overdubs can be proved.
- **Comparison/landmark:** Comparisons in reviews to Daniele's earlier *own* soundtrack are explicitly **self-references** and excluded. Anna Magnani is dedication/non-musical inspiration, not `influenced_by`. Orixàs are guest artists; never add as influences or comparisons. Reviewer praises technical guitar but OndaRock faults repetitive material. `landmark=0` under independent genre/career-transforming threshold.

## `pinodaniele-unuomoinblues` — Un uomo in blues (1991)

- **Genres:** OndaRock names **electric pop-blues** on the album's signature hit “‘O scarrafone”; keep distinct new token `popblues`, accompanied by broad `blues` and `pop`. AllMusic calls the LP Italian Pop. 1991 album release-style metadata additionally lists **Jazz-Funk**, **Soul-Jazz**, **Funk/Soul** and jazz; include `jazzfunk|souljazz|funk` but do not infer album-wide fusion solely from session players. Critics explicitly describe several `ballad` pieces. The `popblues` single is the album's biggest hit, so qualifies within first three under the prompt's weighting rule; `souljazz` and `jazzfunk` rank afterward. The word blues in title alone would be insufficient, but the reviews directly discuss its guitar/blues music.
- **Instruments:** Official album credits confirmed by original LP transcription: Daniele vocals/guitars and additional keys; guest Mick Goodrick **electric guitar solos on two tracks only**; Harvie Swartz both electric bass and double bass; Gary Chaffee drums; Danilo Rea piano/keys; Rosario Iermano percussion; Daniele harmonica on “Domani”; Giulio Clementi programming. `electricguitar|bass|doublebass|drums|piano|keyboards|percussion|harmonica`. Original site lists “Guido Clementi” additional keys and “Giulio Clementi” sound programming whereas LP transcription combines keys/programming under Giulio; neither can be securely equated to a single person in `producers` (where neither belongs), so omit named keyboardist reconciliation pending sleeve check. Do not enter instrument `synth` or named studio hardware from `programming` alone.
- **Production:** `programming` explicitly named, no named machine or vocal treatment. Produced and arranged by **Pino Daniele alone**; Gregg Jackman mixed, Pinaxa engineered; neither is a co-producer on this release. Voice `powerful` on electric songs based independent review aggression, with romantic songs softer but no justified album-wide separate type.
- **Languages and topics:** `it|nap|en` from official lyrics; language balance turns toward Italian (“O scarrafone” mostly Italian despite Neapolitan title); continued Neapolitan on “Invece no”, “Femmena”; English phrases in “For your love” and “Leave a message” (inspect whether there are full English verses; if incidental only, Claude may remove `en`). Political/social antiracism and anti-xenophobia (“'O scarrafone”), class/consumerism irony (“Che soddisfazione”), difficult family relationships (“Gente distratta”) and love/heartbreak (“Domani”, “Femmena”, “Solo”). `energy 3`, `tempo 3`, `groove 3` from energetic pop-blues/dance rhythms with ballads; `mood 3` from ironic/happy hooks and serious family/social commentary; `electronic 2` for credited programming within a predominantly electric-guitar/bass/drum live ensemble; `theatrical 2`, `experimental 2`, `polish 4` studio polish without arbitrary presumed processors.
- **Guest influence rule:** Daniele says learning from **Mick Goodrick** changed his guitar harmonies from this album onward, but Goodrick appears *on this record as a guest soloist*. The user explicitly excludes a musician who only worked on an album as a guest from `influenced_by`, so leave the field blank and record the first-person statement only here. No critic compares the *sonic album sound* to another named artist, so `sounds_like` blank; no peer link is fabricated from the celebrity guest.
- **Landmark=1:** Separate independent OndaRock and DeBaser appraisals identify *Un uomo in blues* as the 1990s career/style transition, with pop-blues electric sound and national breakthrough, not simply a favorite album. Contrast OndaRock's lower artistic rating with DeBaser's enthusiasm: this landmark score is for a changed career phase, not aesthetic quality.

- **Sotto 'o sole** (1991 original artist site; 1992 some later database editions): OndaRock calls it explicitly an **antologia**, reconstructed earlier songs plus new songs. User orders **compilations excluded for now, even landmark ones**. Therefore no descriptor row or catalogue addition for it in this phase; check whether Claude wants a separately tracked later anthology item but not in this scope. Source discrepancies in date are noted, not auto-corrected.
- **Genres and musicological caution:** Genre list is intentionally long on some LPs because album-specific critical account + release metadata name substyles; the per-genre CSV makes long-tail and track-only genre sources explicit. Only new tokens requested here: `soulrock`, `popblues`. No new instrument or production tokens.
- **Credits:** `Pino Daniele|Gregg Jackman` on Schizzechea depends on accepting a *recording producer* like phase-02's Ferryboat precedent; reviewer should verify consistency. On *Le vie* there is no named album producer in open sources, so the cell is deliberately empty, not guessed from film composition.
