# Björk — Phase 02: album-by-album evidence notes

Scope: **1996 Telegram; 1997 Homogenic; 2000 Selmasongs; 2001 Vespertine only.** Studio ratings are editorial comparisons to Fandome's 0–5 anchors, not a measured instrument-by-instrument spectrogram. Linked sources and strength appear in `bjork_phase02_sources.csv`; every genre receives a separate `bjork_phase02_genre_evidence.csv` claim. Some genre tags are stylistic judgements inferred from directly described sound, as identified in the evidence table. Blank cells mean not established.

## Telegram (1996) — `bjork-telegram`

**Identity and date.** Björk's official store calls this her *second remix compilation* and gives 1996. Discographic market metadata gives **25 November 1996** UK, while AllMusic shows **14 January 1997**, a later US release. Use 1996. The ten-track UK album is the reference, not the eleven-track US edition that adds the original 'I Miss You' [T1, T2, T5]. `type=remix` is a Fandome type choice even though the label also describes a compilation.

**Album is not uniformly techno.** LFO/Mark Bell, Dillinja, Outcast and Mika Vainio bring abrasive electronic remixes; Brodsky Quartet turns 'Hyper-Ballad' into chamber strings; 'My Spine' with Evelyn Glennie makes percussion central; Eumir Deodato recasts 'Isobel' as a live-band/orchestral arrangement. Therefore album-wide `electronic|electronica|experimental` with techno, drumandbass, industrial and electro relegated to *some tracks*; folk or global genres are not invented from participating artists [T1–T5]. AllMusic's `alternativedance`, `experimentalrock`, `alternativerock` broad labels are retained with weaker scope for the rock labels. Hip-hop vocal guest Rodney P is bonus/pressing dependent; not an overall `hiphop` genre.

**Scales.** Energy 3 and tempo 3 reflect ambient/string passages offsetting fast breakbeat/jungle; electronic 4 because the core is computer/electronic remixes with a significant live/acoustic minority; mood 2 and theatrical 3 reflect reinterpreted tense, melancholic and open-ended moods, not merely the upbeat singles; experimental 4 corresponds to genre crossing and radical deconstruction; polish 3 reflects abrupt contrast of glossy orchestral tracks and raw/abrasive remixes; groove 3 because several tracks are for listening rather than dancing. Confidence **medium**, as these are per-track heterogenous estimates [T2, T5–T7].

**Voice, language, lyrics.** Björk's *re-recorded* lead vocals vary from forceful to delicate, with an occasional rap (Rodney P on 'I Miss You' on relevant pressings) and instrumental arrangement parts. Core UK lyrics English; themes follow original Post songs, mainly relationship uncertainty, intimacy and internal transformation. The `rap` type is track/edition dependent [T2, T5, T6]. **No voice-pitching machine is assigned** without track-specific technical testimony.

**Instruments/techniques.** Strings (Brodsky Quartet, cello and viola), percussion/drums (Glennie; other remixes), electric/acoustic guitars, bass and keys are corroborated by personnel entries, including [T4, T5]. The `sampling` tag is a documented property of constructing remixes from pre-existing masters; mark it *method level*, with remix-specific sources [T2,T3]. Re-recorded vocals do **not** automatically establish the `layeredvocals` process; that production tag is deliberately blank for these remixes apart from verified `sampling`.  Do not borrow instrument credits of US-only bonus 'I Miss You'.

**Producer semantics.** Listed names are original/new *producer or additional-producer* roles evidenced by track credits, not every remixer. In particular Dillinja, Mika Vainio and Dobie were omitted from `producers` because remix credit alone is insufficient. Brodsky Quartet has an *additional producer* credit; Deodato a producer/re-produced role; Evelyn Glennie co-production of 'My Spine'; some recording producers stem from the earlier Post masters. `sounds_like` is editorial sonic similarity; `influenced_by` blank. **Critical disagreement:** Erlewine calls the electronic techniques conventional in context despite radical rearrangements, whereas later Pitchfork values the collection's experimental choices [T2,T7].

## Homogenic (1997) — `bjork-homogenic`

**Identity and production concept.** Björk described her tightly constrained palette as **beats, strings and voice**: deliberately raw 'Icelandic techno' percussion paired with Icelandic String Octet and Deodato arrangements [H5,H8]. This restricts genre weighting: art-pop and electronic art-song first; techno, trip-hop, IDM, neoclassical and breakbeat qualify at subgenre / track level; the critic describes neoclassical passages and Arvo Pärt-like harmonic motion [H2,H3]. Electronica is directly catalogued by AllMusic. `avantgarde` comes from official label's 'avant-garde' tag, not an invented synonym. `orchestralpop` is a qualified stylistic mapping, not an explicit primary-source genre.

**Scales.** Energy 3 for the changing weight from 'Pluto' to 'Unravel'; electronic 4 because severe programmed beats are foundational alongside a prominent *acoustic* octet; mood 1 for heartbreak, unrest and sombre strings; theatrical 4 from forceful, cinematic vocals and string arcs; experimental 4 from rhythmic disruptions and instrumentation; polish 5 for intricate highly controlled production; tempo 3 mixed breakbeat and slow tracks; groove 2 as dance breaks coexist with many non-dance pieces [H2,H3,H5].

**Instruments.** Direct credits from [H4,H9]: Icelandic String Octet; Mark Bell/Dravs drum programming; keyboards (Björk, Mark Bell, Guy Sigsworth); accordion (Coba); organ and clavichord (Guy Sigsworth); glass harmonica (Alasdair Malloy); harp (Helen Tunstall); programmed electronic kit (Trevor Morais). `clavichord` and `glassharmonica` are **new Fandome instrument tokens**. Synth is supported by Björk's described synthetic riff and keyboard sound in her Musician interview [H5,H6]. `harp`, `accordion`, `organ` are not implied by the art-pop genre.

**Production.** Producer credits: Björk, Mark Bell, Howie B and Guy Sigsworth are verified on individual tracks [H4,H9]. Artist-label Bandcamp overview also names **Markus Dravs** as a producer, while some track-level liner transcription refers to him chiefly as creative engineer/programmer; retain but flag role discrepancy for import [H1,H4,H5]. Do not add Eumir Deodato to `producers`: he arranged/orchestrated the strings. `sampling`, Roland **TR-909** and Akai **S3000** studio sampler are documented by technical recording analysis [H7]; the same investigation identifies Björk's **Yamaha QY20** as a sequencing sketch tool. These are new production tokens `akais3000`, `yamahaqy20` requiring validation-map additions. `distortion` is supported by critical descriptions of processed/crunching percussion [H2], but **not** proof that a specific distortion pedal was used. Note QY20 is composition/preproduction, not proof of final mix chain. **Do not insert TR-808** merely because the era suggests it.

**Text/themes/influence.** Relationships and heartbreak, identity and determination. Pitchfork identifies rhythmic influence from **Aphex Twin** and harmonies resembling **Arvo Pärt**, a qualified critical assessment (not a claim Björk herself acknowledged compositionally). Steve Reich is described as a *faint sonic analogue* for part of a track, so he remains in critic notes and not automatically in `influenced_by` [H2]. The Icelandic sound is aesthetic/arrangement, not an Icelandic-language album. Strong confidence for broad features, nuanced confidence for technical model names.

## Selmasongs (2000) — `bjork-selmasongs`

**Identity.** Released as seven-track soundtrack to *Dancer in the Dark*, with a film musical narrative about Selma and the music of her fantasies. The official store states the record differs from the movie in **lyrics**; the Thom Yorke duet belongs to the soundtrack. Do not mix up the soundtrack, dialogue edition and film actors' spoken performances [S1,S2,S4]. Track-list conventions choose `Selmasongs` as the album's canonical abbreviated name, original release year 2000.

**Genres and scales.** `soundtrack` is **format only**; the actual musical genres are theatrical musical numbers, electronic art-pop, experimental pop, orchestral passages, programmed industrial rhythms. The factory sound source supports a *some-track* industrial style, not an industrial album. AllMusic catalogues trip-hop/electronica stylistically, both low in ranking. Energy 3 (varied from playful 'Cvalda' to solemn overture); electronic 3 (scored orchestra and sampled factory textures); mood 2 (bright fantasies shadowed by tragedy); theatrical 5 (songs function as characters' fantasy musicals); experimental 3 (found machinery sounds alongside traditional film-score tropes); polish 5; tempo 2; groove 2 [S1–S4].

**Instruments.** Orchestra/strings and brass, harp and xylophone/mallets per AllMusic and Pitchfork. Björk and Guy Sigsworth **celesta** are specifically in credits [S2,S3,S4,S6]. `mallets` groups xylophones according to source acoustics but does not claim a particular marimba. Timpani appear in a critic's Overture analysis, but are not inserted as a new token given uncertain original personnel credit [S3]. Do not add imaginary drum-machine models.

**Production.** Artist's official notes say machine/train sounds were used to *compose melodies and beats*: support `fieldrecordings` and `sampling` in an album-specific sense, but warn that `fieldrecordings` is a broad taxonomy tag, not proof they carried a field recorder on location [S1,S3]. `protools` is based on Jake Davies **Pro Tools technician** credit in original CD; it documents production software's involvement but not every stage of recording [S5]. Only credited producers are Björk and **Mark Bell**; Mark 'Spike' Stent mixed, Vince Mendoza arranged/conducted, Valgeir Sigurðsson handled vocal editing, not credited production [S4–S6].

**Lyrics/voice.** Selma's storyline incorporates visual loss, work, social circumstances, love, resilience and tragedy. The lead can be delicate or powerfully theatrical; 'Overture' instrumental, duets appear with Yorke and Deneuve. `social|spiritual` are secondary themes; spiritual concerns are interpretive rather than literal gospel. Language English. Influenced-by left **blank**; generic reference to Hollywood musicals is an aesthetic influence, not a sourced artist name.

**Critics' disagreement.** Heather Phares finds the musical/industrial combination poignant and inventive; Pitchfork argues the album reiterates Homogenic instead of breaking new ground. Reflect disagreement in experimental=3 rather than treating soundtrack as intrinsically experimental [S2,S3].

## Vespertine (2001) — `bjork-vespertine`

**Core sound.** Björk explicitly planned **microbeats and home-scale sounds**: intimate whisper-level lead vocal, orchestral strings/choir, Zeena Parkins harp, music-box motifs and domestic noises. The primary study of the album's microbeats describes glockenspiel and the custom music-box arrangement; independent 2001 review confirms 'Cocoon'/'Hidden Place' vocal intimacy [V1–V5]. Source `V8` describes it as chamber music; `chamberpop` is the qualified corresponding pop subtype. `glitchpop`, `ambientpop`, `orchestralpop`, `downtempo` are editorial mappings from described specific textures; see per-genre evidence rows, not quoted labels. `triphop`/`idm` are minor textures [V1,V2].

**Scales.** Energy 2 (muted percussive pulse), electronic 3 (laptop microbeat design over acoustic strings/harp/choir), mood 4 (warm intimacy and love with darker songs), theatrical 2 (restrained whispering despite lush crescendos), experimental 4 (microsound composition and custom music boxes), polish 5 (highly controlled multi-track orchestral/electronic layers), tempo 1 and groove 1 (mostly intimate listener-oriented, not dance club). The stark shift from Homogenic's weight is important to recommendation accuracy [V1,V2,V3,V8].

**Instruments.** Zeena Parkins' harp, orchestra strings, Guy Sigsworth celesta/clavichord, glockenspiel and bespoke music boxes all substantiated [V1,V5,V6,V7]. Björk said string and music-box arrangements were written using **Sibelius** (the notation app), not a synthesizer model; `sibelius` belongs in production, not instruments [V4]. More general keyboard, synth and bass credits/specific textures documented in reviews/credits [V2,V6]. **New instruments:** `musicbox`, `glockenspiel`, `clavichord` already introduced above. Avoid claiming `synthbass` unless engineering credits prove it rather than programmed bass notes.

**Production.** `microbeats` refers to documented pointillistic small-sound beat writing, not a named physical drum machine [V5]. `sampling` supported by Oval source sampled in 'Unison' plus concrete domestic sounds; `layeredvocals` supported by multiple tracked Björk harmonies and choir in reviews [V2]. `fieldrecordings` denotes recorded household/found noises but should not be interpreted as literal outdoor soundscape collection [V1,V8]. Björk repeatedly describes selecting/positioning sounds and leading **~80% of the beat production**. Producer credits from published album booklet [V7] include **Björk and Thomas Knak**; **Marius de Vries** is *assistant producer*, while **Matmos and Matthew Herbert** are programmers: do **not** automatically add them as credited producers despite Pitchfork casually calling Matmos co-producers [V2,V3,V6,V7]. `sibelius` is attested as a named app by Björk in *Wired*, not inferred from period [V4].

**Lyrics.** Privacy, love, bodily sensuality, belonging, domestic self-sufficiency, yearning. Whispered/soft lead is the majority, but occasional expressive swells; chorus is significant on tracks like 'Hidden Place' and 'Unison'. Only English credited. `influenced_by` deliberately blank: general admiration of Rephlex/drill'n'bass and *Oval* sampling are not statements of direct album-level influence from artists [V2,V8].

**Critics' disagreement.** Heather Phares (AllMusic) praises intimate subtlety and novel domestic percussion; Pitchfork's Ryan Schreiber describes an elegant album he considers less inventive than its predecessor. The linked academic article analyses music-box authorship and microbeats without resolving review preference. Experimental 4 is tied to specific mechanisms, not critical consensus [V1,V2,V5].

## Import review reminders

Genre evidence preserves track vs album scope; a single track's glitch or jungle does not dominate ranking. Production models must not be guessed from adjacent albums. Do not label `Matmos`, `Eumir Deodato`, `Vince Mendoza`, `Mark Stent` or any remixer as record producer solely because of an arranging, mixing or remix credit. New tokens are declared in `CLAUDE_IMPORT.md`. Every `status=draft` until second review.
