# Snap! — Fandome research / evidence notes — phase 01

Created 27 October 2026. Album-only scope: 1990, 1992, 1994. Descriptors are original editorial judgments, not review text. `status=draft` throughout. Sources are fully cited in accompanying CSVs. Scores 0–5 anchored to project guide.

## World Power (1990) — `snap-worldpower`

- **Genres**: The 1990 Logic German CD explicitly lists Hip-House and Euro House; contemporary LP shows House. AllMusic adds Electronic / Techno while some US 1990 editions classify Pop Rap. Historical accounts call debut Eurodance; genre ordering gives most weight to the early pressing and vocal mix. `hiphouse` is a NEW exact genre token (do not substitute hiphop).
- **Singers**: Turbo B's raps and Penny Ford's sung refrains, though exact versions of “The Power” initially sampled Chill Rob G/Jocelyn Brown and were later recut. Voice = rap|powerful|clear, not a claim of a fixed lead voice in every track.
- **Instruments/production**: Sampling is documented for “The Power” and “Blasé Blasé” with sources MusicBrainz, Goethe-Institut and Rolling Stone. `sampler` and `sequencer` are generic instrument-level representations of the sampled, computer-sequenced original sound (Münzing's first-person computer-arrangement quote and Anzilotti's long-running sequencer workflow from 1994). **These are lower-certainty inference for exact album instrumentation**; no retroactive assignment of any 1994 studio gear model. No drum-machine model is claimed for 1990.
- **Production**: `sampling` only; names Mantronix, Chill Rob G, Jocelyn Brown and James Brown are sample provenance, **not** `influenced_by` evidence. Snap! was the producer credit; the two members are Michael Münzing (Benito Benites) and Luca Anzilotti (John “Virgo” Garrett III).
- **Scales**: energy4/tempo4/groove5 based on dancefloor singles and AllMusic energetic/club descriptions; electronic5 from sampled/computer-produced house sound; mood4 from playful celebratory review tags; theatrical3, experimental2, polish4 are informed editorial assessments and should be further playback-reviewed.
- **Landmark1** debut established international group; `sounds_like` editorial parallels Technotronic and C+C Music Factory, not artist-declared inspiration. `lyrics` blank as album lyric analysis was not sufficiently reliable.
- **Edition caveat**: 8-track original LP versus CDs with “Only Human” and a “The Power” mix. Do not include compilation/remix-only material as part of underlying album sonic profile.

## The Madman's Return (1992) — `snap-themadmansreturn`

- **Genres**: Original 1992 pressing lists Euro House and Downtempo. Later pressings also list Hip-House. AllMusic categories include Electronic/Techno. Eurodance is supported by album review. Genre evidence CSV records track-versus-album scope.
- **Instruments**: Original CD sleeve transcriptions credit **sequence design** to Anzilotti/Münzing and guitars to Andy Plöcher (“Colour of Love”) and Daniel Iribarren (“Don't Be Shy”), plus wind instruments by Bobby Sattler (“Money”). Hence `sequencer|guitar|woodwinds`. No exact 1994 equipment imported to this album.
- **Production**: `programming` from original sleeve’s sequenced design; `sampling` from music metadata identifying the Newcleus “Automan” riff incorporated into “Rhythm Is a Dancer”; this does **not** turn Newcleus into an influence automatically. All individual songs credited `Snap!` producer.
- **Vocals**: Original album-era Turbo B and Thea Austin (rap and strong melodic singing). Niki Haris associated with later bonus tracks / lineup change; do not assume she is frontwoman across the original 1992 album. `voice=rap|powerful|clear` mirrors this mixed vocal approach.
- **Scales**: Energy4, electronic5, groove4 from club/dance and hip-house review; mood3 because softer/downtempo tracks interrupt euphoric hits; experimental2 due to conventionally structured club/pop productions; other scales are editorial and require listening confirmation.
- **Edition caveat (important)**: FIRST original album exists in shorter versions; several later 1992 CDs add edits/“Snap at the Movies”, and editions of “Rhythm Is a Dancer” differ substantially (spoken word versus single rap). Keep one album row for the original 1992 entity; don't calculate averages by compiling every expanded reissue track. **The magazine’s casual “Jan 1993” discography is not the original release year**; 1992 confirmed by actual original pressings, MusicBrainz and album listings.
- **`sounds_like`** Culture Beat, Technotronic as editor-level sonic comparison; no album-specific declared musical influence.

## Welcome to Tomorrow (1994) — `snap-welcometotomorrow`

- **Genre and sonic development**: Original 1994 74321 22384 2 CD classifies Trance, Euro House and Downtempo. AllMusic and contemporary Billboard call this a trance-oriented new incarnation. German producer Luca Anzilotti told Roger Brown, *The Mix*, November 1994, that it is more melodic and less rap-based than earlier Snap! albums, maintaining club beats.
- **Instruments**: **First-hand direct evidence** in *The Mix*: sequencer-driven loops; samples of drums and synthesized patterns processed with ReCycle; a guitarist used on the album (original jacket names Markus/Marcus Deml on “Waves”). Thus sampler|sequencer|synth|guitar. The drum loop is no proof of a specifically-played drum machine model.
- **Production**: In same direct Nov 1994 interview Anzilotti describes actual album methods with Pro Tools, Steinberg Cubase, ReCycle and sampling/reassembly; code `sampling|programming|synthprogramming|protools|cubase|recycle|digitalrecording|sonicmanipulation`. A reissue sleeve transcription identifies **“Voxalator V01b”** synthetic voices on “Dream on the Moon”; the project token `voxalatorv01b` is NEW. Studio inventory lists TR-808, Prophet-5, Akai S3200, etc., but **ownership is NOT evidence they were used on this specific album**, so do not enter those in its production cell.
- **Lead voice**: Paula Brown (“Summer”) sings on tracks 1,2,5,6,7; Rukmani guest on “Rame”; occasional studio-processed voices and other vocal guests elsewhere. No rap-led Turbo B sound in 1994; voice `clear|soft|spoken` for the contrast.
- **Scales**: Energy3/mood4/tempo3/groove4 from the mix of spacious melodic trance and slower tracks; electronic5, experimental3, polish5 supported by technical account of Pro Tools/Cubase, edited samples and experimental synthetic voices; theatrical3 from big choruses. Interpretive, not measured facts.
- **Lyrics**: `love|social` supported by explicit “People Need to Love One Another” lyric credit/title and “We Are One”, but treat as lower-confidence than production credits. Comparisons Jam & Spoon / Culture Beat are editorial, *not* evidence for `influenced_by`.
- **Versions**: September 30 1994 original album; some catalogs list 1995 US reissues, or generic digital platform January 1994 dates. Version of “Rame” varies (later single mix swapped in on reissues). Bonus movie/TV cue placement must not cause soundtrack type assignment.

## Influences rule

As of these sources, **no album-specific artist/producer-declared named musicians proven as musical influences** for the three albums. Anzilotti's 1994 interview actually says the duo did not base writing on what others were doing. The named samples (Newcleus, Mantronix, Chill Rob G, Jocelyn Brown, James Brown) are source audio and are **not** entered as influences. Producers/singers and his mention of Klaus Schulze's *quote* also don't qualify. Artist-wide influences section in `CLAUDE_IMPORT.md` accurately reports **none verified**, rather than inventing a list.
