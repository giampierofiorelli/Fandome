# Evidence notes — Depeche Mode, Phase 02

All judgments use **the original UK editions** and the Fandome eight 0–5 anchors. Album-level tags from music databases are distinguished from supported track textures. Values in the CSV remain proposals (`status=draft`). All evidence paths below also appear as clickable full URLs in the accompanying source table. The source domain `depmode.com` transcribes original label material and is not the band's official site; original Mute credits and the band archive take precedence. The phase-01 `CLAUDE_IMPORT.md` contains **artist-wide influences**, which must not be recopied here.

## The Singles 81–85 (1985) — `depechemode-thesingles8185`

**Edition/importance.** Official Depeche Mode Archives lists UK Mute MUTEL1, 15 October 1985, and the 13-track *vinyl* sequence. Contemporary original UK CD/cassette editions can have 15 songs (`The Meaning of Love`, `Somebody`). The 1998 *The Singles 81>85* reissue has *two more* tracks (`Photographic` and the `Just Can't Get Enough` Schizo Mix). AllMusic's page principally describes that later edition: use its historical music tags cautiously, **do not** treat its extras as part of the 1985 master. US *Catching Up With Depeche Mode* (1985) is a separate, different compilation, not an album to add in this batch. Landmark 1 as a widely circulated, representative 1981–85 early singles document, without claiming fresh 1985 studio sessions beyond the two then-recent singles.

**Scales.** Energy 3 and tempo 3 average bright quick early tracks and darker, midtempo late songs. Electronic 5 describes overwhelmingly synthesizer/sequencer/drum-machine-based 1981–85 singles even where sampled percussion or piano appears. Mood 3 deliberately averages bouncy `New Life`/`Just Can't Get Enough` and brooding `Blasphemous Rumours`/`Shake the Disease`; it does not characterise every track as happy. Theatrical 2: tightly written synth-pop singles, relatively direct stage/persona at this point. Experimental 2: hook-based single format wins, although production becomes markedly more unusual from 1983. Polish 3: production changes across four years; avoid interpreting a collection's mastering as a single newly produced album. Groove 4: danceable singles predominate but with subdued exceptions (`Shake the Disease`). AllMusic and the 1986 Gore/Wilder contemporary interview inform these judgments.

**Genres.** `synthpop` primary from AllMusic. `newwave` and `electropop` are named in historical critical/1986 contemporary reporting; use both terms verbatim, never equate to `electronic`. AllMusic's `alternativedance`, `postpunk`, `dancerock`, `clubdance`, `alternativepoprock`, `contemporarypoprock`, `punknewwave` are explicit but broad retrospective library metadata. `industrial` is last and scoped **only to selected 1983–85 tracks**, from Sputnikmusic's `proto-industrial` discussion; it is not a claim that 1981 Clarke material was industrial. A different editorial choice could drop the weak AllMusic umbrella tags while preserving evidence; Claude should not silently reorder primary genres.

**Voice, instruments.** `clear`/`powerful` represent Gahan's direct 1981 approach and stronger late 1984–85 delivery; `soft` covers Gore's gentler shared leads (`Somebody`). Across the selection: `synth`, `drummachine`, `sequencer` remain central (Paul Tingen/E&MM). `sampler` is present **in later original masters**, notably Synclavier and E-mu technology documented by Gareth Jones; not attributed to Clarke-era debut. `piano` is supported by `Somebody`; `acousticguitar` was sampled for the 1984 `People Are People` period according to Gore's first-person 1986 remarks. `percussion` includes recorded metal/hit sounds in later singles. `keyboards` is the wider credit category; not shorthand for specific unidentified hardware. No machine listed here is asserted to have been used on *each* recording.

**Production and producers.** `synthprogramming|drumprogramming` reflects original electronic production throughout. `sampling|synclavier|emulator|emulatorii` belongs to 1983–85 original source tracks; sources refer to original Emulator generation and subsequent Emulator II, rather than identifying both as the same model. `analogtape` reflects original pre-digital recording workflows and Jones' account of layered recording to tape, **not** the audio carrier of the 1985 CD. The compilation has no single session producer. Across source tracks, original credited **producers** are Depeche Mode and Daniel Miller, with Gareth Jones credited as a producer on 1984 and some 1985 masters; Jones was an **engineer**, not producer, on *Construction Time Again*. No remastering engineer is entered. `influenced_by` blank because this is a retrospective, not a newly developed 1985 album. `sounds_like` is an editorial-era similarity to contemporary electronic-pop ensembles, not an influence claim. Lyrics include earnest love, darker interpersonal themes, corporate/social criticism, and a religious-fatalistic angle on `Blasphemous Rumours`.

**Disagreement/limitations.** Music-platform data variously describes the 1998 reissue instead of the 1985 original. Single-version variations matter. The row's confidence is **medium**, rather than artificially high, because it compresses materially different production years and album content.

## Black Celebration (1986) — `depechemode-blackcelebration`

**Edition/importance.** UK Mute STUMM26, released 17 March 1986; eleven tracks from `Black Celebration` through `New Dress`. The US bonus song `But Not Tonight`, CD bonuses and 2007 reissues **not** used. Major step in the band's sonic identity, hence landmark 1. Gareth Jones recalls recording real fireworks for `Stripped`, while Paul Tingen interviewed Martin Gore and Alan Wilder immediately after sessions (E&MM, August 1986).

**Scales.** Energy 3: forceful `A Question of Time`, `Stripped` and `New Dress` meet comparatively quiet `Sometimes`, `A Question of Lust`, `World Full of Nothing`. Electronic 5: 1986 original interviews describe a synth/sampler-centric ensemble; acoustic guitar is sampled, and piano is a notable secondary colour. Mood 1 for recurring isolation, existential fatigue and dark romance, despite sensuality and warmth; contrast the pop-exterior/dark-interior distinction. Theatrical 3 for designed atmosphere, transitions and dramatic lead delivery, not stadium bombast. Experimental 4: found sounds, pitched female vocal sample, precise machine manipulation, tape layers and extensively edited sequences. Polish 4: ambitious 48-track recording and final effects work, not a raw industrial session. Tempo 2: Gore explicitly says four drumless tracks and many gentle songs. Groove 3: strong rhythmic cuts offset by numerous ballads. Source: contemporary Tingen interview, Raggett's album appraisal, producer Jones.

**Genres.** `synthpop`/`darkwave` primary AOTY retrospective genre categories; `postindustrial` and `gothicrock` are explicitly named on that same aggregator but describe a darker *aesthetic*, **not** a move to a conventional guitar-band arrangement. AllMusic attaches `alternativedance|dancerock|alternativepoprock|collegerock` as secondary metadata; the latter is a radio-market genre rather than proof of prominent guitars. `electropop` is the term contemporary interviewer Tingen uses while explicitly discussing the record. Raggett argues the album moved *away from* its preceding industrial-pop fusion, so **do not** add `industrialpop` simply from that negative comparison. Critics differ over whether the album's key feature is amplified dance music, rich orchestrated electronica, or a darker ballad cycle.

**Instruments.** Tingen quotes Gore on the `sampler` and `sequencer` in use, describing both the Emulator II and Synclavier. Synthesized drums, the ARP 2600 and drum programming justify `drummachine`/`percussion` (these are sources, not a guess from genre). Gore says the `Here Is the House` mandolin-like line is **two sampled acoustic guitar articulations**: add `acousticguitar`, not `mandolin`. `piano` on `Sometimes` is identified by AllMusic and studio credits. `guitar` credited by album personnel. Keyboards and synth are distinct generic categories, not an assumption of any vintage model.

**Production.** These names and processes are **firsthand 1986 evidence**: `sampling`, `synthprogramming`, `drumprogramming`, `synclavier` (primarily drum sounds), `emulatorii` (predominant sampling), `arp2600` (analogue drums, notably `New Dress`), PPG Wave 2.3 => **new token `ppgwave23`** (do not silently substitute unspecified `ppgwave`), Akai S612 => **new token `akais612`**, BBC Micro => **new token `bbcmicro`**, UMI 2B interface/software => **new token `umi2b`**. `fieldrecordings`: motorbike sound, power drill, fireworks. `pitchedvocals`: Gore describes a moan from the vintage `She Wants to Mambo` record repitched for `A Question of Time`; this is sampling, **not a source of songwriting influence**. `sonicmanipulation`: repeating and treating sampled sounds and the sequenced vocals on `It Doesn't Matter Two`. `analogtape`: 48-track multitrack discussion, overdubbing of separately recorded sounds; no automatic `overdubbing` field to avoid redundancy without an explicit individual technique claim. The interview mentions Roland 100M, RSF Kobol and Syrinx as **owned/retained machines**, but does not prove a specific recorded part; omit those. The article distinguishes **live DX7/Jupiter-8/OB-X** from these album machines. Never substitute live gear as studio evidence.

**Producers.** `Depeche Mode|Gareth Jones|Daniel Miller` from official production credits and MusicBrainz; assistant engineers NOT promoted to producers. Jones and Gore both remember tense sessions, but production is collaborative. Voice powerful (Gahan), soft (Gore on four tracks) and clear; no unsupported named vocal register machine. Lyrics intimacy/relationships and desolation, with political-media critique **specifically** in `New Dress` (original lyrics on official site). `influenced_by=Philip Glass` **only** because Gore conceded in 1986 that `It Doesn't Matter Two` was probably remotely inspired by Glass; qualified as a **single-track** influence, not a general album-wide source or an accusation of plagiarism. The sampled doo-wop record is *not* credited as an artistic influence. Similarities to Cabaret Voltaire/Fad Gadget/The Cure/New Order are **editorial**, not claims of direct borrowing.

## Music for the Masses (1987) — `depechemode-musicforthemasses`

**Edition/importance.** Original UK Mute STUMM47 LP 28 September 1987. The album's ten songs, plus briefly credited `Interlude #1`, are separated from 1987 CD B-sides and 2006–07 bonus reissues. Stadium expansion is backed by Raggett and 101 concert trajectory. Landmark 1.

**Scales.** Energy 3: muscular `Never Let Me Down Again`, `Behind the Wheel`, `Nothing` but quieter `Little 15`, `The Things You Said`, `I Want You Now`. Electronic 4: strong samplers/synths with conspicuous recorded guitar, piano and accordion, therefore less completely electronic than 1986 album. Mood 1 for recurrent tension, desire, alienation and nocturnal intimacy. Theatrical 4 for widescreen/reverberant orchestral scale and chorale-like instrumental `Pimpf`. Experimental 3: found vocals and sequenced instrumental soundscapes inside comparatively accessible structures. Polish 5: dense reverb, carefully balanced sampled orchestral effects, arena-ready mixes. Tempo 3 and groove 3: meaningful variation between slow pieces and dance-rooted singles. Source: Bascombe, Raggett, Apple Music editorial.

**Genres.** The AOTY album-level descriptors include `synthpop|darkwave|neoclassicaldarkwave|electroindustrial`. **New** exact-name tokens `neoclassicaldarkwave` and `electroindustrial`: retrospective, narrower associations centred on sampled strings and mechanized tracks respectively; do not imply a whole-album neoclassical composition or conventional industrial band. AllMusic lists `alternativedance|dancerock|alternativepoprock|collegerock`; weaker broad radio/style tags. Apple's release editorial calls the album Pop and `Behind the Wheel` electro-driven, justifying `pop` as broad and `electro` **last** (specific-track texture). No `country` genre from Apple's *country-tinged guitar* metaphor for `Strangelove`. The record blends these without relabeling `electro` as electropop.

**Instruments.** Contemporary original press release and personnel credit `guitar`, `keyboards`, `piano`, `accordion` (on `I Want You Now`), `sampler`, `drummachine` and programming. Bascombe confirms Gore's performed `guitar` on `Never Let Me Down Again` was sampled into a Synclavier by an accidental trigger; **this guitar is an original performance, not solely synthesized emulation**. `synth|sequencer|percussion` are directly consistent with credits and production narrative. Accordion can be played as airy texture even without chords/keys. Orchestral sampled sounds are **not evidence** of a live `orchestra`, `strings`, `brass`, `cello`, or choir: avoid those instrument tokens.

**Production.** `sampling|synclavier` specifically supported by Dave Bascombe's retrospective first-person description of `Never Let Me Down Again`. Bascombe also mentions sampling work with the Emulator II, supporting `emulatorii` on the 1987 sessions. `synthprogramming|drumprogramming|programming` supported by original band credits plus engineer account. `heavyreverb` from Bascombe on album-scale reverberant/cinematic sound (`Little 15`) and critic description, **not** a claim about every track. Do not use `logic`, `protools`, `ableton`, `emax` (the latter is explicitly proven on the later *tour*, not album sessions), or an unverified drum-machine model. DM Live's sample library identifications are marked with different certainty levels; only clear first-person named machines enter the row.

**Producers and influences.** Crucial correction: **original 1987 Mute inner-sleeve press release credits `Produced by Depeche Mode/David Bascombe` and separately `Additional production and help Daniel Miller`**. Thus row is only `Depeche Mode|David Bascombe`. Some fan summaries list Miller in a producer field, but his source-level specific credit is *additional*, disallowed under Fandome rules. Direct **album-specific** influence: Alan Wilder identified Michael Nyman's **1985 *A Zed & Two Noughts*** score as the template for `Little 15`, and later reiterated this on his Shunt site. So include `Michael Nyman (A Zed & Two Noughts)` with qualification *only one song*. This statement is independent of any sampled audio reported by fans. Lyrics `love|introspection|sensuality|spiritual|heartbreak|storytelling`: narrative of `Little 15`, relationship power, erotic tension in `I Want You Now`, sacral metaphor of `Sacred`, nuanced despair. `sounds_like` is editorial New Order/Cure/OMD/Simple Minds adjacency, **not** direct influence.

## 101 (1989) — `depechemode-101`

**Original/importance.** Mute double LP **13 March 1989** documenting the final 18 June 1988 Rose Bowl *Music for the Masses* tour show. The original LP has **17 tracks**; the double CD has **20**, including `Sacred`, `Nothing` and `A Question of Lust`. These edition choices do not form separate new albums. The D.A. Pennebaker documentary is a **separate film**; it is not an album `soundtrack` or a separate second 101 row. Venue/crowd evidence is official and Mary Valle's 33 1/3 research. Only exceptional Depeche Mode live LP selected among future batches; landmark 1.

**Scales.** Energy 4 for louder, percussive and emphatic Rose Bowl renditions compared with studio originals. Electronic 4 because samplers/keys and pre-recorded backing continue to drive performances while guitar, melodica, piano and percussion pads enter the arrangement. Mood 3 averages ecstatic shared crowd performance against the songs' dark themes; distinct from Black Celebration's studio `mood=1`. Theatrical 4 accounts for set-piece instrumental openings and stadium call-and-response. Experimental 2: arrangements are adapted to stage and the repertoire is retrospective, less original sonic experiment than 1986. Polish 3: mixed, professionally captured concert, but deliberate crowd and live variability, not a new spotless studio master. Tempo 3 and groove 4 reflect rhythmic set and slower Gore-led intervals. These are **interpretive**, not exact BPM measures.

**Genres.** AllMusic names `synthpop|alternativedance|dancerock|alternativepoprock|collegerock` for the album. The latter three are **critical/music-industry style metadata**; they do not prove a guitar/rock-band configuration. Do **not** add `soundtrack` (recording is not the film score), `concert` as a genre, or all parent album genre tags without source. A little guitar and audience singing do not justify new source-free tags.

**Instruments.** Original 101 record personnel and touring listings explicitly name *keyboards/synths*, *sampler*, *guitar*, *melodica*, *piano* (Wilder), and *percussion pads*. `sampler` corresponds to the band's E-mu Emax HD/SE live system, distinct from old studio `emulatorii`. The 1987–88 tour inventory specifically names **Yamaha DX7 IID**: token `dx7iid`, deliberately distinct from `dx7` (first-generation generic model). `emax` is also a **new production token**. Percussion pads are encoded as existing `percussion`, not `drums` (no source confirms a live acoustic drum kit). Do not populate `drummachine` from studio masters; taped percussion tracks were played back live and could be samples.

**Production and producers.** Original UK sleeve explicitly **`Produced by Depeche Mode`**; Alan Moulder is listed as *engineer*, while **Randy Ezratty recorded** the Rose Bowl concert with assistants Mark Shane/John Harris/Billy Yodelman. A few MusicBrainz track-level relations mistakenly also list Alan Moulder as a `producer`: **original sleeve takes priority**, do not promote engineer to co-producer. `concertrecording|liverecording` are obvious from master history; `sampling|emax|dx7iid` from the contemporary tour setup. `analogtape`: the 1987–88 tour used tape backing; later touring archival research contains excerpts from original tour tape sequences. **This does not claim the 1989 finished release is an unedited analogue master.** No `onetake`, `overdubbing`, `digitaltape` or vocal corrective technique inserted: Alan Wilder's guarded comment about live-record touch-ups is too imprecise to document per-song edits. 2004 surround sound remastering is excluded.

**Voice/lyrics.** Gahan's front-of-stage `powerful|clear` tone and Gore's vulnerable `soft` leads on multiple ballads documented by Raggett and sleeve tracklist. Crowd participation supports mood/groove, but `choral` is *not* asserted to be a lead vocal type. Lyrics span older original songs' love, alienation, politics, religion and sensuality; it is a career set, not a new 1988 authorial theme. `influenced_by` blank: replaying compositions already written does not introduce newly substantiated influences; `sounds_like` represents editorial sonic peers. Producer of the 101 **film** Frazer Pennebaker and film executive producer Daniel Miller are irrelevant to the *album* producer field.

**Conflicting counts.** Historic concert articles cite roughly 60,000–65,000 spectators; never enter a precise unsupported count in descriptor metadata. Exact numbers do not alter this row.
