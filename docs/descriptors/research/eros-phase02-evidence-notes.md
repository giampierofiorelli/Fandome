## In ogni senso (1990) — `eros-inognisenso`

**Release and producer.** The 12-track original is credited to Piero Cassano, with distinct **arrangers** Maurizio Bassi and Celso Valli. German and Yugoslav 1990 LPs substantiate this year; one Qobuz digital display says 1989 and AllMusic sometimes assigns 1991, likely metadata/issue differences rather than original release. A review by AllMusic's Alex Henderson explicitly calls it ballad-heavy, tightly produced American-style adult contemporary. The negative evaluation is **not** converted into a numeric quality judgement.

**Instruments, production.** 1990 German pressing: Celso Valli piano, Hammond organ and synth bass; Marco Forni piano and programming; Paolo Gianolio/Giorgio Cocilovo on guitar, John Giblin/Pino Palladino on bass, Charlie Morgan/Gabriele Melotti drums. Tracks document acoustic double bass on “Andare…”, strings directed by Valli on “Amarti è l'immenso per me” and “Cantico”; alto/tenor sax, trumpet and trombone on “C'è una strada in cielo”/“Oggi che giorno è”; clarinet on “Canzoni lontane”; congas on “Taxi Story”. “Dammi la luna” credits Eros with **sampler [vocals]**: `sampling` thus track-specific, not an album-wide electronic genre. Do not assume a particular sampler make/model. `programming|stringarrangements|sampling` are traceable. Electronic 2 reflects limited synth bass/sampling relative to rhythm section; polish 5 and theatrical 4 reflect deliberately grand, densely arranged adult-pop tracks. Tempo 2/groove 2 account for ballad dominance, though not every track is slow.

**Lyrics / voice / comparisons.** “Se bastasse una canzone” is socially outward-facing, while the bulk is love and interpersonal narrative; `love|social|introspection|storytelling` at album scale. Guest Antonella Bucci and Piero Cassano do **not** turn the record into an English album. Raspiness approximates documented reedy/gritty tenor; `powerful` reflects performance dynamics, editorial. Richard Marx and Umberto Tozzi are **sonic comparisons**, not recorded influences. No album-specific stated influence found, so field blank.

**Critic disagreement.** AllMusic's review judges the songwriting generic despite recognizing polished adult contemporary sound; record-company metadata and collector taxonomy give a pop-rock/soft-rock focus. Genre claims preserve both musical categories and avoid importing the review's subjective quality judgment.

## Tutte storie (1993) — `eros-tuttestorie`

**Release and production discrepancy.** 1993 original Italian CD has 13 tracks. Piero Cassano is indisputably producer. Estatica's original CD transcription and Qobuz list **Adelio Cogliati as assistant producer**, whereas original Italian **vinyl LP** metadata explicitly credits him **co-producer**. Therefore `producers=Piero Cassano|Adelio Cogliati` is supported for **one edition**; Claude should inspect the physical LP sleeve before accepting the secondary co-producer claim across editions. Celso Valli is credited *arranger / realization / keyboards*, not substituted for a producer. If pressing inspection shows the supposed co-producer credit is a database mistake, remove Adelio Cogliati from the row.

**Instruments and programming.** Original 1993 LP credits Steve Ferrone drums; Neil Stubenhaus/Tony Levin on bass; Phil Palmer and Steve Farris guitars; Celso Valli keys; Jai Winding piano/Hammond on “Un grosso no”; Luis Conte percussion; Brandon Fields sax on two tracks. Luca Bignardi credited `Recorded By, Programmed By [Sound, Computer]`, so `programming` is defensible; the type of synthesizer and drum-machine model is **not** documented. Do not infer `drumprogramming`. Qobuz includes song-level confirmation. `electronic=2` assesses digital programming integrated with an acoustic rhythm section.

**Genres.** The 1993 original **Italian LP** calls it Soft Rock, Europop, Pop Rock. AllMusic additionally lists Italian Pop, Adult Contemporary, Adult Alternative Pop/Rock, Latin Pop and Alternative/Indie Rock. The last three are kept **at the tail** as conflicting database taxonomy rather than an assertion that the music is genuinely alternative rock or primarily Latin pop. The Spanish-language counterpart *Todo historias* is **not** folded into `language`: original Italian vocals only. `sounds_like=Richard Marx|Raf` editorial; `influenced_by` blank.

**Themes and scales.** Romantic relationships, nostalgia, interpersonal conflict and social observation appear across tracks. The latter is especially relevant to “Esodi” and “L'ultima rivoluzione”; no unsupported political genre inferred. Mixed tempo and energetic guitars give energy 3/tempo 3; high polish reflects detailed transatlantic sessions; theatrical 3 rather than 4 as vocal affect sits closer to conversational pop than the previous record. The reader should not confuse the presence of a bass guitarist with `doublebass`: Qobuz's “Acoustic Bass” metadata for a few tracks is ambiguous, so the token remains `bass`.

## Dove c’è musica (1996) — `eros-dovecemusica`

**Album-level producers.** Source transcribed from original CD states **Prodotto da Eros Ramazzotti**. Celso Valli is **arranger / realization**, and Luca Bignardi is engineer/programmer, not producers. The record is his first **self-produced original album**; the earlier hits re-recording collection of 1997 is excluded from the phase.

**Instrument credits.** European 1996 CD listing names Michael Landau guitar, Vinnie Colaiuta drums, Nathan East and Paolo Gianolio bass, Lenny Castro percussion, Celso Valli keyboards and drum programming, Luca Bignardi programming/drum programming, Steve Tavaglione sax, Sid Page violin. “Yo sin ti” includes acoustic guitar, brass and trombones, multiple saxophones, David Garfield piano. MusicBrainz/Qobuz credits for “Più bella cosa” specifically identify *electric/acoustic guitar*, Hammond organ, piano, bass, drums and percussion. `production=drumprogramming|programming` retains only explicit techniques, no invented sampling or model-specific drum machine.

**Genres, lyrics, languages.** Original CD pressed as pop rock and soft rock; AllMusic tags Italian pop and Apple tags Pop. 1996 *Dove c'è musica* includes “**Yo sin ti**” sung in Spanish on the *original Italian album* (the remaining songs are predominantly Italian), so `it|es`; no conflation with Spanish-language release *Donde hay música*. Emotional love songs balanced with social/other narrative; mood 4 (brighter than other phases) and theatrical 4 are editorial anchors. Eros's growly tenor approximated `raspy`; compare Laura Pausini / Richard Marx only as editorial similarity.

**Release date uncertainty.** Apple Music original currently displays 30 April 1996; AllMusic shows 21 May 1996 while other discographies show 13 May. A single **release year 1996** is reliable; do not choose an exact initial release day from these conflicting retailer dates.

## Stilelibero (2000) — `eros-stilelibero`

**Non-uniform production.** Multiple credited *producers by track*, rather than one global producer. Original-era CD metadata: Trevor Horn tracks 1/4/11; Celso Valli tracks 3/7/9; Claudio Guidetti tracks 5/8/10/12; Rick Nowels tracks 2/6; Qobuz identifies **Charles Judge as co-producer** on track 2 “Fuoco nel fuoco” (likely track 6 in other credits; do not assert without a corroborating per-track credit). Accordingly the row carries all five; this is not a co-billed album with Cher, whose guest performance is limited to track 6. `producers` preserves credited heads, not mere press-release collaborators. Rockol's August 2000 preview mentions the four principal named collaborators but does **not** itself prove producer credits.

**Specific instrumentation.** Contemporary Rockol release preview and 2000 CD credits include Michael Landau and Tim Pierce guitars, Nathan East / John Pierce bass, John “J.R.” Robinson / Steve Ferrone / Alfredo Golino drums, Charles Judge keyboards, Greg Phillinganes piano, Jamie Muhoberac keyboards, Tim Carmon/Brian Auger organ, Luis Conte/Lenny Castro percussion, Dean Parks and Tim Pierce mandolin, strings/violins/viola/cello (especially “Lo spirito degli alberi”, “Il mio amore per te” and “Improvvisa luce ad est”), harmonica by Fio Zanotti on “Per me per sempre”. *Drum programming* is credited to Steve Hilton on 1/4/11 and Wayne Rodrigues on 2; other programmers appear Qobuz, hence `drumprogramming|programming`. Any stronger claim about an exact synthesizer model would be speculative and is intentionally absent. Solo violin vs general strings are both credited.

**Genre disagreement / scope.** Original-era edition collector metadata lists soft rock, Europop, ballad, pop rock, general pop. Jose F. Promis (AllMusic) calls the **opening track** “L'ombra del gigante” Euro-dance-leaning: therefore `eurodance` goes **last** with `scope=some tracks`, and its presence does not justify treating the album as primarily a dance record. The critic also hears a soul quality on “Improvvisa luce ad est” but does **not** classify the full record as soul; `soul` omitted. Source phrase “MOR rock” is adult-contemporary orientation, represented conservatively through soft rock/pop rock rather than adding an invented token. Experimental 1 reflects a slightly wider production palette, not experimental composition.

**Language, voice, collaborators.** Original album includes bilingual Italian/English “Più che puoi” with Cher: original release `it|en`, not two separate album rows. Eros’s nasal/reedy voice remains the principal voice, Cher's dramatic lead guest a feature but not a new `voice` token. `sounds_like=Bryan Adams|Richard Marx` is an editorial cross-album comparison only, **not** proof of a musical influence. `influenced_by` remains blank, not filled from producer/guest identities.

**Editions / date.** Official site lists 12 original musical tracks; some enhanced CD editions add a **CD-ROM video/software section** that is not an extra studio song. Release dates in stores vary (Rockol contemporary preview: scheduled October 26, Spotify digital catalogue: October 30, some AllMusic editions: November 7), so this phase records 2000 only. Spanish *Estilo libre* is a separate-language counterpart and excluded here.
