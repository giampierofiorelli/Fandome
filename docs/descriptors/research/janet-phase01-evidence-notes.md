# Janet Jackson — Phase 01: album-by-album evidence notes

The eight 0–5 scales below are **editorial judgments anchored to the project guide**, not numerical measurements from sources. The instrument and production lists are **credit/interview-led**; they are not predicted from genre. An empty `influenced_by` means that album-specific influence evidence was insufficient, not that the album had no influences. Source keys resolve to complete URLs in `janet_phase01_sources.csv`.

## Janet Jackson (1982) — `janet-janetjackson`

**Sound and genres.** [j82spin] calls the debut early-1980s post-disco R&B/boogie and describes a Moog bassline. [j82all] gives R&B/pop, dance-pop and disco characterization; [j82apple] independently characterizes R&B-pop and the horn-driven single “Young Love”. [j82herb] lists disco, while [j82spin] specifically treats “Come Give Your Love to Me” as the album's pop/rock *outlier*, not a dominant genre. `poprock` is accordingly last. `boogie` is electronic dance-oriented R&B here, **not** the unrelated token `boogiewoogie`.

**Instruments, names, production.** The A&M release credits [j82am] list bassists (Bobby Watson, James Jamerson Jr., Leon Sylvers III), drummers (André Fischer, John Robinson), synthesizer players, keyboards, guitars, percussion, handclaps, and strings. [j82spin] identifies René Moore's Moog bass; `synthbass` and production `moog` are supported specifically by this account, not by the genre. [j82am] explicitly credits Jerry Hey with horn arranging, Gene Dozier with string-and-horn arranging and Benjamin Wright with string arranging: `hornarrangements|stringarrangements`. `brass` is corroborated by Apple's horn-led “Young Love”. Five credited album producers are René Moore, Angela Winbush, Bobby Watson, Foster Sylvers and Jerry Weaver [j82am][j82dork]. No instrument-specific drum-machine, digital model or vocal processing is asserted.

**Voice and words.** Apple describes youthful, bubbly vocals; the list `soft|clear` is the restrained reading (not powerhouse belting). Romantic songs dominate; `love` is safer than adding unsupported political or autobiographical topics. `sounds_like` identifies broad sonic neighbors editorially, **not documented influences**. `influenced_by` remains empty: personnel and stylistic neighbors do not qualify.

**Eight judgments:** energy 3 (dance songs plus slow material); electronic 2 (synths/Moog, numerous live rhythm players); mood 4 (sunny, bright); theatrical 1 (little dramatized narrative); experimental 1 (conventional pop structures); polish 4 (finished mainstream studio arrangements); tempo 3 (mixed tempos); groove 4 (boogie/post-disco dance focus). No landmark flag despite historical importance as the debut: later records define her identity more clearly [j82all][j82spin].

**Disagreement/caveat.** Public databases assign differing *calendar dates* to 1982 issues (original stock numbers, digital storefront display dates, later pressings). The reliable shared claim is original release **year 1982**. Don't treat pop/rock single as characteristic of the whole LP.

## Dream Street (1984) — `janet-dreamstreet`

**Sound and genres.** [d84apple] describes urban electro-funk, synth-oriented R&B and a soft youthful pop delivery. [d84aoty] labels dance-pop, contemporary R&B, synth funk, teen pop and freestyle; [d84press] supplies synth-pop as a label for a 1984 vinyl edition. [d84allrelease] directly calls “Pretty Boy” Minneapolis funk and identifies conventional pop ballads. This permits the specific genres to co-exist rather than collapsing electro-funk/synth-funk/synth-pop into one. `freestyle` and `funk` appear late because the evidence is secondary or narrower in scope. The `identity` theme is tied only to the title song's fame narrative and is lower in prominence than romance [d84spin]; keep separate from later *Control*'s autobiographical emancipation.

**Instruments and production.** [d84am] original-release archive credits John Barnes with drum programming, keyboards and Synclavier; Marlon Jackson with drum programming; Greg Phillinganes on synth; Arthur Barrow on bass/keyboards; Jonathan Moffett on drums; Richie Zito on guitar. [d84soul] explicitly names Moog bass and Moog synthesizer parts; `synthbass` and `moog` here refer to those track/session credits, not an assertion of pervasive Moog production. Generic `guitar` is used because the principal credit does not safely identify guitar type. `synclavier` is a named machine, not a synonym for sampler. Four producer credits: Marlon Jackson, Jesse Johnson, Giorgio Moroder and Pete Bellotte [d84am]. **John Barnes is only listed as an associate producer, so excluded.** Michael Jackson is a backing singer; he is not treated as album producer or influence [d84am][d84apple].

**Eight judgments:** energy 3 (active dance songs with ballads); electronic 4 (synth-forward); mood 4 (playful and glossy, though breakup songs); theatrical 1; experimental 1; polish 4; tempo 3; groove 4. Vocal `soft|clear` draws on Apple's editorial description. `love|heartbreak` includes the ballads, `identity` narrowly the title track. No asserted documented album-specific influence; an actual guest, producer or resemblance cannot populate `influenced_by`.

**Caveat.** Genre metadata on genre-aggregation sites is not as strong as producer interviews or original credits. The original-release date is inconsistently given by archives, music services and catalogs (September/October 1984); only the original release year is stored. The album is distinct from Janet's much later self-titled `janet.`.

## Control (1986) — `janet-control`

**Sound and genres.** [c86aoty] describes dance-pop, contemporary R&B, new jack swing, Minneapolis Sound, synth-funk and synthpop. [c86all] calls it classic pop with strong R&B beat architecture. [c86acclaimed] tags **“The Pleasure Principle” specifically** as freestyle, so that genre goes last. `newjackswing` is a legitimate retrospective musical description, **not a claim that the term was current at recording in 1985**. `minneapolissound` is a distinct sourced genre absent from the repository's current tokens.

**Instruments and production.** In first-person [c86rb], Jimmy Jam names the **Linn Drum** as the core machine, explicitly rejects using the Oberheim DMX for this record, and identifies the **Ensoniq Mirage** sampling keyboard on “Nasty” and “When I Think of You” and Yamaha **DX7** bass sounds on “When I Think of You”. He describes analog tape and tape overload for controlled distortion and a real guitar (an old Telecaster) on “When I Think of You”. Therefore `drumprogramming|linndrum|sampling|ensoniqmirage|synthprogramming|dx7|analogtape|distortion` are documentary; `oberheimdmx` is **deliberately excluded**. The release credits [c86am] also document piano, saxophone (Troy Anthony), guitar, acoustic guitar (elsewhere independently credited), percussion, keyboards and synths. There is no license to assume MIDI keyboard sequencing just because drum patterns were programmed: Jam says the keyboard parts were *played* [c86rb].

**Producers.** The credit field includes **Janet Jackson, Jimmy Jam and Terry Lewis** as core credited producers/co-producers, **Monte Moir** as track producer, and track co-producers **Jellybean Johnson, Spencer Bernard, Steve Wiese** [c86am][c86qobuz][c86mb]. John McClain is an **executive producer**, hence excluded. `sounds_like` is an editorial sonic comparison; these are not established album influences. The only defensible album-specific entry in `influenced_by` is **KC and the Sunshine Band**: Jimmy Jam explicitly says their music inspired the *beat* of “When I Think of You” [c86rb]. His report that Janet was a fan of The Time is documented, but is not alone enough to treat The Time as an explicit influence on this album.

**Voice and lyrics.** [c86all] and [c86rb] support assertive lead vocals, soft moments and spoken passages in the title track. `powerful` signifies assertive delivery, not unusually high vocal range. `identity` leads because the title track frames independence; relationship and sensuality songs follow. The theme `political` is not imported from *Rhythm Nation*.

**Eight judgments:** energy 4 (emphatic dance tracks); electronic 4 (machine-led but guitars/other instruments); mood 3 (defiance and romance); theatrical 3 (spoken assertion and dramatic beat stops); experimental 2 (inventive sampled sonics in accessible verse/chorus forms); polish 5; tempo 4; groove 5. `landmark=1` on multiple critic and producer accounts [c86all][c86rb].

**Disagreement/caveat.** Credits can vary by pressing and data aggregators. The producers column errs on inclusion of *explicitly credited track co-producers*, not vocal-production or remix-only staff. The Mirage model is preserved separately from Emulator and generic samplers.

## Rhythm Nation 1814 (1989) — `janet-rhythmnation1814`

**Sound and genres.** The Library of Congress study by Ayanna Dozier [r89loc] expressly lists **glam rock, pop, new jack swing, hip-hop and R&B**, and notes that “Black Cat” is the glam-rock excursion. [r89pitch] describes synth-funk, funk/metal intersections and industrial-sounding interludes; the album's heavy programmed groove does not make every song industrial. [r89aoty] gives new jack swing and dance-pop as primary and post-industrial, Minneapolis Sound, sophisti-pop and pop soul as secondary classifications. `postindustrial` is therefore **texture** only, not dominant composition. [r89spin] labels the “Black Cat” song hard rock. Janet's own reprinted account [r89janet] names “Black Cat” a **rock-funk** song: preserved exactly as `rockfunk` (a new genre token), assigned *some tracks*. Distinct `glamrock`, `hardrock`, `rockfunk` are limited to one rock-centered track. The album explicitly moves between political anthems, club songs and romantic ballads.

**Instruments.** Original-release personnel [r89am] credit Janet with keyboards, Jam with keys/bass/piano/percussion, Terry Lewis with bass/percussion, Jellybean Johnson with guitar/drums/percussion, plus strings/violin/viola/cello, harp and Herb Alpert's trumpet. The Ensoniq Mirage is a sampling keyboard, supporting `sampler` [r89rb]. `electricguitar` is supported by the central electric-guitar arrangement of “Black Cat” [r89janet][r89spin]. Session strings and trumpet are documented but **not** as album-wide primary sonic sources; placed late.

**Production technology.** Jimmy Jam identifies the **E-mu SP-1200** for “Miss You Much”, “Escapade” and “Love Will Never Do” [r89rb]. [r89reverb] names **Oberheim OB-8** writing keyboard and **Sequential Circuits Drumtraks** tuned for machine/industrial percussion textures; these are entered as *specific new* tokens `oberheimob8` and `drumtraks`, not as generic `oberheim`. The **Ensoniq Mirage** survives from the earlier album [r89rb]. Jam says recording was on analog tape and that the keyboards were not sequenced [r89rb]; **do not** mark `sequencer` as an instrument or imply keyboard-sequencing production. Sampling is explicit for the Sly & the Family Stone loop [r89rb][r89loc]. Additional cymbals/percussion overdubs are explicitly described in [r89reverb]; the [r89am] string-arranger credit supports `stringarrangements`. Drumtraks emulation of clattering objects supports `sonicmanipulation`; no unsupported field-recording source is claimed. The **Linn Drum** also appears on the title track; the overall album is *not* wholly Linn programmed [r89reverb].

**Documented influences vs samples.** Sly and the Family Stone's “Thank You” provided a sampled riff and suggested the title-track rhythm [r89rb], but Fandome's rule explicitly excludes **sampled records** from `influenced_by`. Marvin Gaye's *What's Going On* is frequently **compared** with this album [r89rb][r89reverb], yet comparison is not proof it shaped its composition. Neither is included. `sounds_like` may include Sly, Public Enemy or Prince as *editorial sonic comparisons*, not claims of artistic influence. `influenced_by` is therefore empty.

**Producers and thematic split.** Jimmy Jam, Terry Lewis and Janet Jackson are credited as producers/coproducers; Jellybean Johnson co-produced “Black Cat” [r89am][r89janet][r89book]. John McClain is executive producer only, excluded. The original artist statement explicitly discusses racism, drugs, illiteracy and poverty alongside a wish to unite through music [r89janet]; [r89loc] discusses the album's movement from social issues to sensual and love songs. `social|political|identity|love|heartbreak|sensuality|introspection` reflects the contrast, without pretending every track is political. `spoken|choral` corresponds to the manifesto and group response interludes, not an operatic lead.

**Eight judgments:** energy 4 (heavy anthems, mitigated by ballads); electronic 4 (machines and synths with acoustic guitar/strings); mood 3 (political urgency offset by joy and romance); theatrical 4 (manifesto, interludes, dramatic framing); experimental 3 (interleaved storytelling, uncommon genre shifts, sound design); polish 5; tempo 3 (dance material plus multiple slow ballads); groove 5. Landmark on both academic recognition [r89loc] and major criticism [r89pitch].

**Title/date caveat.** Original artwork is often catalogued as *Janet Jackson's Rhythm Nation 1814*, while Fandome currently uses *Rhythm Nation 1814*. Keep Fandome's title and `janet-rhythmnation1814`. Avoid turning the attached 1989 film, music videos or later remixes into separate studio albums.
