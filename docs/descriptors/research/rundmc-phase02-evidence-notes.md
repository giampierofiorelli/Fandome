# Run-D.M.C. — phase 02: album-level evidence and open questions

Sources and source-specific genre scope are mapped in `rundmc_phase02_sources.csv` and `rundmc_phase02_genre_evidence.csv`. **Eight 0–5 scores are editorial estimates** from reported production/arrangement and need independent listening review, not objective measurements. This is the final studio-album phase; only its three rows should be imported. Original album versions are the target.

## Back from Hell (1990) — `rundmc-backfromhell`

- **Stylistic hierarchy:** AllMusic and contemporary Los Angeles Times emphasize rougher hardcore, heavy spare beats, scratches and street themes. SPIN and Albumism identify the divergence into New Jack Swing/R&B on the notable singles “Pause” and “Faces”; those tracks justify `newjackswing` in third position without turning the whole record into an R&B album. The original LA Times finds a comparatively isolated relapse into rap-rock on the title track; Louder identifies Stone Roses/Minor Threat material on “What’s It All About.” `raprock` is therefore strictly a track-level tail.
- **Instruments:** Jam Master Jay/Jason Mizell and Bobby Walker are **credited on drums**; Mizell and Stanley Brown on **keyboards** (Qobuz/original sleeve); Tony Battaglia on **guitar** (Qobuz/AllMusic); the 1990 LA Times documents **scratching** supporting `turntables`. Albumism separately identifies a **saxophone** solo on “Pause” by Stanley Brown. A particular drum-machine model is NOT specified and `drummachine` is not asserted from genre stereotypes. Bass lines could be replayed or sampled: do not assert a separately performed `bass` without verified credit.
- **Production:** The contemporary LA Times identifies sampling and scratches explicitly; `sampling` is supported, but neither `sp1200` nor another sampler model is recorded by the source. Qobuz lists a number of engineers as remixers, which is not proof this album was a remix project. No corresponding `remixing` token.
- **Producers:** The 1990 original LP credits **Run-D.M.C. and Jam Master Jay** as producers and Russell Simmons as **executive producer**. Qobuz track-by-track credits additionally identify **Frankie Inglese**, **Russell Simmons** (co-producer on “What’s It All About”), **Glen E. Friedman** (co-producer on the same track), **Stanley Brown**, **Davy D**, **Larry Smith**, and **David Reeves** as **co-producers on specific songs**. They are included because the requested definition includes documented co-production, not because they performed other roles. The original album-level sleeve does not enumerate every song-specific collaborator, so Qobuz is the source for these names. Russell's executive role by itself would not qualify.
- **Voice/lyrics:** Run and D.M.C. dominate with rhythmic emphatic rap; Aaron Hall’s sung guest part on “Don’t Stop” justifies secondary `clear`. Social content includes the anti-drug message of “Pause”, urban conditions on “The Ave.” and “Faces”; party music appears on “Party Time”. Jam Master Jay raps on some tracks. There is no sourced *album-specific* musician stated by the group as an influence; `influenced_by` is blank, and rock *samples* are NOT evidence of declared influences.
- **Criticism:** AllMusic/1990 Los Angeles Times are relatively sympathetic to its tougher sound; Albumism and SPIN criticize the New Jack Swing detour. This is a genuine track-level tension in style as well as reception.

## Down with the King (1993) — `rundmc-downwiththeking`

- **Genres:** AOTY classifies the album as East Coast/Hardcore hip-hop, Boom Bap and Jazz Rap; AllMusic details jazzier, more ominous bass-led production with piano/organ and horn samples. `jazzrap` is a **new genre token** (not found in prior phase's canonical guide) that must be added to `scenes.ts` if absent at import. A contemporary LA Times critic explicitly describes the album as pop rap; it is included low-priority because other contemporary and retrospective reviews stress its aggressive edge. The single “What’s Next” with **Mad Cobra** provides the dancehall tail. “Big Willie”, with **Tom Morello**, is a track-local rap-rock case.
- **Instrumentation:** Bass and **electric guitar** (including Tom Morello) are listed in AllMusic credits. AllMusic and Albumism describe **organ**, **piano**, **keyboards**, **drums** and sampled horns (horn *samples*, not documented live brass players); turntables from Jam Master Jay's work and scratching. Instruments refer to documented sounds/credits across multiple tracks. `brass` not included because sampled horn figures are not proof of a live brass section. `saxophone` likewise not separately asserted.
- **Production:** Albumism gives concrete examples of reused records and recording extracts, including the musical-theatre guitar intro in the lead single and horn samples in tracks made by the Bomb Squad: `sampling`. Neither SP-1200 nor MPC hardware is established as the record's machine by the inspected sources. No hardware tokens invented. Producers' different approaches account for the stylistic changes within the album.
- **Production personnel:** Producer credits in AllMusic and Qobuz identify **Run-D.M.C., Jam Master Jay, Pete Rock, Q-Tip, EPMD, DJ Kay Gee, The Bomb Squad, Daniel Shulman, Chyskills, Jermaine Dupri, Clifton “Specialist” Dillon**, and AllMusic credits **Rich July** as producer. The field uses **EPMD** as collective credit instead of repeating Erick Sermon and Parrish Smith as duplicate identities; **Pete Rock** rather than guest rapper C.L. Smooth. Albumism occasionally attributes production of tracks differently from the distributor's track sequence, most visibly EPMD/Kay Gee and Bomb Squad/Run-D.M.C. credits; all included parties have documentary production credits somewhere on the LP, but per-track reconciliation should be checked against original liner notes before marking reviewed. A producer's presence is NOT proof that their own earlier albums inspired this one.
- **Sonic comparison:** AllMusic and Albumism compare the new harsher flow/texture with **Onyx** and **Naughty by Nature**; Albumism also likens a hook/beat to **Kris Kross**; AllMusic mentions **Das EFX** borrowed rap patterns. For Onyx, *Bacdafucup* is the contemporaneous cited album; for Naughty by Nature, *19 Naughty III*. Guests Onyx, Pete Rock, Mad Cobra, etc. are not automatically influences. Album-specific `influenced_by` is left blank (no independent firsthand statement establishing a specific musical source as influence rather than collaboration/sampling).
- **Lyrics/scales:** “Down with the King” and “To the Maker” establish `spiritual`; party/identity themes coexist. Mood 3 is a compromise between celebratory comeback cuts and darker sampled production. The most upbeat pop-rap reading is explicitly attributed to the 1993 Los Angeles Times, not treated as consensus.

## Crown Royal (2001) — `rundmc-crownroyal`

- **Release-year discrepancy:** AllMusic's top metadata displays **12 Oct 1999**, and Metacritic's details show **27 Feb 2001**. However original physical **2001 LP/CD** variants are listed even on AllMusic, and a first-person April 2001 interview says the original planned **1999** release was delayed and the actual album appeared **3 April 2001**. Catalogue year is unequivocally **2001**, never 1999. Do not use a 2003 deluxe version's bonus “It’s Tricky 2003” as part of 2001 original track list.
- **Genres:** The LP includes hip-hop tracks (e.g. “Queens Day”, “Simmons Incorporated”) and several rap-rock / alternative-rock / rap-metal experiments (“Them Girls”, “Rock Show”, “Here We Go 2001”). **A.V. Club** explicitly uses *rap-metal* and **AllMusic** describes an alternative rock departure, so precise terms stay in the tail. “Let’s Stay Together” with **Jagged Edge**, keyboards, guitars and a choir is the source for `contemporaryrnb`, limited to that track. The whole album is not a nu-metal or R&B record.
- **Instruments and recording:** Original US LP and AllMusic give **guitar** (Eric Ambel, Tony Fredianelli, Donny Mathis), **bass** (Tony Fredianelli), **drums** (Brain on “Rock Show”), **organ** (Irwin Fisch), **keyboards** (Richard Johnson) and **turntables** (DJ Flare). These are verifiable performance credits, not deductions from `rapmetal`.
- **Production:** Qobuz records Randy Allen and Jam Master Jay as **programmers** on numerous original tracks, supporting `programming`; “It’s Over” and “Rock Show” use identifiable reused materials according to RapReviews and NME, supporting `sampling`. Nothing inspected identifies a specific drum machine or sampler, so do not assign one. Rap/clear melodic singing/one choir-backed track support secondary `clear|choral` voice categories.
- **Production credits:** The album has numerous true track producers: Jam Master Jay, Randy Allen, LaMarquis Jefferson, Jermaine Dupri, DJ Lethal (AllMusic/original pressing; Qobuz credits him at least as composer), Kid Rock, Mike Bradford, Dante Ross & John Gamble (credited together as **Stimulated Dummies** in some sources), Jason Carmer, Stephan Jenkins, DJ Homicide (co-producer), Tadone Wilson (co-producer), Mike Rapley (co-producer), and Run-D.M.C. (co-production on “Simmons Incorporated”). Run's own credit is subsumed by **Run-D.M.C.**, rather than duplicating the name. Qobuz's track roles sometimes differ from the printed original sleeve (particularly DJ Lethal/DJ Homicide); importers should retain the caveat and verify actual liner notes. **L.A. Reid is executive producer only**, excluded.
- **Sonic comparisons:** The `sounds_like` choices reflect the documented guest-heavy alternative crossover sound: Limp Bizkit, Third Eye Blind, Sugar Ray and Kid Rock. These are editorial comparisons to sound, supported by AllMusic, A.V. Club and NME, *not* a claim these invited artists influenced the group. They also are actual featured collaborators; this field must NOT be read as influence evidence. The album has insufficient first-person, album-specific independent musical-influence testimony, so `influenced_by` remains blank.
- **Personnel limitation:** Contemporary critics disagree on precisely **two versus three** D.M.C. appearances, likely depending on whether very short cameos count. The safe inference is that his participation is limited across the LP; neither exact count is asserted in the row. D.M.C.'s lack of presence is a contextual factor in critical reception, not a reason to remove it from the Run-D.M.C. discography.
