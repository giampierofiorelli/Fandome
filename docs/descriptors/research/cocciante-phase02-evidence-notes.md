# Riccardo Cocciante — Phase 02: independent evidence and editorial ratings

## General methodology

Use the original Italian 1976, 1978, 1979 and 1980 studio LP editions. Every CSV genre has a corresponding record in `cocciante_phase02_genre_evidence.csv`; critic-named one-track styles appear after the central genres except a lead single can appear within the top three. Eight ratings are editorial judgements 0–5, not facts extracted from metadata. The `source` URLs are explicitly recorded; sources are distinguished from speculation. We use `songwriter` as the Italian cantautore album context, and `stornello` for one 1978 track only. `krautpop` is the exact genre a critic named for a 1980 title track. Both `krautpop` and `stornello` were verified as ALREADY registered in current `src/data/scenes.ts`; no token additions required. All `influenced_by` remain empty because no artist/producer testimony links an external model to these exact albums.

### Concerto per Margherita (1976; RCA Italiana TPL1-1220; 10 tracks)

- **Original credits:** producer **Marco Luberti** (also lyricist); **Vangelis / Evangelos Papathanassiou** arranger, studio engineer and project coordinator, **not producer** on the original sleeve or RCA/Sony track credits. English Chamber Choir conducted by **Guy Protheroe**. Recorded partly at Nemo Studios in London and RCA Studios Rome. Vangelis is not added to `influenced_by`, as an arranger is a collaborator.
- **Distinctive sound:** Rockol describes electronically rich Vangelis keyboards, introductory synth swells on Primavera, drums and big choral finale. Do not fabricate a Moog brand, theremin, full strings session or whole-record live-band personnel: Vangelis electronic keyboards and drums are the documented sound sources; the English Chamber Choir goes in `choirvocals`, **not** `orchestra`. No unsupported `stringarrangements` entry. The orchestral-pop descriptor is a critical *style*, not a fabricated string credit.
- **Genres:** original pressing explicitly Pop / Soft Rock / Chanson / Pop Rock; review calls the album almost a concept LP (not proven continuous narrative and not `rockopera`), elaborately arranged for a singer-songwriter, while AllMusic calls Margherita a *power ballad*. `orchestralpop` describes broad Vangelis/choir architecture. `powerballad` fourth because a named 1/10 song, even if major lead hit.
- **Scales in order energy/electronic/mood/theatrical/experimental/polish/tempo/groove:** **3/2/2/4/3/4/2/1.** Energy 3 due to climactic choruses versus calmer narrative tracks; electronic 2 due to Vangelis synth keyboards within song-based record; mood 2 contrasts love and renewal with Inverno, Violenza, loss and death; theatrical 4 for emotional belting and recurring dramatic choir; experimental 3 for album-cycle reprise and synth/chanson juxtaposition without true prog suite; polish 4 because Nemo/RCA studios and elaborate choirs; tempo 2 and groove 1 due to ballad-led pacing.
- **Voice:** `raspy|powerful` from Rockol's specific descriptions of raw raspy anger on Violenza and explosive Margherita phrasing; do not call it classically operatic. `sounds_like`: Rockol explicitly compares the **intro** of Sul bordo del fiume to Bee Gees' *I Started a Joke* (first appeared on *Idea*, hence `Bee Gees (Idea) [1/10]`), and the **music** of Violenza to *Il Guardiano del Faro* (1/10). A passing Beach Boys theremin *resemblance* on Ancora does not justify an actual theremin instrument, so omitted. Neither critic comparison establishes historical influence.
- **Lyrics:** love, fraught relationships, sexual violence, changing seasons, mortality and persistence; no simplistic all-romantic mood.
- **Landmark 1:** Rockol expressly frames album and *Margherita* as career-defining classics and documents substantial original sales; AllMusic likewise marks the hit.

### Riccardo Cocciante (1978; RCA PL 31332; 9 tracks)

- **Original date:** **1978**, despite late-1977 single A mano a mano and DeBaser's 1977/78 contextual shorthand. RCA 1978 original pressing, Sony/Qobuz dated 1978 on all nine tracks, and Hit Parade Italia 1978 album chart agree. The later French-language *Richard Cocciante* release is not an extra original album.
- **Producers:** **Marco Luberti AND Giacomo Tosti** on all nine tracks, as spelled out consistently by Sony/Qobuz; Giovanni Tommaso is conductor/arranger, **not producer**. Giuseppe Bernardini is engineer, not producer. Album's musicians are **not** fully documented in accessible sleeve transcriptions; no speculative bassist/drummer/horn tokens.
- **Instruments:** `piano` directly documented by original *A mano a mano* arrangement transcription, credited to Cocciante; `orchestra` specifically named in DeBaser's Notturno / Storie discussion; `electricguitar` in exactly two songs according to DeBaser. Do not add `strings` or specific orchestra sections without individual credits. Production column blank instead of guessing recording technology, orchestral string scoring, chorus or studio effects.
- **Genres:** Pop and singer-songwriter with orchestrated setting; critic explicitly notes tentative pop-rock on two tracks, and traditional *stornello* form only on **Stornello d’amore** (1/9). `ballad` is the mournful slow piano *A mano a mano*. The `stornello` token may already have been requested for Gabriella Ferri phase 01 but is still absent from currently checked `scenes.ts`, so treat it as **one shared token addition**, not a fresh incompatible variant.
- **Scales:** **2/1/1/4/1/3/2/1**: predominantly slow/intimate, acoustically led piano/orchestra with an electric guitar on only two tracks; explicitly melancholy; excessive theatrical orchestration noted by critic; conventional forms, polished enough but less elaborate than Vangelis 1976, limited dance rhythms.
- **Voice:** blank rather than importing a singer-wide descriptor without an album-specific source. **Landmark 0:** *A mano a mano* is canonical but LP's whole-album landmark status not established by critic; Hit Parade rankings alone do not suffice. **Important non-influence:** Rino Gaetano's famous later rendition of the song is a **cover of this recording**, never `influenced_by` or a sonic precedent. No critic-sourced musical artist comparison documented for this album, so `sounds_like` blank.

### ...E io canto (1979; RCA PL 31421; 9 tracks)

- **Producers:** **Marco Luberti** explicitly named on original gatefold LP and Sony original-song credits. **Dino Kappa** marked *consultant*, not producer; **Riccardo Cocciante** named arranger, piano, songwriter and backing singer, **not producer** on original 1979 credits. 
- **Instrument credits:** AllMusic original-musician index: piano Cocciante; keyboards Maurizio Guarini and Alberto Visentini, guitar Carlo Pennisi, bass Dino Kappa, drums Derek Wilson, percussion Alex Serra, backing vocals Cathy, Guarini and Cocciante. RCA/Sony original *Io canto* and *Il treno* credits independently replicate these names. `choirvocals` reflects group backing vocals, not a literal orchestral choir.
- **Genres:** original RCA LP classified Pop, Chanson in specialist physical pressing listings; AllMusic uses broad Latin/Pop-Rock. `poprock` describes the full band and catalogued shelf style; we **exclude** `latin` because AllMusic's broad parent genre does not establish any specific Latin rhythm on the original nine tracks. No unverified `jazz`, `disco` or `newwave` just because session personnel worked in those genres elsewhere.
- **Scales:** **3/1/2/4/2/3/3/2**: mixed narrative-driven rock-tinged arrangements and softer *Io canto*, singer's high-intensity vocal delivery, more direct than Vangelis 1976; predominantly electric band but **not electronically generated** (keys ≠ documented synth), emotional colour shifts between social/personal tragedy in *Il treno* and quiet title song, moderate production finish and rhythm.
- **Voice:** `powerful`: VICE notes Cocciante's characteristically aggressive vocal treatment in this album period while singling out *Io canto* as calmer. No opera training, robotic vocal processing, rap or named microphone effects.
- **Topics:** *Il treno* concerns destructive life choices; *Carnevale* catastrophe, title song the expressive self, *Fiaba* narratives. Critic's interpretation not treated as direct artist intent. **Landmark 0**: title track is popular but the 1979 LP not independently described as a defining overall record. No named music artist similarity/album-specific influence proven.
- **Edition caution:** AllMusic's prominent date `April 15, 2003` is a CD **reissue date**. Original 1979 RCA PL31421 is corroborated by Recordsale and independent RCA vinyl listings. Likewise AllMusic sometimes displays *Le mani in tasca* as metadata rather than first track; the original LP has **9 tracks starting Le mani in tasca**.

### Cervo a primavera (1980; RCA PL 31546; 10 tracks)

- **Original date & personnel:** Sony/RCA album, first issued February 1980; credits on original LP distinguish three production participants: **Shel Shapiro** principal producer (8 tracks, plus co-producer on the two other tracks), **Serge Perathoner** main producer on **Non è stato per caso** and **Carolina amatissima**, and **David Rose** co-producer for those two. All three are documented producers under Fandome rules; `producers=Shel Shapiro|Serge Perathoner|David Rose`. RCA/Sony metadata uses Shel Shapiro's full legal name **Norman David Shapiro**; it is the SAME person, never add twice. Giovanni Tommaso and David Rose also arrange, not additional producers. Mogol wrote lyrics for 9 of 10 tracks, **not producer**. Avoid imported out-of-album track credits from collections.
- **Original instruments:** Sony/Legacy explicitly notes Alessandro Centofanti synthesizers on title track, acoustic guitars, David Rose violin on Piero, piano and marimba on Il soufflé con le banane, electric guitars and drums on Suonare suonare. VICE adds Footing synthbass and funky guitar, Piero strings and bass/drums, Ci vuol coraggio jazz keyboard playing, Gomma tense synths and bells, Carolina amatissima electric piano, fretless bass and violin, the slide guitar and chorused electric piano on several tracks. Original-era personnel named in VICE include Giovanni Tommaso, Roberto Colombo, Centofanti, Paolo Donnarumma, Perathoner, Shel Shapiro and Stefano Pulga; don't infer any particular musician played a specific instrument merely from their other bands. `mallets` maps explicitly named **marimba**; no new instrument token is required. `strings` from Piero and `stringarrangements` from its arranged passage; `choirvocals` from layered choruses on Tu sei il mio amico carissimo (VICE).
- **Genres:** Unlike previous albums, this one is a documented hybrid. Sony calls it expressive, high-quality pop with rock textures. VICE analyzes *new-wave drums/guitars* (Piero / Non è stato per caso), *fusion* jazz harmonies (Ci vuol coraggio), funk guitars (Footing) and groove (Gomma), disco interlude (Piero), and expressly calls title song **krautpop**. Track-specific terms are ranked after `pop|songwriter|poprock`; the 1/10 title hit gets `krautpop` near tail rather than abusing a dominant position. No generic whole-record `rock`, `punk` (VICE means lyrical provocation), `psychedelicrock`, `citypop`, `neapolitansong` or `prog` imported from stray commentary/cover songs. `jazzfusion` captures VICE's explicit jazz+fusion account but not a claim that the entire album is fusion.
- **Scales:** **3/3/4/3/4/4/3/3**: energetic drums and sparkling rhythms but many mellow songs (energy 3, tempo 3); actual synth and synth bass not predominant entire record (electronic 3); clearly more hopeful/bright (mood 4), restrained vocal setting compared with 1976 (theatrical 3); dense genre-crossing arrangements, unusual new-wave/disco + songwriting (experimental 4); sophisticated layered sound and dual sessions (polish 4); intermittent funk/disco with restrained softer songs (groove 3).
- **Voice:** `raspy|powerful`, specifically described for this 1980 title and album by Sony/Legacy, Rockol and VICE. The LP does not become a female/gospel choral record because of backing vocal textures.
- **`sounds_like`:** VICE explicitly likens **Non è stato per caso** to **Ultravox** (1/10), **Piero**'s sound to **Martha and the Muffins** (1/10), and **Carolina amatissima** to **Japan** (1/10). These are later comparisons, NOT creative influence evidence. VICE also invokes Style Council (formed 1983): its later formation confirms retroactive resemblance cannot mean influence. PFM is the **source of a song covered on track 10**, so **NEVER** in `influenced_by` just for the cover; likewise Shel Shapiro's Rokes/Perathoner's Transit Express are past bands, not influences. Mogol had previously worked with Battisti, which is a songwriting collaboration, **not** an album-specific Cocciante musical influence.
- **Landmark 1:** Sony calls the 1980 record a reset with new Mogol partnership, Rockol describes a classic hit; VICE explicitly calls the album a career watershed, not just successful single. (This is an editorial flag grounded in those accounts.)

## Source-quality cautions

- Qobuz distributes Sony's original Italian phonograms but its artist *collection* combines 1974–1982 material. Quote only credits bearing the exact year/track and original issue; don't infer a producer from a compilation page heading.
- Do not mistake VICE's informal statement that Vangelis 'produced' 1976 for an LP credit; both original RCA and Sony digital explicitly credit **Marco Luberti** Producer while Vangelis was arranger/engineer/coordinator.
- 1978 original album is **1978** although A mano a mano single was issued in December 1977. 1979 album is **1979** even when AllMusic's page displays a 2003 reissue heading.
- 1980 co-production credits on specific tracks are included because they are co-producer (not 'additional' or merely executive); Claude should validate against the exact original PL 31546 sleeve if it becomes available.
- Source-supported one-track subgenres and sound comparisons are weighted explicitly `Name [1/10]`. Record instrumentation is an album-level union of documented passages, **not** a claim every track features every instrument.
