# Ariana Grande — Phase 01 | Album-specific evidence notes

## Research scope and constraints
Four original studio albums only: 2013–2018. All are existing Fandome catalogue items, none currently has an Ariana descriptor CSV on the checked `descriptors` branch. No previous-artist rows. Live releases excluded by owner instruction. *Christmas Kisses* (2013) and *Christmas & Chill* (2015) are seasonal EPs, not selected as documented landmark releases; *Bang Bang* is not a standalone Ariana album. Original editions take priority over reissues and bonus-track editions. The `producers` list includes credited producers/co-producers appearing on the original album but excludes personnel credited solely as writers, mixers, engineers, featured vocalists or executive producers. Avoid conflating every producer with a whole-album credit: contributions are track-level. Alternative spellings and credit database inconsistencies require importer review.

### Interpreting categories
Every token in the row has a corresponding `genre_evidence.csv` entry. `scope=whole album`, `substantial`, `some tracks`, `texture` are deliberately different levels of support: *Honeymoon Avenue* doo-wop does not turn all of *Yours Truly* into a doo-wop album; *Be Alright* does not make every track deep-house. Genre ordering reflects salience. No imposed maximum for any multivalue column, just the 15-word `notes` limit. Editorial 0–5 scale scores are evidence-grounded estimates, not acoustic measurements. The only new tokens in this phase are `doowop` and `cloudpop` (which must be added to both Genre and GENRE_PARENTS/validator as applicable). Proposed parents: doowop → [rnb, pop], cloudpop → [pop, electronic].

## Yours Truly (2013) — ariana-yourstruly
**Evidence** Y_PF, Y_AM, Y_Q, Y_Q2, Y_RET. Pitchfork (Andrew Ryce, 2013) separates the 1950s doo-wop styling from its explicit 1990s hip-hop-soul orientation, modern programmed accents, the unusually orchestral beginning and a sole late EDM track. AllMusic (Matt Collar) emphasizes skilled melisma, commercial production and retro R&B. The Guardian later describes the 1960s girl-group / nineties R&B combination. Album-wide no claim of pure doo-wop, EDM or jazz.

**Eight scales:** energy **3**: mid-tempo material and lively Baby I/Piano contrasting slower ballads; electronic **2**: studio beats share focus with horns, piano, strings and traditional R&B instrumentation; mood **4**: bright flirtation and romance with some wistful tracks; theatrical **3**: virtuosic belting but no fully theatrical concept; experimental **2**: nostalgic methods and accessible structures; polish **4**: precisely layered, clean studio work; tempo **3**: predominantly midtempo plus dance songs and ballads; groove **3**: hip-hop soul pulses and selected dance tracks, not an uninterrupted club album.

**Vocals** powerful, clear, soft: strong melismatic lead spans lower close-miked moments and substantial high belting. **Lyrics** love and heartbreak recur; no unsupported all-album party theme. **Influence** Mariah Carey is not merely a voice comparison: AllMusic and Pitchfork tie the album's deliberate nineties pop-R&B template to the Carey lineage. **sounds_like** other acts are proposed listening comparisons, not demonstrated direct influence. **Producer list** assembled from licensed Qobuz/UMG track roles; do not merge artists whose only role is composer or remixer.

**Critical disagreement:** Pitchfork values the unusual inter-era production but faults conventional songwriting; AllMusic is much more enthusiastic about the polished arrangements and voice. Both agree on the broad musical architecture.

## My Everything (2014) — ariana-myeverything
**Evidence** M_PF, M_AM, M_SL, M_GU, M_GU2, M_Q, M_MB. AllMusic and Pitchfork document the pop/EDM turn. Slant explicitly calls parts electropop and hip-hop soul, while the Observer singles out *Break Free* as hi-NRG. AllMusic classifies original edition as dance-pop, contemporary R&B and pop. `electropop` is retained as distinct from `synthpop`.

**Eight scales:** energy **4**: beat-driven hit singles and arena-sized choruses moderated by several slow romantic tracks; electronic **4**: EDM, synthesizers and loops dominate the record's public sound but are not universal; mood **3**: ebullient release songs coexist with breakups, longing and reflective ballads; theatrical **3**: emphatic vocal/dramatic choruses amid standard chart-pop arrangements; experimental **2**: broad producer experimentation within familiar pop conventions; polish **5**: heavily processed, expensive-sounding multi-producer production; tempo **4**: dance-heavy highlights, with lower-tempo tracks preventing top anchor; groove **4**: clear club-oriented section but not continuous dance mix.

**Voices and lyrics:** soaring clear/powerful notes with softer close passages; relationship songs emphasize love, breakup and sensuality. **Influence** blank: similarity to Britney, Rihanna and The Weeknd is editorial/critical resonance, not direct creative influence proven for this album. **Production ambiguity:** David Guetta wrote *One Last Time* and is often advertised as a producer in general reviews, but the licensed track lists Carl Falk, Rami Yacoub, ILYA and Giorgio Tuinfort as producer/co-producer. Do not insert Guetta without separately verified track producer status. *Bang Bang* appears on deluxe configurations and does not determine the 12-track original row. The extensive production column reflects credited producers/co-producers across the original; verify alias Pop & Oak against Oak Felder/Pop Wansel if a different edition's credits are imported.

**Critical disagreement:** Pitchfork regards the embrace of hits as an arrival, Slant and Guardian describe a scattershot or impersonal album. This changes subjective `experimental`/`polish` interpretation but not the documented presence of programmed dance tracks.

## Dangerous Woman (2016) — ariana-dangerouswoman
**Evidence** D_PF, D_AM, D_GU, D_SL, D_Q, D_Q2. Pop-R&B underpins original tracks, with specific side genres: *Moonlight* doo-wop and jazzy inflections, *Be Alright* deep-house/1990s house, *Greedy* disco-funk, and *Into You* electro-driven. This is not a whole-album deep-house or jazz record. Slant and Guardian identify the precise examples. Qobuz disambiguates multiple credited producers on original tracks; `Billboard` means the musical producer alias of Mathieu Jomphe-Lépine, not the publication.

**Eight scales:** energy **4**: club tracks punctuated by substantial ballads; electronic **4**: programmed synthesizers and electronic bass dominate rhythm tracks, while live/torch details persist; mood **3**: sensual confidence balanced by darker longing; theatrical **3**: strong vocal drama particularly in Leave Me Lonely, with mostly mainstream pop delivery; experimental **2**: varied pastiche but conventional songs; polish **5**: tightly engineered maximalist pop studio textures; tempo **4**: up-tempo cluster offset by slow tracks; groove **4**: steady dance and R&B rhythms prominent throughout.

**Voice** powerful/clear/soft (distinct lead dynamics, not a claim that guest rappers define Ariana's voice). **Lyrics** sensuality, romance and emerging sexual independence; some heartache. **Influences** blank to avoid turning Slant's Elvis, Brandy or Nina Simone listening comparisons into documented production motives. **Critical disagreement:** Pitchfork says the seductive alter ego can feel safe or impersonal; Guardian and Slant praise genre-specific refinements.

## Sweetener (2018) — ariana-sweetener
**Evidence** S_PF, S_AM, S_GU, S_SL, S_NME, S_FAD, S_Q, S_WP. Distinguish Pharrell's skittering, unusual low-intensity beats from Martin/ILYA's mainstream pop tracks. NME explicitly describes cloud-pop and hip-hop beats. Pitchfork hears funk-lite, trap-era programming, doo-wop singing within R.E.M and gospel harmonies, AllMusic reports disco/EDM accents, Slant identifies track-specific reggae on God Is a Woman. *No Tears Left to Cry* is not a reliable proxy for the whole LP.

**Eight scales:** energy **3**: mostly controlled, elastic rhythms vs bangers; electronic **4**: loops, sub-bass, synths and digital beat programming dominant but with some live/string/gospel coloration; mood **4**: hope and intimate happiness foregrounded while anxiety and breakup material remain; theatrical **2**: restrained breathy delivery and sparse arrangements compared to prior albums, punctuated by occasional belts; experimental **3**: unconventional loops, subdivisions, spoken/pitched sound fragments amid highly accessible pop songs; polish **4**: precise but intentionally airy/less maximalist studio sound; tempo **3**: moderate across much of 15-track album; groove **3**: skittery head-nod tracks and some obvious dancefloor beats.

**Voice** soft, clear, powerful, plus occasional sung-spoken and rap-like lead phrasing documented in Pitchfork/Slant, not a full rap album. **Themes** self-reflection/anxiety, love, confidence/female agency, social/political cues (especially town-hall sample *The Light Is Coming*), relationship difficulties and sensuality. **Influenced_by** Imogen Heap: Washington Post reports Grande's own description of Heap as inspiring her, corroborated by direct reinterpretation of *Goodnight and Go*. Pharrell is credited as producer and thus excluded from this column even though his sonic imprint is central. **Producer list** follows explicitly headed producer credits per track in The FADER; executive producers are not counted.

**Critical disagreement:** Pitchfork praises the more personal album and unusual Pharrell loops. Guardian notes inconsistency across Pharrell tracks; Slant finds some writing metaphors unsuccessful. Because styles, instrumentation and the original tracklist agree between reviewers, `confidence=high` for descriptive evidence despite different ratings.
