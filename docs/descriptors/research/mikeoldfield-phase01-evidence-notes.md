# Mike Oldfield — phase 01: per-album evidence notes

Research basis: original versions of albums, not expanded anniversary editions. Numeric descriptors are editorial inferences grounded in the cited reviews, track analyses and credits. `sounds_like` values are **editorial sonic comparisons**, not artist-attributed influences. Blank fields mean that the evidence does not justify a claim.

## Tubular Bells (1973) — mikeoldfield-tubularbells

- **Genres**: AllMusic assigns prog-rock, art rock, progressive electronic, chamber music, classical crossover, experimental electronic and New Age. The official artist discography explicitly discusses prog and folk elements of the debut. `newage` is **retrospective**, not a claim that Oldfield or reviewers used the term in May 1973. The work is not a film soundtrack: *The Exorcist* reused a passage after it was composed.
- **Scales** (`3,1,2,4,4,3,2,1`): material varies from quiet piano/folk textures to loud guitar and bell climaxes, and is architecturally adventurous despite largely acoustic/electric instruments. The 20-minute-plus sides give low continuous tempo and weak dance groove. The constructed roll-call of instruments heightens theatricality. [AllMusic](https://www.allmusic.com/album/tubular-bells-mw0000191174); [official history](https://www.mikeoldfieldofficial.com/discography/).
- **Instruments**: original sleeve personnel at [Tubular.net](https://tubular.net/discography/TubularBells) and [MusicBrainz](https://musicbrainz.org/release/8af75d99-2ae3-444c-8130-a9f17107f8dd). `flageolet` is an *explicitly credited instrument*, not silently normalised to whistle; add as a new token. The original credits say 'mandolin-like guitar', so **do not** claim an actual mandolin was played. Double bass (Lindsay Cooper) is separate from Oldfield's electric bass. The voices are largely choral with Viv Stanshall's spoken English introduction; hence instrumental first and `language=en`. There are no sustained original lyrical themes.
- **Production**: multi-instrument overdubbing and the original 16-track analogue Ampex are documented by [Sound on Sound](https://www.soundonsound.com/techniques/classic-tracks-mike-oldfield-tubular-bells) and [Oldfield's Roland interview](https://www.tubular.net/articles/1999_06/Interview-with-Mike-Oldfield-from-Roland-PowerOn-magazine). The [Dark Star technical notes](https://www.mikeoldfield.org/tubular-bells) document half-speed guitar recording and fuzz guitar, supporting `varispeed` and `distortion`; Oldfield explicitly describes an organ-chord tape loop, supporting `tapeloops`. Farfisa is explicitly on the sleeve. Do not add synthesizers based on the later electronic reputation.
- **Producers**: the original album credits name Oldfield, Simon Heyworth and Tom Newman; do not demote the two co-producers to engineers, although their engineering contributions were significant.
- **Influence**: in [The Guardian interview (2014)](https://www.theguardian.com/music/2014/mar/20/mike-oldfield-interview-tubular-bells-drugs), Oldfield directly connects the piece's beginnings with music he listened to by Terry Riley and TONTO’s Expanding Head Band. These two are eligible for the row, unlike later critics' loose stylistic comparisons.
- **Criticism**: AllMusic's enthusiastic innovation framing coexists with later accounts that stress the album's eclectic, sometimes uneven sequence of musical ideas. `sounds_like` is simply an editorial similarity pointer, not an allegation of imitation.

## Hergest Ridge (1974) — mikeoldfield-hergestridge

- **Genres**: direct AllMusic album labels are Art Rock, Prog-Rock, Progressive Electronic and New Age. The New Age classification is retrospective. Pastoral character is a *texture*, not an unsourced claim that the album is a folk album.
- **Scales** (`2,1,3,3,3,3,2,0`): [Louder](https://www.loudersound.com/music/albums/mike-oldfield-hergest-ridge) and [AllMusic's Part Two analysis](https://www.allmusic.com/song/hergest-ridge-pt-2-mt0006283470) describe calm/bright pastoral regions contrasted with loud, repetitive, distorted guitar mass. It should not be given a high album-wide energy score solely on that climax. `groove=0` because the long suites lack dance-driven rhythms.
- **Instruments**: [original personnel](https://www.mikeoldfield.org/hergest-rridge) explicitly list organ families, guitars, mandolin, glockenspiel, sleigh bells, timpani, gong, oboes, trumpet, snare drum and string ensemble. `nutcracker` is a real item in the album credits and is marked as a **new instrument token**. The website says bass and tubular bells are only *believed* to appear: both are omitted. No model-specific synth is supported. Bedford conducted, but is not listed as a producer. The original credits name only Mike Oldfield as producer, Newman as assistant.
- **Production**: repeatedly layered guitars underpin the mid-Part-Two crescendo (overdubbing); the AllMusic song analysis identifies a distorted repeated passage. No later **remixing** token is applied to the original recording.
- **Versions**: 1974 stereo master, significantly stripped-down 1976 *Boxed* quadraphonic remix and 2010 revision differ in audible instrumentation. In particular some snare-drum parts were removed in the later mix. The row captures **the original 1974 recording**, not a later reissue: see [Tubular.net](https://tubular.net/discography/HergestRidge) and [Dark Star](https://www.mikeoldfield.org/hergest-rridge). Clodagh Simonds's syllables cannot responsibly be labelled with a natural language. Therefore `language` and `lyrics` blank.
- **Criticism**: contemporary mixed reception noted by Louder, while Sally Oldfield recalls the record as soothing. The row's mid-level `mood` recognises the contrast rather than declaring uniformly joyful melancholy.

## Ommadawn (1975) — mikeoldfield-ommadawn

- **Genres**: [FOND/SOUND](https://www.fondsound.com/mike-oldfield-ommadawn-1975/) specifically calls the style progressive folk, Celtic and ambient. [AllMusic](https://www.allmusic.com/album/ommadawn-r129948) adds prog rock, art rock, progressive electronic, ambient and experimental electronic. The official site calls the album a precursor to New Age, so `newage` is explicitly retrospective. African drumming is a documented element of the record, but does **not** license the token `afro`, which usually labels quite different music.
- **Scales** (`3,1,2,4,4,3,2,2`): [contemporary Melody Maker review](https://www.tubular.net/articles/1975_10b/Beyond-the-Ridge-Portrait-of-a-Genius-Ommadawn-Review) describes its massive, lengthy African-drum climax; other sections feature delicate pastoral music and a simple English-language sung coda. This creates greater rhythmic movement than *Hergest Ridge*, although it remains a long-form listening record. The Celtic/African instrumentation and shape of the two suites support high theatrical and experimental values without placing it in electronic dance music.
- **Instruments**: original [credits](https://tubular.net/discography/Ommadawn) identify harp, a large family of electric and acoustic guitars, acoustic/electric **bass guitars**, mandolin, bodhrán, bouzouki, banjo, spinet, piano, electric organ, synthesizers, glockenspiel, varied percussion, uilleann pipes, recorder, pan flute, timpani, cello and trumpet. The **spinet** has no current token and must be added. Jabula supplied South African drums. Northumbrian bagpipes are *credited, but documented as cut from the finished album*, therefore **bagpipes is excluded**. Generic `drums` reflects the credited African drum ensemble; instruments whose type cannot be specified remain under percussion.
- **Production**: [critical recording study](https://tubular.net/analysis/ommadawn/) establishes 24-track tape, repeated overdubbing and the failing earlier tape reel. The row's `overdubbing|analogtape` therefore describes the **original** recording process, not reissue remastering. Oldfield's [1978 Guitar Player interview](https://www.mulhern.com/articles/oldfield1978.htm) describes three 12-string guitar layers treated with a limiter and repeat echo on *Ommadawn*, directly supporting `compression|delay`. The study also discusses a restarted version, so avoid treating unreleased versions as extra albums.
- **Voice/language**: primarily instrumental with choral/wordless passages and audible vocals, and the English-language song 'On Horseback' at the end of Part Two: `language=en`. Invented syllables on the choral passage do **not** justify a Gaelic language tag. Credited singers are not co-producers. **Only Oldfield and Phil Newell** are credited as producers in the specialist discography; some contemporary credits shorten this to 'composed, produced and engineered by Oldfield.' This edition/credit disagreement should be reviewed at import rather than replacing both names by assumption.
- **Criticism**: Karl Dallas's 1975 contemporary review is intensely admiring of the African-drum climax; later criticism concentrates on cohesion and folk fusion. `sounds_like` is a loose editorial comparison and **does not** imply Clannad or the Chieftains directly influenced this album.

## Incantations (1978) — mikeoldfield-incantations

- **Genres**: [AllMusic](https://www.allmusic.com/album/incantations-mw0000193927) categorises it as prog rock, art rock, progressive electronic and New Age. [Official artist site](https://www.mikeoldfieldofficial.com/releases-archive/incantations-deluxe-edition/) explicitly calls it minimalist music: `minimalism` is thus documented. New Age is retrospective.
- **Scales** (`2,2,3,4,3,3,2,1`): the four sides are extended, dynamically varied suites with repeating motifs, large choral/string passages and restrained sections. The longer choral/orchestral delivery supports theatricality 4, but low dance groove; electronics are present alongside acoustic ensembles, so not predominantly synthetic. Official site and [AllMusic review](https://www.allmusic.com/album/incantations-mw0000193927) document scale and long-form development.
- **Instruments**: [credits](https://tubular.net/discography/Incantations) confirm trumpet, flute, Pierre Moerlen's drums and vibraphone, strings, choir and African drums by Jabula. Crucially, Oldfield's [1978 *Guitar Player* interview](https://www.mulhern.com/articles/oldfield1978.htm) **directly confirms** bodhrán, marimba/vibraphone, many electric guitar solos, acoustic bass guitar, electric bass guitar and a string section for *Incantations*. Thus `mallets` is supported by two independent sources and `bodhran` by Oldfield. The fan source **speculates** about Oldfield's piano and synthesiser; those two were deliberately omitted from the row instead of silently treating conjecture as fact. The 1978 sleeve does not split all uncredited instruments by track, so `confidence=medium` remains appropriate. Maddy Prior, Sally Oldfield and Queen's College Girls Choir handle the sung and choral portions. `language=en` because the vocal setting draws on English texts by Henry Wadsworth Longfellow and Ben Jonson. Their literary content supports `storytelling|spiritual`, without treating the writers as musical influences.
- **Production**: left **blank**: a credible explicit technical claim about specific studio techniques/machines for this album was not found. Do not extrapolate 1973's 16-track Ampex setup, a later remastering process, or synthesizer model to 1978.
- **Producers**: original [credit transcription](https://www.mikeoldfield.org/incantations) gives Mike Oldfield alone. David Bedford is credited as choir/strings conductor, **not** co-producer.
- **Critical disagreement**: Oldfield's official archive calls the record a major minimalist work; [Steven McDonald's AllMusic review](https://www.allmusic.com/album/incantations-mw0000193927) finds the long structure dramatically impressive yet overstretched. Consequently this is a non-landmark **candidate** (`0`) pending the owner's preference, not a factual assertion that it lacks importance. The explicit artist/producer influence field is empty: no direct statement was found tying this particular album to Philip Glass or Steve Reich. These names appear only as editorial `sounds_like` comparisons.
