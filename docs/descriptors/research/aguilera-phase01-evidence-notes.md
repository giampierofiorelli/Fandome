# Christina Aguilera — Phase 01 evidence notes

Method: the 27-column rows are deliberately **album-level judgements**, not averages of named singles. Scores are anchored against `docs/album-descriptors-guide.md`. `sources.csv` records external evidentiary support and `genre_evidence.csv` records one source/scope per assigned genre. MusicBrainz/Qobuz/session-credit reprints are used for musician/producer *roles*, not for artistic interpretation. Original editions control; remixes and reissues are not used to add instruments to the original release. All rows remain `draft`.

## `aguilera-christinaaguilera` — Christina Aguilera (1999)

- **Genres:** AllMusic calls the record teen-pop/dance-pop, general pop and adult contemporary; the R&B/soul classification is limited to particular songs in Sessiondays and contemporary interviews. AllMusic labels the *single* “Genie in a Bottle” contemporary R&B and pop-soul; those sit last, **some tracks**, not a misleading album-wide claim. The associated 2000 single remix of “Come On Over Baby” must not silently replace its 1999 album version.
- **Scales:** energy 3, tempo 3 and groove 3 represent dance singles balanced with numerous slower love ballads (AllMusic). Electronic 3 reflects heavily programmed rhythm/keyboards plus recorded guitars/piano (Sessiondays). Mood 4 is predominantly bright; theatrical 3 reflects big singing in conventional radio-pop material. Experimental 0 indicates standard teen-pop arrangements rather than a judgement on virtuosity. Polish 4 reflects the unusually clean, radio-oriented production (AllMusic).
- **Voice:** `powerful|clear`: contemporary critics agree on her unusually forceful, precise singing, even when the songs are commercial; neither rap nor whispered delivery defines the lead.
- **Instruments:** David Frank and Steve Kipner play keyboards/programmed drums on “Genie”; guitars appear in “What a Girl Wants,” “I Turn to You” and “So Emotional”; piano (Heather Holley) and bass/drums (Robin Thicke) are specifically credited on other tracks. John Glaser is credited with a Moog on “I Turn to You”: `synth` therefore rests on credited equipment and not genre inference. These are multiple track-level contributions, **not a claim that all play everywhere**.
- **Production:** `drumprogramming` is credited on “Genie” and other album cuts. `moog` is documented on “I Turn to You”; do not confuse a Moog *instrument* with a track-wide electronic sound. No invented TR-808, Auto-Tune, LinnDrum, workstation or software tokens.
- **Producers:** Sessiondays' **producer** list is taken per original track, including Doreen Dorian's explicit co-producer credit; **Diane Warren as executive producer** is excluded. Ron Fair's album-level A&R/executive work is excluded unless a distinct original-track producer credit is established. Producers associated solely with later single remixes are likewise excluded.
- **Influenced by:** during original recording, Steve Kipner names Chaka Khan, Etta James and Mariah Carey as Aguilera's internalised riffs (LA Times); Whitney Houston appears as a clear contemporary influence/comparison rather than a mere genre inference. This is **vocal influence**, not a claim these artists wrote the material.
- **Resemblance, not influence:** contemporary teen-pop peers Britney Spears, Jessica Simpson and Mandy Moore are editorial sonic comparators, not credits.
- **Critical disagreement:** AllMusic praises accessible songwriting and relatively uncluttered production; Aguilera retrospectively told the Washington Post she was frustrated by the restricted R&B improvisation and label-driven pop sound.

## `aguilera-mireflejo` — Mi Reflejo (2000)

- **Genres:** Latin pop and translated teen/dance-pop occupy most of the track list (AllMusic). Specific counterexamples are the hard-salsa “Cuando No Es Contigo” and the bolero standard “Contigo en la Distancia” (Christopher John Farley, TIME, Sept. 2000). Because `bolero` is not an available Fandome genre token, it must be added by Claude; **salsa and bolero are track-level, not full-album labels**.
- **Scales:** energy 3, tempo 3, groove 3 and mood 3 account for both driving Latin grooves and slow regretful ballads. Electronic 2 reflects acoustic guitar, live percussion/bass/piano/horns and isolated drum machines/Moog. Theatrical 4 reflects sustained melismatic, often dramatic vocal lines (TIME); experimental 1 reflects the relatively conventional pop/Latin arrangements. Polish 4 matches AllMusic's appraisal of its sophisticated, tidy recording.
- **Voice / lyrics:** `powerful|clear`; songs mainly concern love and loss; `identity` is secondary, informed by this record's stated language/cultural project (TIME), not a claim every song is about heritage. Entire original 11-song album is Spanish, with Spanish renditions of selected debut material.
- **Instruments:** MusicBrainz documents piano, acoustic guitar, bass, drums and Latin percussion across original new tracks; “Cuando No Es Contigo” credits salsa piano, trumpets, saxophone, trombone, electric bass and congas/percussion. Handclaps are explicitly credited on “El Beso del Final.” String players on “Si No Te Hubiera Conocido”; Moog appears on “Por Siempre Tú.”
- **Production:** “Falsas Esperanzas” and “Si No Te Hubiera Conocido” credit programmed drum-machine parts; “Por Siempre Tú” explicitly credits John Glaser's Moog; “Si No Te Hubiera Conocido” has credited string arrangements. **Do not** reclassify generic keyboard programming as named synthesizer software or hardware.
- **Producers:** Rudy Pérez is primary; Sergio George has explicit producer credit for the salsa track (TIME/MusicBrainz). MusicBrainz lists David Frank, Steve Kipner, Johan Åberg, Paul Rein, Guy Roche and Matthew Wilder on **specific remade original songs**; Ron Fair and Diane Warren are listed as *executive* producers on relevant tracks and are excluded. Producer roles from the 2001 remix edition are not adopted.
- **Influences:** none added: Latin-market contemporaries and songwriting collaborators alone do not establish a sourced direct musical influence on this particular recording.
- **Critical disagreement:** TIME praises the increased stylistic range and traditional songs; AllMusic calls most of the project too familiar, mostly translated from existing material. Both can hold because salsa/bolero are few tracks.

## `aguilera-mykindofchristmas` — My Kind of Christmas (2000)

- **Genres:** AllMusic calls it Christmas/holiday, dance-pop and teen-pop. Contemporary Philippine Star criticism explicitly calls it pop/R&B with gospel shading. Ron Fair's contemporary statement names blues and gospel dimensions; MusicBrainz gives `contemporaryrnb` and `jazzpop` tags but those are **limited/subordinate**, so row confidence is medium and both genres are near the tail. `soul` refers to original singer/producer remarks on selected performances rather than the entire record. `christmas` is a thematic holiday genre supported in Fandome, **not a proxy for acoustic sound**.
- **Scales:** energy 3, groove 2 and tempo 2 reflect both electronic-leaning upbeat originals and ballad/orchestral standards. Electronic 2 because live orchestra and rhythm section occupy much of the record even though the contemporary originals use drum programming. Mood 4 reflects seasonal celebration tempered by more solemn hymns. Theatrical 4 reflects gospel choir, 70-piece orchestra and huge dynamic vocal runs; polish 4 and experimental 1 reflect polished mainstream pop-holiday productions.
- **Voice:** `powerful|clear|choral` is supported by AllMusic's explicit descriptions of belting and Qobuz credits for LA Mass Choir and Celebrity Voices Choir; `choral` applies only to selected songs.
- **Instruments:** Official Qobuz track credits specify strings, a large orchestra including violin/cello/woodwinds, piano (Dr. John and Tom Ranier), electric keyboards, organ (Myron McKinley), horn section, drum kit, percussion and programmed pop drums. Instruments appear across the album, not together on each track. Drum programming is documented for “Christmas Time” and others, and strings/horn arrangements are explicitly identified in album credits.
- **Production:** `belting` comes from AllMusic's original review, `drumprogramming` from label credits, `stringarrangements` / `hornarrangements` from AllMusic and Qobuz. Do not add Auto-Tune, sample machines or specific DAWs without evidence.
- **Producers:** producer-only labels reflect original recording track credits and contemporary music databases. Include Ron Fair, The Matrix, Robbie Buchanan, Bassy Bob Brockmann, Barry Harris, Chris Cox, ChakDaddy, Sol Survivor and E. Dawk as credited. Do **not** import remix producers from later issues except those credited to the actual 2000 original track/remix that already belongs to its 11-track edition.
- **Influences:** no artist is named as a documented direct **album-production** influence. The traditional song repertoire and Dr. John guest appearance do not automatically make their performers direct influences.
- **Critical disagreement:** AllMusic hears an engaging and concise (if over-sung) seasonal album; Washington Post dismisses the overly decorative melismas. Historical reviews are somewhat polarized.

## `aguilera-stripped` — Stripped (2002)

- **Genres:** pop is the overarching commercial framework (AllMusic). Contemporary R&B, neo-soul and pop-rap have album-specific critical taxonomies (Album of the Year), with reviews substantiating hip-hop and soul components. Rock (“Fighter,” “Make Over”), Latin pop (“Infatuation”), gospel and drum-and-bass (“Keep on Singin' My Song”) are specific-track elements; **do not weight the latter as the core album genre** (Slant, Guardian, AllMusic, producer accounts). `drumandbass` is one closing section, not album-wide.
- **Scales:** energy 3, tempo 3 and groove 3 are an album average across the beat-led “Dirrty,” “Can't Hold Us Down,” slower ballads and mid-tempo confessionals. Electronic 3 reflects programmed rhythm tracks coexisting with live guitars, drums, bass, strings and piano (MusicBrainz/Sessiondays). Mood 2 reflects recurring insecurity, family trauma, heartbreak and anger despite playful/empowering tracks. Theatrical 4 and polish 5 follow power-ballad arrangements and critics' descriptions of lavish, sometimes overwrought production. Experimental 2 reflects genre mixing but predominantly familiar structures.
- **Voice / lyrics:** `powerful` remains dominant, with `raspy`/`soft` for some raw and tender sections; `rap` describes substantial featured rap in “Can't Hold Us Down” and “Dirrty,” **not Aguilera's main lead delivery**. `identity`, `introspection`, `sensuality`, `social`, `heartbreak`, `love` cover self-definition, family abuse, feminist stance, relationships, sexual agency (Guardian and Aguilera interviews). English dominates; Spanish material is limited to the short “Primer Amor” interlude and brief references, so `es` is secondary.
- **Instruments:** Sessiondays / MusicBrainz / AllMusic independently credit live drums/bass, electric and acoustic guitars (Navarro, etc.), piano (Alicia Keys on “Impossible”), keyboards and strings; some tracks feature horns. `synth` has credit basis from original album's AllMusic credits. Note that “Fighter” begins from Scott Storch's **keyboard imitation of guitar**, but later used actual guitar (W oral history). No inference from genre.
- **Production:** `drumprogramming` and `protools` are directly credited in album documentation; string/horn arrangements appear in credits. `belting` appears in contemporaneous reviews; Linda Perry consciously preserved a more exposed original demo *vocal* on “Beautiful,” but **the whole album is not lo-fi or one-take** and neither tag is used.
- **Producers:** Scott Storch, Linda Perry, Alicia Keys, Heather Holley, Rob Hoffman, Balewa Muhammad, Steve Morales, Rockwilder and Glen Ballard have distinct per-track production roles, as does Christina Aguilera on specified songs. E. Dawk and Jasper Cameron are **explicit vocal producers**, included as production roles; Ron Fair is **executive producer only** on Stripped and therefore excluded. Songwriters and instrumental guests are not silently elevated to producers.
- **Influenced by:** contemporaneous Allure interview describes Etta James as an inspiration **for the album's new direction**; Scott Storch specifically identifies his admiration of Jane’s Addiction in choosing Dave Navarro for “Fighter” (W oral history). They are not simply general career or cover-song influences.
- **Critical disagreement:** Slant, Guardian and AllMusic complain about excess ambition, length or overproduction, while RTÉ is broadly favorable. Later firsthand accounts frame the artistic decisions as deliberate self-definition. The notes preserve these differences rather than treating later retrospective enthusiasm as contemporary unanimity.
- **Edition caution:** original *Stripped* was a 20-track album. Some platforms use **22 October 2002** while AllMusic reports **29 October 2002**, likely due to territory/date conventions; year 2002 is unambiguous. 20th-anniversary bonuses and remixes are excluded from sonic evidence.
