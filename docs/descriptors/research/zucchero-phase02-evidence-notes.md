# Zucchero — phase 02 evidence notes

Research date: **29 October 2026**. Scope: four new works (1988–1995); original Italian editions, not reissues. Only phase 02 rows occur in the import CSV. All numeric scales are **editorial estimates** on the 0–5 Fandome anchors, not measured observations or claims made by cited sources; assess by audition before marking reviewed.

## `zucchero-snackbarbudapestoriginalmoviesoundtrack` — *Snack Bar Budapest Original Movie Soundtrack* (1988)

- **Identity and scope:** Zucchero's website files it as his soundtrack and dates it to 11 November 1988, but original 1988 CD track credits separately name **Zucchero, David Sancious, Lisa Hunt and Nina Simone**. It is a collaborative, mostly instrumental film album, not an ordinary Zucchero vocal studio album. **Keep `artist=Zucchero` for the catalogue room; do not mislabel all 15 tracks as sung/performed by him.** The LP cover artist is listed as `Zucchero Sugar Fornaciari` rather than `Various Artists`. Propose a note in coBilled.ts only if appropriate to the site's semantics, since guests and per-track credited musicians are *not* automatically co-billed on the album cover.
- **Genres:** Original Polydor LP metadata explicitly gives `bluesrock` and `poprock`, KQEK film-score reviewer calls out *synth-pop* underpinnings and a noir-blues ambience, hence `synthpop`. `soundtrack` is a type/genre, not a substitute for the musical styles. `funk` is only the original *Something Strong* single's 1988 metadata; it is a long-tail, one-song cue, not a score-wide verdict. 15 tracks include many passages of less than one minute. The final Nina Simone track is an **older performance** of *My Baby Just Cares for Me* and is not proof Nina collaborated in the 1988 sessions.
- **Instruments & production:** KQEK specifically hears synthetic/electronic sounds, electric guitar, bass and a **drum sequencer**; `synth|electricguitar|bass|sequencer` and `drumprogramming` reflect those concrete observations. Do not add piano, drum kit, horn arrangement, or a named synth model without record-specific credits. A casual credit of Sancious as keyboardist elsewhere is not conclusive for this score.
- **Producers conflict:** KQEK says David Sancious is credited as arranger/performer/**producer** for music; an Italian discography lists **Corrado Rustici** as the soundtrack's producer. Both are carried provisionally with `confidence=medium` pending a photograph of the 1988 original insert establishing **track-level vs album-level** roles. No evidence supports adding Zucchero merely because he composed the score.
- **Sounds like:** KQEK compares the cues with **Vangelis's *Blade Runner***, **Jan Hammer's *Miami Vice***, and a sequenced passage with **Éric Serra's *La Femme Nikita***. These are critic's sonic comparisons only; NO `influenced_by`. The Serra comparison is retrospective (1990 film), not a claim that the 1988 album copied it.
- **Voice, language, themes, scales:** Many cues instrumental; Lisa Hunt's *Something Strong* English; Zucchero's *Sei di mattino* Italian; Nina Simone English. Thus `instrumental|raspy|powerful` and `en|it` are an *album-level mixture*, not a single singer. Themes `love|heartbreak` are provisional for the relatively few vocal tracks; no claim about instrumental cue lyrics. Low average `tempo/groove=2`, elevated `electronic=3` relative to the singer's guitar-based LPs.

## `zucchero-oroincensobirra` — *Oro incenso & birra* (1989)

- **Exact release:** Official 13 June 1989 original **nine tracks**. The 1990/1991 international re-recordings (including a **Paul Young** duet) and the 2019 bonus demos are **NOT** part of this row. *A Wonderful World* appears among the 1989 nine tracks; Eric Clapton played on the song, while the 2019 edition also includes a separately labelled 1990 international arrangement. Do not mistake the new 2019 remaster publication date or AllMusic 1992 reissue date for the original.
- **Genres and rank:** Original 1989 LP `bluesrock`; 1989 German CD `bluesrock|softrock|poprock`; AllMusic `modernelectricblues|italianpop`. Gospel choir appears in **Diamante** and Adventist voices in *Il mare...*. Blues and soul lineage is described by Zucchero in the anniversary notes. `funk` is a lower-importance groove-level assessment from singles, not a claim all nine songs are funk. Do not use AllMusic's anomalous *Political Folk* style for this record.
- **Instrumentation:** Universal Music credits Corrado Rustici electric guitar, Polo Jones bass, Giorgio Francis drums, David Sancious keyboards/Hammond, Jimmy Smith Hammond on *Overdose*, Memphis Horns trumpet/sax, Rosario Jermano percussion; original pressing transcription further documents Rustici **sitar/computer programming**, Sancious **synthesizer and piano**. The genres alone would not justify those instruments. Voices include Lisa Hunt, Ruby Wilson, Rufus Thomas and two named choirs, without implying they are all lead singers.
- **Production and producers:** Corrado Rustici has the original overall producer credit and a documented programming credit; hence `production=programming`, *not* a named Fairlight/MIDI machine. **Universal Music labels Zucchero and Luciano Luisi as producers specifically for *A Wonderful World***. They are included in `producers` **after** Rustici, but this needs reconciliation against the **original 1989 physical insert**, which may name only Rustici at album level. Ennio Morricone arranged/composed *Libera l'amore*, **not produced it**. Gordon Lyon mixed, not produced.
- **Influence:** Zucchero calls the album a diary balancing **American blues** and childhood memories of Emilia, a general musical tradition but not a named album-specific artist; leave `influenced_by` empty. Eric Clapton and Jimmy Smith are **guests**, never stylistic influences by virtue of participation. `sounds_like=Joe Cocker|Ray Charles` is editorial vocal/idiom comparison, not an interview attribution.
- **Energy and landmark:** The 1989 LP is defining, spirited rock/blues/soul with slower *Diamante* and *Libera l'amore*. High polish is justified by four international studios and carefully arranged production; `energy=4` reflects the heavier main tracks but `groove=4` reflects strong band rhythms, not dance-floor disco.

## `zucchero-miserere` — *Miserere* (1992)

- **Date and versions:** Official 1 October 1992 original **Italian edition**, 12 tracks; international English tracks are alternatives, not a second studio album. Mixed language `it|en` because *It's All Right (La Promessa)* is partly English and *Miserere* includes a guest tenor; *L'urlo* has Léo Ferré as guest. Bono contributed English lyrics to the song **Miserere**, not generally to the full Italian album; do not treat lyricists as producers or album influences.
- **Genre, mood and themes:** Italian discography classifies the record `popsoul` and discusses R&B musical grounds; English credits list `bluesrock|soul|funk`. UK Gospel Fusion supports a small gospel presence, and Pavarotti contributes a distinctive operatic **title song only**. The autobiographical post-divorce depression and introspection are corroborated by the artist's career accounts. Hence `mood=1`, introverted lyrics and `operatic` after primary raspy/strong vocal tags; it is **not** a wholly classical/opera record.
- **Instruments:** AllMusic's Rustici musician credits explicitly name acoustic and electric guitars, dobro, mandolin, harmonium, Synthaxe, keyboards, synthesizer, tubular bells, E-Bow, Leslie guitar and tremolo. Other album personnel are documented for bass, drums, percussion, Andy J. Forest harmonica, Wayne Jackson/Andrew Love horn section, the Budrio ocarina ensemble, and Michael Kamen's strings. This justifies **one NEW instrument token `synthaxe`**, the proprietary guitar-like MIDI instrument (do not silently convert to `guitarsynth` or infer which synth module it triggered). `ocarina` is an existing token, because *Ocarine di Budrio* is literally an ocarina ensemble.
- **Production:** Rustici is credited with `programming`, E-Bow, tremolo, guitar through Leslie and `hornarrangements`; Michael Kamen arranged the orchestra/strings, hence `stringarrangements`. An *Anna Solatia* spoken voice with studio effects is documented, but the precise manipulation is unspecified: **do not add `vocoder`, `vocalfilter` or other named processing**. Tech-heavy textured session supports `experimental=3` rather than generic pop benchmark.
- **Producer conflict that needs original-source resolution:** Original 1992 single insert says **Corrado Rustici producer**, **Michele Torpedine executive producer**, but current **Universal Music label album track metadata explicitly calls Torpedine a co-producer on tracks 3 and 4**. Included both Rustici and Torpedine in `producers` strictly because of these album-track credits, with Torpedine **track-limited and disputed**; do not extend that attribution to all 12 or to the title single. If the original LP insert contradicts the digital metadata, remove Torpedine. Similar Zucchero remaster compilation production metadata is not enough to add him automatically.
- **Comparison:** Joe Cocker and Peter Gabriel are loose editorial sonic/vocal comparisons, not album-documented influence assertions. Leave `influenced_by` empty. `landmark=1` for a pivotal introspective record and celebrated Pavarotti collaboration.

## `zucchero-spiritodivino` — *Spirito DiVino* (1995)

- **Date and edition:** Official original 27 May 1995 Italian album of **ten songs**. English version *Stray Cat in a Mad Dog City*, Spanish-language versions, 2023/2025 remasters are not new original album rows. Do not import the later US compilation/alternate track order as if it were a separate original album. Language `it` refers to original Italian edition.
- **Genres:** Italian album discussion gives `popsoul|blues|musicaleggera`, English album listing adds `softrock|poprock|bluesrock`. Gospel is album-specific and recurrent: Universal Music lists the New Orleans Gospel Choir on *Voodoo Voodoo*, *Pane e sale*, *X colpa di chi?*, *Senza rimorso*, *Papà perché*, *Così celeste* and *Alleluja*. Its presence is **recorded vocals**, not proof Zucchero himself sang operatically.
- **Instruments:** Album personnel identify Zucchero **piano, Hammond organ, acoustic and electric guitars, Mellotron, percussion**; Rustici acoustic/electric guitar/keyboards; Luciano Luisi keys; Polo Jones bass; Steve Smith drums; Johnnie Johnson piano/organ/bass; Sheila E percussion; Clarence Clemons/Memphis Horns sax/trumpet/trombone; Jeff Beck electric guitar on *Papà perché*. Acoustic credits for this album are documented, not deduced from generic blues idiom. Stewart Copeland's drum cameo appears in secondary musician list but not the short official musician roster: treat as supplementary/unresolved, and do not present as core drummer.
- **Production:** Corrado Rustici is credited **Producer on every original track** in Universal's track metadata; Zucchero and Luciano Luisi are **recording arrangers**, not producers. Lester Snell directs/arranges the choir. There is not enough direct evidence to select a named machine or specific production technique from the allowed list. Therefore `production` is **deliberately empty** instead of fabricated. This does not imply the album was unproduced.
- **Lyrics, sound, reception:** Higher mood than *Miserere* after its dark introspection, but *Il volo* and *Così celeste* retain softer, reflective love themes. The original is polished and accessible without claiming all songs are identical. AllMusic's historic assessment rates it 3/5 and praises presentation while criticising the memorability of much of the songwriting; thus successful and important is not necessarily universally critically acclaimed. `sounds_like=Joe Cocker|Dr. John` is cautious editorial comparability grounded in gritty soul and New Orleans gospel context, not explicit artist testimony.
