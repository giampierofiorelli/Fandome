### olamide-baddestguyeverliveth (Baddest Guy Ever Liveth, 2013)

Sources:
- OkayAfrica, Audu Bey, 19 Nov 2013. https://www.okayafrica.com/olamide-baddest-guy-ever-liveth/175874
- Music In Africa, "How Olamide became bigger than Nigerian rap", 16 Dec 2016. https://musicinafrica.net/node/16356
- Music In Africa, "10 of the best: Olamide". https://www.musicinafrica.net/node/15629
- Zikoko, Olamide's solo albums ranked. https://zikoko.com/?p=142285
- AlbumTalks, T.J. Martins. https://albumtalks.com/?p=7202
- NotJustOk ranking. https://notjustok.com/article/ranking-olamide-best-albums/
- Pulse 2024 ranking. https://www.pulse.ng/story/a-ranking-nigerian-superstar-musician-olamides-9-albums-2024072615261976006
- Unorthodox Reviews. https://unorthodoxreviews.com/?p=36842
- Discogs CD (YBNL Nation, tracklist). https://api.discogs.com/releases/15297889

Cells:
- **genres**
  - hiphop, rap: Music In Africa treats BGEL as a rap album ("the rap song, 'Sitting on the Throne'"). OkayAfrica calls him a "Nigerian MC".
  - pop: Unorthodox, "married Yoruba lyricism with pop structure". Zikoko: "BGEL also elevated Olamide to true pop star status with songs like 'Durosoke' and 'Turnup'".
  - fuji (one track): Music In Africa on "Anifowose": "a song that makes you wonder if a marriage between hiphop and fuji has been rendered better". OkayAfrica: built "off a sample from fuji legend K1 the ultimate".
  - makossa (one track): OkayAfrica, "the makossa-esque 'Position Yourself'".
- **energy 3, groove 4**: NotJustOk says "This album is littered with bangers" and that Olamide "entered the studio intending to break people's waists with gbedu". Music In Africa: "The best songs are two dance songs, 'Eleda Mi' and 'Durosoke'". The same source calls "Sitting on the Throne" "laid back", which keeps energy at 3.
- **mood 4**: Music In Africa, "BGEL succeeds mostly because of its melodious tunes"; the hits are dance and club songs. "Anifowose" is nostalgic and about poverty (Zikoko), so not 5.
- **experimental 1**: OkayAfrica notes "the somewhat derivative nature of some chosen beats". Unorthodox: pop structure.
- **electronic, theatrical, polish, tempo**: no source describes them. Left empty.
- **voice rap**: OkayAfrica, "squeal-inducing rhymes and a core audience connecting chorus".
- **production sampling**: OkayAfrica says the album samples K1 on "Anifowose" and Mumford & Sons on "Motivation".
- **lyrics**
  - storytelling: OkayAfrica, the "grass to grace story" of "Anifowose".
  - introspection: Zikoko, "Olamide explained his fear of failure and poverty".
  - party: Pulse, "the club with 'Turn Up'".
  - identity: Zikoko, "a masterclass in street knowledge"; Unorthodox, it "gave the streets a new language".
- **language yo|en**: OkayAfrica on rapping "in their native tongue". Music In Africa says Olamide switches seamlessly between languages, and says Street OT, the next album, has more Yoruba "than on any other album".
- **producers Pheelz**: Music In Africa, "Pheelz ... produced Olamide's third studio effort BGEL". V.I.C ("Sitting on the Throne") appears only on Wikipedia, so it is not listed.
- **landmark 1**
  - Zikoko: "If Olamide ever made a classic, it's this one."
  - AlbumTalks: "Olamide at his magnus opus in the mainstream".
  - AlbumTalks also reports that Olamide said "CD and BGEL are the best albums he's put out".
- **sounds_like, influenced_by**: no critic comparison of the sound and no album-specific statement. The samples (K1, Mumford & Sons) are excluded by rule.
- **confidence low**: the contemporary reviews couldn't be opened; the sound descriptions are thin.

Cells:
- **genres**
  - hiphop, rap: Pulse, "rap in its most immaculate form".
  - pop: Afrocritik, on Talk About It and MI2, "MI lightly mixed rap with Pop music. He brought Pop artistes to do vocals".
  - conscioushiphop (one track): Pulse, "the conscious 'Crowd Mentality'".
- **energy 3, groove 3**: Pulse, "With the uptempo 'Anoti', there was a 'Short Black Boy' created to steady the pace". Pulse also speaks of "mainstream records that are commercially appealing".
- **mood 4**: Pulse calls "Safe" "playful". Afrocritik says he is "effortlessly hilarious. A slice of this talent is sprinkled all over his projects".
- **experimental 2**: The Native, "his adventurous Talk About It debut". In the same interview M.I says "I was just in the studio putting in hard work and trying new sounds".
- **polish 3**: Music In Africa on "Safe", "excellent production". Pulse, "without the use of big-name producers at the time".
- **theatrical, tempo**: unsourced. The skits ("An Outrageous Intro Starring the President", "Random Guy Buying Blaze") are in the MusicBrainz tracklist, but no critic describes the staging.

- **lyrics**: Zikoko, "M.I rapped unapologetically about his fair-weather friends, faith, and societal pressures, all while exuding unmatched confidence". That gives identity, spiritual and social. Music In Africa on "Hustle": "an encouragement to young persons striving in Nigeria".
- **language en**: Pulse, "MI Abaga was an English-speaking Nigerian rapper".
- **producers**: Pulse debut list, "Producers: E-Kelly, Masterkraft, Jay Sleek, Samklef, DJ Klem", with "Fun fact: Kraft produced 'Fast Money, Fast Cars.'" M.I confirms in The Native: "Wizkid came in and heard the beat that Kraft had sent me". I read Kraft as Masterkraft, as Pulse does. Uncertain: search snippets claimed M.I produced most of the album himself, but the source page (Pulse 2024) is a 404 and I found no other credit, so M.I is not listed.
- **landmark 1**: Pulse, "'Talk About It' is the key to the glory age". Zikoko, "Talk About It not only defined his career".
- **influenced_by**: empty. The "Short Black Boy" boast ("He flow like Jigga and he sound like Nas") is a playful self-comparison, not a stated influence.
- **sounds_like**: no critic comparison of the sound.

### psquare-gameover (Game Over, 2007)

Sources:

- The NET, "A review of 'Game Over'", 28 Jul 2017. https://thenet.ng/review-album-psquare/

- Discogs CD, TJoe Entertainment: "Written-By, Producer – P-Square". https://api.discogs.com/releases/2208556

Cells:
- **genres**
  - afropop: The Native, "an Afropop album reported to have sold nearly 8-Million copies".
  - rnb, hiphop: The Native, "a hip-hop and R&B title-track".
  - pop: Pulse, "one of the best pop albums in Nigeria".
  - ballad: The NET, "Try listening to the ballads 'No one like you', 'Miss you die' and 'Ifunanya'".
  - highlife is not used: The Native's "hi-life-infused r&b/pop" describes the duo's career, not this album.
- **energy 3, groove 4**: Pulse, "This album can still rock any Nigerian party hard ... millions of Nigerians would dance to". The NET, "beats that get you off your seats". The ballads keep energy at 3.
- **mood 3**: a mix of love ballads, party tracks and a title track of break-up "rage" (The Native).
- **experimental 1**: The NET, "filled with P-square's regular tricks: repetition, often meaningless poetry, interpolations, sampling".
- **polish 4**: Pulse, "hypnotic melodies and standout smooth production".
- **voice**: no source describes their voices on this album. Left empty.
- **instruments violin|handclaps** (title track only): The Native, "opens with ominous violins ... dissolves into claps and heavy base drops".
- **production sampling**: The NET ("interpolations, sampling") and The Native ("heavy sampling from a broad range of genres").
- **lyrics**: The Native, a "hard break-up song" (heartbreak) and "the actual rage of losing love". The NET's ballads give love. Pulse's party gives party.
- **language pcm|en|ig**: The Native 2020, "P-Square's music was deliberately delivered in pidgin English, with bursts of their native tongue, Igbo". "Ifunanya" is in Igbo.
- **producers P-Square**: Discogs CD credits. The Native 2020: "Paul was the primary producer". Co-producer J. Martins appears only on Wikipedia, so it is not listed.
- **sounds_like Destiny's Child (Survivor) [1/10] and Timbaland [1/10]**: The Native, "ominous violins, similar to the ones you hear on Destiny's Child's 'Survivor'... like you would hear on a typical Timbaland produced track". This is the title track, one of the 10 songs.
- **influenced_by**: The Native calls Michael Jackson "their icon", but that is the writer's words and tied to other songs, so it is not used.
- **landmark 1**: Pulse, "Many critics and fans consider this album to be the finest work of Psquare's illustrious career".

### tiwasavage-celia (Celia, 2020)

Sources:
- The Native, Adewojumi Aderemi, "5 Takeaways", 28 Aug 2020. https://thenativemag.com/5-takeaways-tiwa-savages-celia/
- Pulse, Motolani Alake, review, 30 Aug 2020. https://www.pulse.ng/story/tiwa-savage-celia-album-review-davido-naira-marley-stefflon-don-dice-ailes-and-more-2024081718565700413
- YNaija, Bernard Dayo, songs ranked. https://ynaija.com/all-the-songs-on-tiwa-savages-celia-ranked/
- NPR, All Things Considered interview. https://www.npr.org/transcripts/921865020
- Music In Africa review. https://www.musicinafrica.net/node/133163
- OkayAfrica tracklist article, 19 Aug 2020. https://www.okayafrica.com/tiwa-savage-celia-album-tracklist/
- Rolling Stone, "Temptation". https://www.rollingstone.com/music/music-news/tiwa-savage-sam-smith-temptation-1047998/
- SA Music Mag. https://samusicmag.co.za/?p=41239
- NotJustOk on Time's list. https://notjustok.com/news/tiwa-savages-celia-makes-time-magazines-10-best-albums-of-2020/

Cells:
- **genres**
  - afrobeats, rnb: YNaija, "Afrobeats and R&B welded together". Savage (OkayAfrica): "Afrobeat combined with soul and RnB". That quote also supports soul.
  - afropop: Music In Africa, "pure Afropop gold". Pulse calls "Koroba" "a beautiful Afro-pop production".
  - pop: The Native, "a formidable pop album".
  - afrobeat: Pulse, "The Afrobeat base of 'Ole'" and "Afrobeat trumpets" on "Park Well".
  - afrohouse: The Native, "the infectious Afrohouse beat on 'Glory'". Pulse calls "Save My Life" "The Afro-house tune".
  - dancehall: YNaija on "Bombay", "Calibrated to a dancehall beat". Pulse says "bashment", mapped to dancehall.
  - afroswing (new token): Pulse on "Temptation", "Bashment/Afroswing glory".
  - edm, trance (one track): YNaija, "Glory ... This trance/EDM pop song".
  - gospel (one track): The Native on "Celia's Song", "brings the gospel feel".
- **energy 3, groove 4**: The Native, "Plunging listeners straight into the depths of the dancery right from the top". Pulse on "Save My Life": "an uncanny will to dance". The interlude "Us" and "Celia's Song" are slow.
- **electronic 3**: Afro-house and trance/EDM programming (The Native, YNaija) next to live-sounding horns, sax and bass guitar.
- **mood 4**: YNaija, "Nothing on Celia sounds as euphoric as Save My Life" and "A feel-good love song" ("Park Well"). "Us" is vulnerable, so not 5.
- **experimental 1**: radio Afrobeats. YNaija: "a regular Afrobeats cut on commercial radio".
- **polish 4**: The Native, "the excellent engineering of Tiwa's vocals"; a major-label production.
- **voice soft|clear**: Savage on NPR, "the texture of my voice is, I feel like, very sweet, very pretty". Pulse, "her distinct soprano". Music In Africa, "delicate vocal majesty".
- **instruments**
  - saxophone: YNaija, "several streaks of saxophone sounds".
  - brass, bass, drums: The Native, "vibrant horns, feel-good drums and an ensorceling bass guitar" ("Ole").
  - trumpet: Pulse, "Afrobeat trumpets".
  - piano: YNaija, "With piano accompaniment" ("Us").
  - guitar: Music In Africa, "aching horn and guitar placements" ("Park Well").
  - flute: Pulse, "the flute riff that introduced" "Koroba".
- **production**
  - sampling: Pulse, "that incredible vocal sample at the end of the song" ("Ole").
  - layeredvocals: The Native, "choir-like harmonies" ("Celia's Song").
- **lyrics**
  - sensuality: NPR, "celebrates femininity as sensual, empowered and strong"; The Native, "an onslaught of carnal numbers".
  - love, party: Pulse, "heavily driven by thematic millennial tendencies of love, sex and enjoyment".
  - social, political: on "Koroba", Savage (NPR) addresses the slut-shaming of girls who date politicians and "the national budget".
  - heartbreak: "Us" is about her divorce (Pulse).
  - spiritual: "Celia's Song" is "like a prayer" (Savage, NPR).
  - identity: The Native, "a memoir of an African woman".
- **language en|yo|pcm**: Pulse, "she also switches her delivery between Yoruba and English". Pidgin lyrics are quoted on NPR ("I no come this life to suffer") and in The Native ("wetin you owe me").
- **producers**
  - London: Rolling Stone, "produced by London" ("Temptation"). Pulse and The Native call "Koroba" and "FWMM" "London-produced".
  - Rexxie: The Native, "'Ole' (produced by Rexxie)".
  - Pheelz: The Native, "Pheelz's ominous kick drum" on "Celia's Song"; Pulse's credits list.
  - Speroach Beatz: Music In Africa, "'Park Well' is produced by Speroach Beatz".
  - Cracker Mallo, Blaq Jerzee, Sak Pase, P2J: SA Music Mag, "Producer credits stem from London, Cracker Mallo, P2J, Sak Passe, Pheelz, Blaq Jerzee, Speroach Beats and more".
  - Not listed, because I found no opened source: Kill September, Rhyme Bamz, Ozedikus, Stone.
  - Executive producers are dropped.
- **sounds_like Liquideep [1/13] and Mi Casa [1/13]**: Pulse on "Temptation", "Liquideep/Mi Casa-esque Afro-House formation".
  - Excluded: the Rihanna ("Bitch Better Have My Money") and Cardi B comparisons are about lyrics.
  - Excluded: the guests (Sam Smith, Davido and others).
- **influenced_by Fela Kuti**: Savage, France 24, quoted by OkayAfrica: "I just really decided to go back to what I love and what I fell in love with as a musician which is Afrobeat combined with soul and RnB", "explaining her homage to Fela Kuti". Pulse also reports that during the album rollout on Hot 97 she called herself an "offspring" of Fela Kuti. Pulse calls that branding forced, so the link is a stated influence of uncertain depth.
- **landmark 1**: The Native, "already being lauded as a 'classic'". Music In Africa: "when ... Tiwa's catalogue is revisited, Celia will be cited very early". It also made Time's 10 best albums of 2020.

### asa-asa (Aṣa, 2007)

Sources:
- The Guardian, Robin Denselow, 15 Feb 2008, 3 stars. https://www.theguardian.com/music/2008/feb/15/worldmusic.shopping
- The Guardian, Paul Lester, New band of the day, 7 Dec 2007. https://www.theguardian.com/music/2007/dec/07/popandrock11

- Consequence, Alex Young, 16 Feb 2009. https://consequence.net/2009/02/album-review-asa-asa/
- PopMatters, Deanne Sole, 6/10. https://www.popmatters.com/70195-asa-asa-asha-2496062471.html
- Popdose, Jeff Giles. https://popdose.com/cd-review-asa-asa/
- The NET. https://thenet.ng/asa-self-titled-album-review/
- The Upper Entertainment, Bomi Anifowose, 2025. https://theupperent.com/2025/01/16/jailers-anthem-how-asa-debut-album-set-us-free/
- The Native, "50 shades of Aṣa", 2017. https://thenativemag.com/loved-asa-10-years/
- Al Jazeera, Wilfred Okiche, 2022. https://www.aljazeera.com/features/2022/3/23/the-reinvention-of-a%E1%B9%A3a-nigerias-premier-soul-music-diva
- NPR interview, 2009. https://www.npr.org/transcripts/100523110
- Culture Custodian, 2024. https://culturecustodian.com/asa-music-femininity-and-defying-expectations/
- Discogs, full CD credits (Dramatico/Naïve). https://api.discogs.com/releases/3546424

Cells:
- **genres**
  - soul, reggae, jazz, pop: NPR, "a mix of reggae, soul, and jazz with a touch of pop".
  - folksoul, funk: Paul Lester, "her blend of funk and pop, reggae and jazz, because her folk-soul is so forlorn".
  - soulpop, folk: Consequence, "a new face of universal soulpop" and "Asa's fresh folk, or airy soul-pop".
  - gospel, rnb, songwriter: PopMatters, "reggae, soul, gospel, R&B", "a definite singer-songwriter feel".
  - indiepop: Al Jazeera, "blending indie-pop, soul, jazz, folk and R&B".
  - afrosoul (new token): Culture Custodian, "Afro-soul influences".
- **energy 2, tempo 2, groove 2**
  - PopMatters: "There are no spikes, no shrieks, no crashes, no roars ... It asks for your attention through a process of slow insinuation", and "An R&B bob bounces in the background".
  - Lester: "Asa's gentle skank".
  - Upper Ent on "Jailer": "a protest song you'll catch yourself dancing to".
- **electronic 2**
  - Denselow: "drowned out by a wash of electronics and slushy strings ... the programming and keyboards kick in".
  - BBC: "glittery programming".
  - The base is acoustic guitar (Discogs credits).
- **mood 2**: The Native, "grim philosophical leaps resurface across the album, with a noble outlook, that lacks optimism". Consequence: "her lyrics aren't all sunshine ... there's always some hope".
- **theatrical 2**
  - PopMatters: "room-fillers rather than stadium-fillers".
  - BBC: "orchestral backing" on "Eye Adaba".
  - Denselow: "over-lush strings".
  - Intimate, with some swells, so 2.
- **experimental 1**: PopMatters, "She's not breaking new ground".
- **polish 3**: critics disagree.
  - Overproduced: Denselow ("slushy strings") and BBC ("clodhopping production").
  - Slick: Popdose ("a fairly slick, pop-grounded approach").
  - Sparse: Upper Ent ("The instrumentation was sparse but intentional").
  - The middle value is noted, not averaged away.
- **voice soft|powerful**: Denselow, "her cool but powerful and soulful voice". Consequence, "Her warm voice wraps a blanket around your shoulder". PopMatters, "soul-alto".
- **instruments**: Discogs credits list acoustic guitar, guitar, strings (violin, viola, cello), keyboards, bass, double bass, drums, percussion and flute (Magic Malik, "Eyé Àdabà"). Consequence: "Jailer ... hooks you with its hand claps".
- **production**: Discogs, "Programmed By – Cobhams Asuquo" and "Arranged By [Strings] – Cobhams Asuquo / Benjamin Constant".
- **lyrics**
  - social, political: Lester, "Asa sings about ecology and slavery"; Consequence, "the issue of all sorts of slavery today".
  - spiritual, love: PopMatters, "war is destructive, her mother is beautiful, God is great, and can lovers be trusted?".
  - identity: Culture Custodian on "Jailer", "Asa's yearning for freedom and self-expression".
- **language en|yo**: PopMatters, "Most of the lyrics are sung in English, with a few excursions into Yoruba".
- **producers Cobhams Asuquo**: Discogs, "Producer – Cobhams Asuquo". The Native: "Cohbams Asuquo ... had produced the entire album". "Producer [Additional]" credits (Benjamin Constant, Sébastien Viguier) are dropped by rule.
- **sounds_like**
  - Tracy Chapman: PopMatters ("closer to the proportions of a Tracy Chapman song") and Popdose.
  - Ayo: Popdose, "comparisons to Ayo and Tracy Chapman, and both are fair enough".
  - Macy Gray: BBC, "The model here would seem to be Macie Gray".
  - The Young Rascals (Groovin') [1/10]: Lester, "Fire on the Mountain sounds like Groovin' by the Young Rascals" (Groovin' is the title track of their 1967 album).
  - Excluded: Bob Marley and Stevie Wonder in Lester (figurative), and the BBC's "twenty-first century Bob Marley" (about the hype).
- **influenced_by Bob Marley and Marvin Gaye**: Aṣa on NPR, about this album: "My father had, you know, good collections of music from Marvin Gaye, Philip Coutre(ph) to Bob Marley ... So I think music started for me then". NPR frames these as "the sounds that would influence the music she creates". Medium strength: childhood listening, not studio references.
- **landmark 1**: Al Jazeera, "became an instant classic"; Pulse ranks it #8 among Nigerian debut albums.

## Scenes (src/data/scenes.ts format)

```ts
olamide: 'NG yo 2010 hiphop,afro', miabaga: 'NG en 2000 hiphop', psquare: 'NG pcm 2000 afro,rnb', tiwasavage: 'NG en 2010 afro,rnb', asa: 'NG en 2000 soul,folk',
```

- **olamide**: the sources stress Yoruba rap. Music In Africa says Street OT "averages more Yoruba words over English than on any other album".
- **miabaga**: Pulse calls him "an English-speaking Nigerian rapper".
- **psquare**: The Native, "deliberately delivered in pidgin English, with bursts of their native tongue, Igbo".
- **tiwasavage**: English first, with Yoruba and Pidgin (Pulse, NPR). The 2010 decade is her solo start (Mavin era, 2013 debut).
- **asa**: PopMatters, "Most of the lyrics are sung in English, with a few excursions into Yoruba".
- **Discovery rule**: miabaga, tiwasavage and asa sing mainly in English. They may not count as a "local scene sung in its own language". Owner to decide.

## Artist-level influences (own words)

- **Olamide** (BellaNaija, "Olamide on 'My Africa'", 2015): "My parents listened to music a lot ... they play music a lot like Wasiu Ayinde back to back, Sunny Ade and all that"; "Me making music is a result of influences from here and there; listening to Pasuma, listening to Orits Wiliki, Majek Fashek, Blakky, Eedris Abdulkareem and all these people." https://www.bellanaija.com/2015/08/olamide-on-my-africa-talks-being-a-father-his-babys-mother-never-listening-to-fela-growing-up-more/
  - Influences: Wasiu Ayinde Marshal (K1 De Ultimate), King Sunny Adé, Pasuma, Orits Wiliki, Majek Fashek, Blakky, Eedris Abdulkareem.
- **M.I Abaga**: none found in his own words. Afrocritik lists Lauryn Hill, Bob Marley, Sarah McLachlan, Pablo Neruda, Jay-Z and DMX as "the bedrock of MI's formative years", but that is the writer's summary, not a quote. The "Short Black Boy" line ("flow like Jigga ... sound like Nas") is a boast.
- **P-Square**: none in their own words. The Native (2020) calls Michael Jackson "their icon" (the writer's words).
- **Tiwa Savage**: France 24 via OkayAfrica (2020): "I just really decided to go back to what I love and what I fell in love with as a musician which is Afrobeat combined with soul and RnB", with homage to Fela Kuti. https://www.okayafrica.com/tiwa-savage-celia-album-tracklist/
  - Influences: Fela Kuti.
  - Her label bio (HIGHRESAUDIO) says she won her Berklee scholarship "Singing a Kim Burrell song". That is not stated as an influence, so it is not used.
- **Aṣa**
  - NPR (2009): "My father had, you know, good collections of music from Marvin Gaye, Philip Coutre(ph) to Bob Marley ... So I think music started for me then." https://www.npr.org/transcripts/100523110
  - PopMatters, paraphrasing her official biography (which speaks for the artist): "As a child, she loved Michael Jackson and Bob Marley, and as an adult she liked Fela Kuti and Angélique Kidjo." https://www.popmatters.com/70195-asa-asa-asha-2496062471.html
  - Influences: Bob Marley, Marvin Gaye, Michael Jackson, Fela Kuti, Angélique Kidjo.
